Respiratory Anatomy & Physiology

Breathing is two problems: get air in and out (ventilation), and move O₂ and CO₂ across the alveolar membrane (gas exchange). Every concept here lives in one of those two stories.     Respiratory · Concept 1 of 12

    Upper airway anatomy A

    The upper airway warms, humidifies, filters, and routes air. By the time air reaches the trachea, it should be ≈37 °C, ≈100% humidity, and largely particle-free.

    Notes

    • Nose & nasal cavity: nares → vestibule → conchae (superior, middle, inferior) create turbulence → trap particles. Lined by respiratory epithelium (pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells). Olfactory epithelium in the superior cavity.
    • Paranasal sinuses: frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, maxillary. Lighten skull, resonate voice, drain into nasal cavity.
    • Pharynx (3 regions):
      • Nasopharynx: air only. Pharyngeal tonsil (adenoid), auditory tube openings.
      • Oropharynx: air + food. Palatine and lingual tonsils.
      • Laryngopharynx: air + food. Continuous below with larynx and esophagus.
    • Larynx: 9 cartilages including thyroid, cricoid (only complete ring), and epiglottis (closes airway during swallowing). Houses true and false vocal cords. Innervated by recurrent laryngeal nerve (branch of CN X).
    • Trachea: ~10-12 cm long. C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings (open posteriorly toward esophagus). Lined by respiratory epithelium. Bifurcates at carina (T4-T5).

    Summary

    StructureFunctionClinical landmark
    Nasal conchae↑ turbulence, warm + humidifyEpistaxis at Kiesselbach's plexus
    EpiglottisDiverts food to esophagusAspiration if reflex impaired
    Cricoid cartilageOnly complete tracheal ringCricothyrotomy landmark
    CarinaTracheal bifurcationAspirated objects → R main bronchus (more vertical)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Aspiration tends to travel to the right main bronchus because it is more vertical and wider than the left. Epistaxis: 90% from Kiesselbach's plexus (anterior septum). Cricothyrotomy through the cricothyroid membrane is the emergency surgical airway, performed below the thyroid and above the cricoid cartilage.

    Respiratory · Concept 2 of 12

    Lower airway, lung anatomy, pleura A

    From the trachea down, the airway divides about 23 times. Each division gets smaller and loses cartilage support, until it ends in the alveoli where gas exchange happens.

    Notes

    • Bronchial tree: trachea → main bronchi → lobar (secondary) bronchi → segmental (tertiary) bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveolar sacs.
    • Right lung: 3 lobes (superior, middle, inferior); horizontal + oblique fissures.
    • Left lung: 2 lobes (superior, inferior); oblique fissure only. Has the cardiac notch and lingula.
    • Hilum: where the main bronchus, pulmonary artery (in front), and pulmonary veins (below) enter/exit the lung. Lymph nodes here.
    • Pleural membranes:
      • Parietal pleura: lines thoracic cavity, diaphragm, mediastinum.
      • Visceral pleura: covers the lung surface, including into fissures.
      • Pleural cavity: thin film of serous fluid; creates surface tension that couples lung to chest wall.
    • Diaphragm: main inspiratory muscle. Innervated by phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5 — "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive").
    • Accessory muscles of inspiration (used in distress): SCM, scalenes, pec minor.
    • Expiratory muscles (forced expiration only; passive at rest): abdominals, internal intercostals.

    Summary

    StructureFeatureClinical note
    Right lung3 lobes, horizontal + oblique fissureRML pneumonia common
    Left lung2 lobes, cardiac notch, lingulaHeart sits left
    Pleural cavitySerous fluid couplingPneumothorax breaks the seal → lung collapse
    DiaphragmMain inspiratory musclePhrenic nerve (C3-5)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pneumothorax: air in the pleural space breaks the surface-tension coupling → lung collapses (recoils inward). Tension pneumothorax pushes mediastinum, drops CO. Needle decompression at 2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line. Pleural effusion: fluid in pleural space (transudative = HF; exudative = infection/malignancy). Phrenic injury at C3-C5 (high cervical spinal cord trauma) → diaphragm paralysis → ventilatory failure.

    Respiratory · Concept 3 of 12

    Conducting zone, respiratory zone, alveolar architecture A

    The conducting zone moves air. The respiratory zone exchanges gas. The dividing line is the respiratory bronchiole, where the first alveoli appear.

    Notes

    • Conducting zone: nose → terminal bronchioles. No gas exchange. ≈150 mL of "anatomical dead space." Cartilage diminishes, smooth muscle increases as you descend.
    • Respiratory zone: respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveolar sacs. Site of gas exchange.
    • Alveolus: ~300 million per lung. Total surface area ~70-100 m² (roughly a tennis court).
    • Alveolar wall cell types:
      • Type I pneumocytes (~95% of surface): thin squamous, gas exchange.
      • Type II pneumocytes: cuboidal, produce surfactant, divide to replace Type I after injury.
      • Alveolar macrophages ("dust cells"): phagocytose debris.
    • Respiratory membrane: ≈0.5 µm thick. From alveolus to blood:
      1. Surfactant film
      2. Type I pneumocyte
      3. Fused basement membranes
      4. Capillary endothelium
    • Pores of Kohn: openings between adjacent alveoli; allow collateral ventilation.

    Summary

    ZoneWhereFunction
    ConductingTrachea → terminal bronchiolesMove + condition air
    TransitionalRespiratory bronchiolesSome alveoli; some conduction
    RespiratoryAlveolar ducts + sacsGas exchange

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Emphysema destroys alveolar walls → loss of surface area + loss of elastic recoil. Pulmonary fibrosis thickens the respiratory membrane → impaired diffusion. Pulmonary edema adds fluid to the membrane → also impaired diffusion. All three reduce DLCO (diffusing capacity).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which alveolar cell type produces surfactant?
    2. DOK 3. Why does pulmonary fibrosis reduce gas diffusion?
    Show answers
    1. Type II pneumocyte.
    2. Fibrosis thickens the respiratory membrane. By Fick's law, diffusion rate ∝ 1/membrane thickness, so a thicker membrane slows gas movement, especially for O₂ which is less soluble than CO₂.

    Respiratory · Concept 4 of 12

    Ventilation mechanics (Boyle's law, pressures) P

    Air moves down pressure gradients. The diaphragm and chest wall change thoracic volume; pressures change; air flows in or out. That is the whole story of breathing.

    Notes

    • Boyle's law: at constant temperature, P × V = constant. ↑ volume → ↓ pressure → air flows in.
    • Three pressures to track:
      • Atmospheric (Patm) = 760 mmHg at sea level.
      • Intra-alveolar (Palv) = pressure inside the alveoli.
      • Intrapleural (Pip) = pressure in the pleural space. Always slightly negative (~ −4 mmHg at rest), creating suction that keeps lungs expanded against chest wall.
    • Inspiration (active):
      • Diaphragm contracts ↓, external intercostals lift ribs ↑.
      • Thoracic volume ↑ → Pip drops more negative (~ −6 mmHg).
      • Lungs expand → Palv drops below Patm (~ −1 mmHg).
      • Air flows in until Palv = Patm.
    • Expiration (passive at rest):
      • Diaphragm relaxes, ribs drop.
      • Elastic recoil of lung ↓ thoracic volume.
      • Palv rises above Patm (~ +1 mmHg).
      • Air flows out.
    • Transpulmonary pressure = Palv − Pip. Keeps the lungs from collapsing. Always positive in healthy lungs.

    Summary

    PhaseDiaphragmPipPalvAirflow
    RestRelaxed−40 (= Patm)None
    InspirationContracts ↓−6−1In
    End-inspirationHeld−60None
    ExpirationRelaxes−4+1Out

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pneumothorax breaks intrapleural negativity. Air enters the pleural space → Pip rises to 0 (or positive in tension) → lung recoils inward → collapse. Mechanical ventilation reverses the physiology: positive pressure is applied to push air in (Palv > Patm). This raises intrathoracic pressure and can ↓ venous return → ↓ CO.

    Quick check

    Show answers
    1. The lung collapses (recoils inward) because the negative pressure that held it against the chest wall is gone — pneumothorax.
    2. Positive intrathoracic pressure compresses the great veins and right atrium → ↓ venous return → ↓ preload → ↓ CO → ↓ BP. Worse in hypovolemic patients.

    Respiratory · Concept 5 of 12

    Lung volumes and capacities P

    Volumes are the things you measure directly; capacities are sums of volumes. Knowing which is which is half of pulmonary function testing.

    Notes

    • The four volumes (everyone has one of each):
      • Tidal volume (TV): ≈500 mL. Quiet breath in and out.
      • Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV): ≈3,000 mL. Extra in after a normal inspiration.
      • Expiratory reserve volume (ERV): ≈1,200 mL. Extra out after a normal expiration.
      • Residual volume (RV): ≈1,200 mL. Left in lung after maximal expiration. Cannot be measured by spirometry alone.
    • Four capacities (sums of volumes):
      • Inspiratory capacity (IC): TV + IRV ≈3,500 mL.
      • Functional residual capacity (FRC): ERV + RV ≈2,400 mL. Lung volume at end of normal expiration.
      • Vital capacity (VC): IRV + TV + ERV ≈4,700 mL. Maximum inhalation to maximum exhalation.
      • Total lung capacity (TLC): VC + RV ≈5,900 mL.
    • Anatomical dead space: ≈150 mL. Air in conducting zone that doesn't reach alveoli.
    • Alveolar ventilation: (TV − dead space) × respiratory rate. The number that actually matters for gas exchange. At 500 mL TV, 12 breaths/min: (500 − 150) × 12 = 4,200 mL/min.
    • FEV1/FVC ratio (forced expiratory volume in 1 sec / forced vital capacity):
      • Normal ≥0.70.
      • Obstructive disease (asthma, COPD): <0.70 — air trapped, expiration slow.
      • Restrictive disease (fibrosis): normal or ↑ ratio with reduced volumes.

    Diagram: spirometry tracing

    Worked calculations

    1. Minute ventilation: RR 14, TV 500 mL.
      • VE = RR × TV = 14 × 500 = 7,000 mL/min.
      • Interpretation: normal (4-8 L/min).
    2. Alveolar ventilation: same patient, dead space ≈150 mL.
      • VA = RR × (TV − VD) = 14 × (500 − 150) = 4,900 mL/min.
      • Interpretation: alveolar ventilation is what matters for CO₂ removal. PaCO₂ ∝ 1/VA.
    3. Shallow rapid breathing trap: Same minute ventilation (7,000 mL/min) achieved with RR 28, TV 250 mL.
      • VA = 28 × (250 − 150) = 28 × 100 = 2,800 mL/min.
      • Interpretation: Same VE, but only 57% of the alveolar ventilation. Shallow breathing wastes ventilation on dead space → CO₂ retention.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Obstructive (asthma, COPD): ↑ RV, ↑ FRC, ↑ TLC (air trapping), ↓ FEV1/FVC. Restrictive (fibrosis, kyphoscoliosis): ↓ all volumes proportionally, FEV1/FVC normal or ↑. Bedside use: incentive spirometry post-op aims to restore inspiratory capacity and prevent atelectasis.

    Quick check

    2. DOK 4. A patient with severe COPD has FRC 4,500 mL (normal 2,400). Explain mechanistically.
    Show answers
    1. Residual volume (RV). Anything containing RV (FRC, TLC) requires special techniques.
    2. COPD causes airway obstruction and loss of elastic recoil. Air is trapped behind narrowed airways at end-expiration, ↑ RV and ↑ ERV → ↑ FRC. The lungs are over-inflated and stiffer to inflate further, increasing work of breathing.

    Respiratory · Concept 6 of 12

    Compliance, resistance, surface tension, surfactant P

    Three things determine how hard you have to work to breathe: how stretchy the lungs are (compliance), how open the airways are (resistance), and how much the alveoli want to collapse (surface tension).

    Notes

    • Compliance (C): ΔV / ΔP. How easily lungs expand for a given pressure change.
      • ↑ Compliance: emphysema (loss of elastic tissue). Lungs inflate easily but recoil poorly.
      • ↓ Compliance: fibrosis, pulmonary edema, atelectasis. Stiff lungs require more pressure to inflate.
    • Resistance (R): mostly determined by airway radius (R ∝ 1/r⁴, like in vessels).
      • Bronchoconstriction (asthma, vagal tone): ↑ R.
      • Bronchodilation (β2 agonists, sympathetic, anticholinergics): ↓ R.
      • Mucus, edema, foreign body: ↑ R.
    • Surface tension: water molecules at the alveolar air-fluid interface attract each other and tend to collapse the alveolus.
      • Laplace's law: P = 2T/r. Smaller alveoli have higher collapse pressure → without surfactant, small alveoli would empty into large ones.
    • Surfactant: dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC) + proteins, produced by Type II pneumocytes.
      • Reduces surface tension, especially in small alveoli.
      • Equalizes pressure across alveoli of different sizes → stable lung.
      • Production begins ~24 weeks gestation; mature by ~35 weeks.
    • Hysteresis: the inflation pressure-volume curve does not match the deflation curve. Energy is lost to overcoming surface tension during inflation.

    Summary

    PropertyDeterminant↑ MeansExample disease
    ComplianceElastic + surfactantLungs stretch easily↑ Emphysema; ↓ Fibrosis, edema
    ResistanceAirway radiusHarder to move air↑ Asthma, COPD
    Surface tensionAir-fluid interfaceCollapse tendency↑ NRDS (low surfactant)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS): premature infants with immature Type II pneumocytes → not enough surfactant → ↑ surface tension → atelectasis → ↓ compliance → respiratory failure. Treated with exogenous surfactant + ventilatory support. Antenatal steroids accelerate surfactant production. ARDS (adults): inflammation damages Type II cells and floods alveoli → also surfactant dysfunction + ↓ compliance.

    DRAW IT

    Draw two alveoli connected by a duct, one small and one large. Apply Laplace's law. Then add surfactant to the small one and re-draw — show how it equalizes pressure.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Why is surfactant especially important in small alveoli?
    2. DOK 3. Predict what happens to compliance in a pulmonary edema patient and why.
    Show answers
    1. By Laplace's law, P = 2T/r. Smaller alveoli have higher collapse pressure for the same surface tension. Surfactant lowers T more in small alveoli, equalizing pressure across alveoli of different sizes.
    2. Compliance ↓. Fluid in the alveoli and interstitium increases surface tension and stiffness → more pressure needed to inflate the same volume.

    Respiratory · Concept 7 of 12

    Gas exchange (Dalton's, Henry's, A-a gradient) P

    Gases move across the alveolar membrane by simple diffusion — driven by partial pressure differences. O₂ enters blood, CO₂ exits, and the system reaches near-equilibrium in about a third of a second.

    Notes

    • Dalton's law: total pressure of a gas mixture = sum of partial pressures. At sea level (Patm = 760 mmHg), air is ~21% O₂, so PiO₂ = 0.21 × 760 = 160 mmHg.
    • Henry's law: amount of gas dissolved ∝ partial pressure × solubility. CO₂ is ~20× more soluble than O₂.
    • Partial pressures along the path (sea level, normal lung):
      • Inspired air: PiO₂ 160, PiCO₂ ≈ 0.
      • Alveolar: PAO₂ ≈ 100, PACO₂ ≈ 40.
      • Arterial: PaO₂ ≈ 95-100, PaCO₂ ≈ 40.
      • Mixed venous: PvO₂ ≈ 40, PvCO₂ ≈ 46.
    • Alveolar gas equation (simplified): PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (Patm − 47) − PaCO₂/0.8.
      • At sea level on room air: PAO₂ ≈ 0.21 × (760 − 47) − 40/0.8 = 150 − 50 = 100 mmHg.
    • A-a gradient: PAO₂ − PaO₂.
      • Normal < 10-15 (young), up to ~25 (older).
      • ↑ A-a gradient = problem at the alveolar-capillary interface (V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limit).
      • Normal A-a with hypoxemia = hypoventilation or low FiO₂.
    • V/Q ratio (ventilation / perfusion):
      • Ideal ≈ 0.8.
      • V/Q = 0: shunt (perfusion but no ventilation, e.g., pneumonia, atelectasis). Does not correct with O₂.
      • V/Q = ∞: dead space (ventilation but no perfusion, e.g., PE).
      • Regional: apex V/Q > 1 (more vent), base V/Q < 1 (more perfusion). Both shift toward 1 with exercise.

    Summary

    DefectA-a gradientResponds to O₂?Example
    HypoventilationNormalYesOpioid overdose
    Low FiO₂NormalYesAltitude
    V/Q mismatch↑Yes (partial)Asthma, COPD
    Shunt↑↑PoorlyPneumonia, ARDS
    Diffusion limit↑YesFibrosis

    CLINICAL THREAD

    The A-a gradient is the single best discriminator of hypoxemia cause. Hypoventilation (e.g., opioid overdose) hypoxemia improves with naloxone or BiPAP. Shunt hypoxemia (pneumonia, ARDS) barely responds to 100% O₂ because the blood passing through unventilated alveoli never sees the higher oxygen. That is why ARDS often needs PEEP and prone positioning to recruit collapsed alveoli.

    Respiratory · Concept 8 of 12

    Oxygen transport: hemoglobin and the dissociation curve P

    Almost all oxygen rides on hemoglobin. The sigmoid binding curve makes Hb both eager to load up in the lungs and willing to let go in tissues. Shifts in the curve change how readily it releases.

    Notes

    • Two forms in blood:
      • Dissolved O₂ (Henry's law): tiny, ~0.3 mL O₂/dL plasma at PaO₂ 100. Drives diffusion.
      • Hb-bound O₂: ~98.5% of carried O₂. 1 g Hb carries 1.34 mL O₂ when fully saturated.
    • O₂ content: CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂). Hb level matters as much as saturation.
    • Hemoglobin: 4 subunits (2α, 2β), each with a heme + Fe²⁺. Cooperative binding → sigmoid curve.
    • Sigmoid binding curve:
      • Flat upper plateau (PaO₂ > 60): drops in PaO₂ have small effect on SaO₂.
      • Steep middle (PaO₂ 20-60): small changes in PaO₂ → large SaO₂ shifts.
      • P50 ≈ 27 mmHg: PaO₂ at which Hb is 50% saturated.
    • Right shift (↓ Hb affinity, easier O₂ release):
      • ↑ PCO₂, ↑ H⁺ (low pH), ↑ temperature, ↑ 2,3-BPG.
      • Mnemonic: "C-BPG-AT" (acidic, hypercapnic, hot, high BPG → release oxygen in working tissue).
    • Left shift (↑ Hb affinity, harder release):
      • ↓ PCO₂, ↑ pH, ↓ temperature, ↓ 2,3-BPG.
      • Carbon monoxide (binds 240× more avidly than O₂), HbF (fetal Hb).

    Diagram: oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve

    Worked calculation

    1. O₂ content: Patient with Hb 12 g/dL, SaO₂ 95%, PaO₂ 90.
      • CaO₂ = (1.34 × 12 × 0.95) + (0.003 × 90) = 15.3 + 0.27 = ~15.6 mL O₂/dL blood.
    2. Anemia paradox: same SaO₂, but Hb drops to 7.
      • CaO₂ = (1.34 × 7 × 0.95) + 0.27 = 8.9 + 0.27 = ~9.2 mL O₂/dL.
      • Interpretation: SaO₂ is still 95% but content is 40% lower. Pulse ox alone misses anemia.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Carbon monoxide poisoning: CO binds Hb 240× more strongly than O₂, shifts the curve left, and impairs unloading. SaO₂ on standard pulse oximetry can be falsely normal — need co-oximetry. Treat with 100% O₂ ± hyperbaric. Fetal Hb: higher affinity (lower P₅₀) so fetus can extract O₂ from maternal blood at the placenta. Acidosis and fever right-shift the curve, which helps tissue unloading in stressed metabolic states.

    DRAW IT

    Sketch the normal Hb curve. Add a right-shifted curve and label four causes. Add a left-shifted curve and label three causes. Mark P₅₀ for normal vs each shift.

    Quick check

    2. DOK 4. A house fire victim has SpO₂ 99% on pulse oximetry but is confused and tachycardic. What's the diagnosis and what test confirms it?

    2. Carbon monoxide poisoning. Carboxyhemoglobin reads as oxyhemoglobin on standard pulse oximetry, falsely reassuring. Confirm with co-oximetry measuring carboxyhemoglobin level. Treat with 100% O₂; consider hyperbaric.

    Respiratory · Concept 9 of 12

    CO₂ transport: three forms and the chloride shift P

    CO₂ is more soluble than O₂ and travels in three forms, with bicarbonate doing most of the work. The chloride shift inside the red cell is what makes it possible.

    Notes

    • Three forms in blood:
      • Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻): ~70%. The main carrier.
      • Carbaminohemoglobin (bound to Hb's N-terminus, not the heme): ~23%.
      • Dissolved CO₂: ~7%.
    • The bicarbonate reaction (inside red cell):
      • CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase).
      • H⁺ is buffered by deoxyhemoglobin (Haldane effect — deoxy-Hb is a better buffer and CO₂ carrier).
      • HCO₃⁻ exits the RBC into plasma in exchange for Cl⁻ (chloride shift, via band 3 anion exchanger).
    • At the lung: reaction reverses. HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O → exhaled.
    • Haldane effect: deoxygenation of Hb (in tissues) ↑ its ability to bind CO₂ and H⁺. Helps load CO₂ at tissues, unload at lung.
    • Bohr effect (mirror image, on O₂): ↑ CO₂ and ↑ H⁺ in tissues ↓ Hb affinity for O₂ → more O₂ release. Same chemistry, different lens.
    • CO₂-acid-base link: PaCO₂ ∝ 1/alveolar ventilation. ↑ ventilation → ↓ CO₂ → respiratory alkalosis. ↓ ventilation → ↑ CO₂ → respiratory acidosis.

    Summary

    FormApprox %Where
    HCO₃⁻ (bicarb)~70%Plasma (after chloride shift)
    Carbamino-Hb~23%Hb N-terminus
    Dissolved CO₂~7%Plasma + RBC

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) reduce the rate of CO₂ → HCO₃⁻ conversion → ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption in the kidney → mild metabolic acidosis. Used in altitude sickness, glaucoma, and as a diuretic. End-tidal CO₂ (EtCO₂) monitoring: rapid drop signals decreased CO production (cardiac arrest), increased dead space (PE), or disconnection from ventilator.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What enzyme catalyzes CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃?
    2. DOK 3. Explain how the Haldane effect helps CO₂ transport in tissues vs lung.
    Show answers
    1. Carbonic anhydrase.
    2. In tissues, Hb releases O₂ → becomes deoxy-Hb → deoxy-Hb has higher affinity for CO₂ and H⁺ → loads more CO₂. At the lung, Hb binds O₂ → becomes oxy-Hb → kicks CO₂ off → CO₂ diffuses out for exhalation.

    Respiratory · Concept 10 of 12

    Control of breathing P

    The medulla sets the rhythm. Chemoreceptors fine-tune it based on CO₂ (primary driver in health) and O₂ (rescue driver in extremes). Voluntary input from the cortex can override briefly.

    Notes

    • Brainstem centers:
      • Dorsal respiratory group (DRG): inspiratory rhythm generator.
      • Ventral respiratory group (VRG): forced expiration and inspiration.
      • Pontine respiratory centers (pneumotaxic, apneustic): fine-tune rate and depth.
    • Central chemoreceptors (medulla):
      • Respond to ↑ CO₂ via ↓ pH of CSF.
      • Dominant driver of ventilation in healthy people.
      • CO₂ crosses blood-brain barrier easily; H⁺ does not. So CSF acid-base shifts mirror CO₂.
    • Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid + aortic bodies):
      • Carotid bodies (CN IX) and aortic bodies (CN X).
      • Respond to ↓ PaO₂ (especially < 60), ↑ PaCO₂, ↑ H⁺.
      • Rapid response. Important in hypoxemia.
    • Hypoxic drive (deep dive):
      • In chronic CO₂ retention (severe COPD), central chemoreceptors blunt to high CO₂.
      • Hypoxia becomes the primary stimulus to breathe.
      • Giving 100% O₂ can paradoxically suppress respiratory drive — titrate O₂ to SpO₂ 88-92% in chronic COPD.
    • Other inputs:
      • Stretch receptors (Hering-Breuer reflex): prevent over-inflation.
      • Irritant receptors: cough reflex, bronchoconstriction.
      • J-receptors: in alveolar wall; activated by congestion → rapid shallow breathing.
      • Cortical override: voluntary breath holding, hyperventilation, speech.

    Summary

    ReceptorStimulusOutput
    Central chemoreceptors↑ PaCO₂ (via ↓ CSF pH)↑ Ventilation (main driver)
    Peripheral (carotid/aortic)↓ PaO₂ (< 60), ↑ CO₂, ↑ H⁺↑ Ventilation
    Stretch (Hering-Breuer)Lung inflationInhibit further inspiration
    J-receptorsPulmonary congestionRapid shallow breathing

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Opioids blunt central chemoreceptor response to CO₂ → ↓ RR, ↑ PaCO₂. Reverse with naloxone. COPD with chronic hypercapnia: use lowest FiO₂ that maintains SpO₂ 88-92% to preserve hypoxic drive. Cheyne-Stokes respiration (crescendo-decrescendo with apnea): seen in HF and CNS injury — circulatory delay between lungs and brain produces oscillating CO₂.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the feedback loop: central + peripheral chemoreceptors → medulla → respiratory muscles → ventilation → blood gases → back to receptors. Add the cortex as a top-down override.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which is the primary driver of ventilation in a healthy person?
    2. DOK 4. Why do we use cautious oxygen titration in some COPD patients?
    Show answers
    1. Central chemoreceptors responding to PaCO₂ (via CSF pH).
    2. Chronic CO₂ retention blunts central chemoreceptors. The patient then relies on hypoxia (sensed by peripheral chemoreceptors) to drive ventilation. Over-correction with high FiO₂ removes that stimulus → hypoventilation → CO₂ retention worsens (hypercapnic respiratory failure). Goal: SpO₂ 88-92%, not 100%.

    Respiratory · Concept 11 of 12

    Respiratory acid-base balance P

    Lungs control CO₂. Kidneys control HCO₃⁻. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation links them. Acid-base disorders are about which one shifted first and whether the other compensated.

    Notes

    • Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PaCO₂)).
      • Normal pH = 7.35-7.45; HCO₃⁻ = 22-26; PaCO₂ = 35-45.
    • Four primary disorders:
      • Respiratory acidosis: ↑ PaCO₂, ↓ pH. Hypoventilation (opioids, COPD, neuromuscular).
      • Respiratory alkalosis: ↓ PaCO₂, ↑ pH. Hyperventilation (anxiety, pain, sepsis, salicylates).
      • Metabolic acidosis: ↓ HCO₃⁻, ↓ pH. DKA, lactic, renal failure, diarrhea.
      • Metabolic alkalosis: ↑ HCO₃⁻, ↑ pH. Vomiting, diuretics, hyperaldosteronism.
    • Compensation (the other system tries to restore pH; rarely complete):
      • Respiratory disorders → renal compensation (slow, days). ↑ or ↓ HCO₃⁻.
      • Metabolic disorders → respiratory compensation (fast, minutes). ↑ or ↓ ventilation.
    • Quick ABG read:
      1. pH: acidemic, alkalemic, or normal?
      2. PaCO₂: respiratory?
      3. HCO₃⁻: metabolic?
      4. Which moves WITH the pH change = primary disorder.
      5. Which moves AGAINST = compensation.

    Summary

    DisorderpHPaCO₂HCO₃⁻Compensation direction
    Resp acidosis↓↑ (primary)↑ (renal, slow)Kidney retains HCO₃⁻
    Resp alkalosis↑↓ (primary)↓ (renal, slow)Kidney excretes HCO₃⁻
    Metabolic acidosis↓↓ (resp, fast)↓ (primary)Hyperventilation
    Metabolic alkalosis↑↑ (resp, limited)↑ (primary)Hypoventilation (limited by hypoxia)

    Worked ABG interpretations

    1. ABG #1: pH 7.25, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 26.
      • pH ↓ (acidemic). PaCO₂ ↑ (moves WITH pH change, primary). HCO₃⁻ near normal (no compensation yet).
      • Acute respiratory acidosis. Causes: opioid OD, COPD exacerbation, neuromuscular weakness.
    2. ABG #2: pH 7.35, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 34.
      • pH near low normal. PaCO₂ ↑, HCO₃⁻ ↑.
      • Chronic respiratory acidosis with renal compensation. Likely chronic COPD.
    3. ABG #3: pH 7.15, PaCO₂ 25, HCO₃⁻ 10.
      • pH ↓↓ (acidemic). HCO₃⁻ ↓ (primary, metabolic). PaCO₂ ↓ (respiratory compensation, blowing off CO₂).
      • Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation. DKA, lactic acidosis, sepsis.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Kussmaul breathing: deep rapid respirations in metabolic acidosis (DKA), blowing off CO₂ to compensate. Anion gap (= Na − Cl − HCO₃; normal 8-12): elevated gap acidosis (MUDPILES — methanol, uremia, DKA, propylene glycol, INH/iron, lactate, ethylene glycol, salicylates) vs normal gap (diarrhea, RTA).

    DRAW IT

    Draw a 2x2 acid-base grid: pH (high/low) × PaCO₂ (high/low). Place the four disorders. Add HCO₃⁻ shifts that fit each.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. An anxious patient has pH 7.50, PaCO₂ 28, HCO₃⁻ 22. Diagnosis?
    2. DOK 4. A DKA patient has pH 7.10, PaCO₂ 18, HCO₃⁻ 6, AG 26. Walk through the interpretation.
    Show answers
    1. Acute respiratory alkalosis. Hyperventilation drops PaCO₂; HCO₃⁻ not yet compensated.
    2. Severe acidemia (pH 7.10). HCO₃⁻ very low → primary metabolic acidosis. PaCO₂ low → respiratory compensation (Kussmaul breathing). Anion gap 26 (high) → high-AG metabolic acidosis. In DKA the gap comes from ketoacids (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate). Treatment: fluids, insulin, electrolyte replacement (especially K⁺).

    Respiratory · Concept 12 of 12

    Common respiratory conditions CLIN

    Most respiratory pathology is one of four patterns: airway obstruction, alveolar collapse or flooding, perfusion failure, or central/neuromuscular drive failure.

    Notes

    • Asthma: reversible airway obstruction. Bronchoconstriction + airway inflammation + mucus.
      • Triggered by allergens, exercise, infection, cold.
      • Findings: wheeze, prolonged expiration, ↓ FEV₁/FVC, responds to bronchodilators.
      • Treatment: short-acting β2 agonists (rescue), inhaled corticosteroids (control), leukotriene modifiers.
    • COPD (chronic bronchitis + emphysema): irreversible airway obstruction.
      • Chronic bronchitis: productive cough ≥ 3 mo × 2 yr. ↑ mucus, inflammation.
      • Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls. ↓ surface area, ↓ elastic recoil → air trapping.
      • Findings: barrel chest, prolonged expiration, hyperinflation on CXR.
      • Treatment: smoking cessation (only proven mortality reduction), bronchodilators, ICS in select, O₂ if hypoxemic, vaccines.
    • Pneumonia: alveolar consolidation from infection.
      • CAP (Streptococcus pneumoniae most common), HAP (Pseudomonas, MRSA risk), aspiration (anaerobes), viral (influenza, RSV, COVID).
      • Findings: fever, productive cough, crackles, ↑ WBC, infiltrate on CXR, ↑ A-a gradient (shunt).
    • ARDS: inflammatory non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
      • Berlin criteria: bilateral infiltrates, PaO₂/FiO₂ < 300, not cardiogenic, within 7 days of insult.
      • ↓ Compliance, massive shunt, hypoxemia poorly responsive to FiO₂.
      • Management: lung-protective ventilation (low TV ~6 mL/kg, plateau pressure < 30), PEEP, prone positioning.
    • Pulmonary embolism: clot in pulmonary artery.
      • Source: deep vein thrombosis. Virchow's triad: stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability.
      • Findings: sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxemia, ↑ A-a gradient (dead space).
      • Treatment: anticoagulation; thrombolytics or thrombectomy if massive (hemodynamic instability).
    • Pulmonary hypertension: sustained ↑ pulmonary arterial pressure. PAH (idiopathic, drugs) vs secondary (HF, COPD, chronic PE). RV strain → cor pulmonale.
    • Pneumothorax: air in pleural space. Spontaneous (tall thin males), traumatic, iatrogenic (central line), tension (medical emergency).

    ConditionPatternBedside clueInitial action
    AsthmaReversible obstructionWheeze, prolonged expirationSABA, O₂, steroids
    COPD exacerbationFixed obstruction + trigger↑ dyspnea, ↑ sputum, hypoxemiaO₂ (titrate), bronchodilators, steroids, abx if infection
    PneumoniaShuntFever, crackles, infiltrateAntibiotics, O₂, fluids
    ARDSSevere shunt + ↓ complianceHypoxia refractory to O₂Lung-protective ventilation, PEEP, prone
    PEDead spaceSudden dyspnea, ↑ A-aAnticoagulation, imaging

    CLINICAL THREAD

    The nursing assessment cascade in respiratory distress: airway patency, RR, work of breathing (accessory muscles, retractions, tripoding), breath sounds (wheeze, crackles, absent), SpO₂, mental status. Look at the pattern, not just one number. A patient with a "normal" SpO₂ on supplemental O₂ but who is using accessory muscles and confused is decompensating.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient with asthma has FEV₁/FVC 0.55, improves to 0.78 after albuterol. What does the change indicate?
    2. DOK 4. A patient with severe ARDS has PaO₂ 60 on 100% O₂. Explain the physiology and the rationale for prone positioning.
    Show answers
    1. Reversibility of airway obstruction — characteristic of asthma. COPD typically shows minimal or no improvement post-bronchodilator.
    2. ARDS causes massive shunt: blood flows past collapsed/flooded alveoli without picking up O₂. FiO₂ doesn't help shunted blood because it never sees the higher alveolar O₂. Prone positioning redistributes perfusion away from the dorsal lung (where the most consolidation typically accumulates from gravity-dependent edema) and recruits dorsal alveoli, reducing shunt fraction → improved oxygenation.
