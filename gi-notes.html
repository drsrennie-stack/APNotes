    GI · Concept 1 of 14

    GI tract overview, peritoneum, mesentery A

    One long tube from mouth to anus, plus three accessory organs. The peritoneum suspends and supports, the mesenteries carry vessels and nerves.

    Notes

    • GI tract (alimentary canal): mouth → pharynx → esophagus → stomach → small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → large intestine (cecum, ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid, rectum) → anus.
    • Accessory organs: teeth, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas.
    • Peritoneum (serous membrane):
      • Parietal peritoneum: lines abdominal cavity wall.
      • Visceral peritoneum: covers organs.
      • Peritoneal cavity: potential space with thin serous fluid.
    • Intraperitoneal organs: stomach, jejunum, ileum, transverse colon, sigmoid colon, liver, spleen.
    • Retroperitoneal organs (SAD PUCKER): Suprarenal, Aorta/IVC, Duodenum (2nd/3rd), Pancreas (except tail), Ureters, Colon (asc/desc), Kidneys, Esophagus, Rectum.
    • Mesentery: double layer of peritoneum that suspends intraperitoneal organs and carries vessels/nerves. Greater omentum (apron over abdomen), lesser omentum (between liver and stomach).

    Summary

    RegionLengthMain job
    Esophagus~25 cmTransport, swallow
    Stomach—Mix, acid, pepsin, intrinsic factor
    Small intestine~6 mMost digestion + absorption
    Large intestine~1.5 mWater absorption, microbiome, feces

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Peritonitis: inflammation of peritoneum (perforation, surgery, dialysis). Rigid abdomen, rebound tenderness, fever, ileus. Surgical/medical emergency. Ascites: fluid in peritoneal cavity (cirrhosis, malignancy, HF). Transudate vs exudate distinction guides workup.

    Quick check

    Show answers
    1. Retroperitoneal (except the tail, which is intraperitoneal).
    2. Rigid abdomen reflects diffuse peritoneal inflammation, often from perforation (bowel, gastric ulcer, ruptured viscus) releasing GI contents and bacteria into a normally sterile cavity → rapid systemic sepsis. Time to surgical source control matters.

    GI · Concept 2 of 14

    GI wall layers (four tunics) A

    The GI wall has the same four-layer architecture from esophagus to anus. Regional modifications support each segment's specific job.

    Notes

    • Mucosa (innermost, faces lumen):
      • Epithelium: type varies — stratified squamous (esophagus, anal canal); simple columnar (stomach, intestines).
      • Lamina propria: loose CT with capillaries, lymphatics, MALT.
      • Muscularis mucosae: thin smooth muscle layer; local movement of mucosa.
    • Submucosa: dense CT, large vessels, nerves, glands. Houses the submucosal (Meissner) plexus — controls secretion.
    • Muscularis externa:
      • Inner circular layer.
      • Outer longitudinal layer.
      • Houses the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus between them — controls motility.
      • Specializations: 3 layers in stomach (extra oblique); taeniae coli (3 longitudinal bands) in colon.
    • Serosa (or adventitia where not intraperitoneal): outer layer. Serosa is visceral peritoneum; adventitia is connective tissue without serous covering (esophagus, retroperitoneal segments).
    • Mucosa modifications for absorption:
      • Circular folds (plicae circulares): small intestine. Three-fold increase.
      • Villi: small intestine. Ten-fold increase.
      • Microvilli (brush border): on enterocytes. Twenty-fold increase.
      • Combined: ~600× increase, total surface area ~250 m² in small intestine.

    Summary

    LayerKey featureFunction
    MucosaEpithelium + lamina propria + muscularis mucosaeSecretion + absorption
    SubmucosaCT, Meissner plexusVessels, secretion control
    Muscularis externaCircular + longitudinal, Auerbach plexusMotility
    Serosa / adventitiaOuterAnchor, friction-free

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Crohn's disease involves all four wall layers (transmural inflammation) → fistulas, strictures, abscesses. Ulcerative colitis is limited to mucosa and submucosa → bleeding, mucus, but not fistulas. Celiac disease: gluten-driven autoimmune attack on small intestinal villi → villous atrophy → malabsorption.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which plexus controls GI motility?
    2. DOK 3. Why does Crohn disease cause fistulas while ulcerative colitis does not?
    Show answers
    1. Myenteric (Auerbach) plexus, between circular and longitudinal muscle.
    2. Crohn inflammation is transmural — extends through all layers and serosa — eroding into adjacent loops or skin to form fistulas. UC stays in mucosa/submucosa, so it bleeds but does not tunnel.

    GI · Concept 3 of 14

    Enteric nervous system + motility A&P

    The gut has its own brain. ~500 million neurons in the enteric nervous system can run motility and secretion locally, with ANS as override.

    Notes

    • Enteric nervous system (ENS):
      • Two intrinsic plexuses: Meissner (submucosal, secretion) + Auerbach (myenteric, motility).
      • Can function autonomously — coordinates peristalsis and secretion even if vagus is cut.
      • Modulated by ANS (parasympathetic ↑ activity, sympathetic ↓ activity) and hormones (gastrin, CCK, secretin, GLP-1, ghrelin).
    • Motility patterns:
      • Peristalsis: wave of contraction-relaxation pushing contents forward. Esophagus and intestine.
      • Segmentation: alternating circular muscle contractions that mix without forward propulsion. Small intestine for digestion/absorption.
      • Mass movement: 3-4× daily in colon, large peristaltic wave moving feces toward rectum.
      • Migrating motor complex (MMC): "housekeeping" pattern between meals — sweeps debris distally. Driven by motilin during fasting.
    • Sphincters control passage:
      • Upper esophageal (UES): voluntary skeletal muscle.
      • Lower esophageal (LES): physiologic, prevents reflux.
      • Pyloric: stomach → duodenum.
      • Ileocecal: small → large intestine, prevents backflow.
      • Internal anal: involuntary smooth muscle.
      • External anal: voluntary skeletal muscle.
    • Hirschsprung disease: congenital absence of enteric ganglia in distal colon → no peristalsis there → functional obstruction in newborns. Failure to pass meconium >48 h.

    Summary

    PatternWhereJob
    PeristalsisEsophagus, intestineForward propulsion
    SegmentationSmall intestineMix + absorb
    Mass movementColonFeces toward rectum
    MMCStomach + small intestine, fastingHousekeeping

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Ileus: functional bowel paralysis. Post-op, electrolyte imbalance (especially hypokalemia), opioids, sepsis. Distinct from mechanical obstruction (which has a physical block). Both cause distention, vomiting, and absent stool/flatus, but management differs. Gastroparesis (often diabetic): delayed gastric emptying → nausea, early satiety, vomiting. Metoclopramide (prokinetic) is used cautiously.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the GI sphincters in order from mouth to anus, marking voluntary (skeletal) vs involuntary (smooth).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. What motility pattern dominates the small intestine to mix nutrients with enzymes?
    2. DOK 4. Why does severe hypokalemia cause ileus?
    Show answers
    1. Segmentation.
    2. Smooth muscle excitability depends on K⁺ gradients. Hypokalemia hyperpolarizes the resting membrane (further from threshold) → impaired contraction → reduced peristalsis → functional ileus. Correct the K to restore motility.

    GI · Concept 4 of 14

    Mouth, pharynx, esophagus + swallowing A&P

    Swallowing is the most complex coordinated motor sequence the body performs. It moves food from mouth to stomach while keeping the airway protected.

    Notes

    • Mouth: mechanical breakdown (teeth) + salivary digestion.
      • Salivary glands: parotid (CN IX), submandibular + sublingual (CN VII). ~1.5 L/day.
      • Saliva: water, mucus, electrolytes, amylase (starch digestion begins), lysozyme, IgA.
    • Pharynx: shared with respiratory tract.
    • Esophagus: 25 cm muscular tube. Skeletal muscle (upper 1/3), mixed (middle), smooth muscle (lower 1/3). Lined with stratified squamous epithelium (durability). UES and LES at top and bottom.
    • Three phases of swallowing:
      1. Oral (voluntary): tongue pushes bolus posteriorly.
      2. Pharyngeal (involuntary): ~1 second. Soft palate elevates (blocks nasopharynx), epiglottis tips (blocks airway), vocal cords close, UES opens, peristaltic wave begins.
      3. Esophageal (involuntary): 5-10 sec. Primary peristaltic wave moves bolus down. Secondary wave clears residual. LES opens then closes.
    • Reflexes during swallowing: respiration is briefly suppressed; gag reflex protects against aspiration if bolus contacts oropharynx prematurely.
    • Dysphagia patterns:
      • Oropharyngeal (difficulty initiating, coughing, nasal regurgitation): stroke, Parkinson, ALS, myasthenia.
      • Esophageal (food sticks after swallowing): stricture, ring, achalasia (LES fails to relax), motility disorder, cancer.

    Summary

    PhaseControlKey event
    OralVoluntaryTongue pushes bolus back
    PharyngealReflex (medulla)Airway protection, UES opens
    EsophagealReflexPeristalsis, LES opens

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Aspiration risk rises with stroke, dementia, sedation, ETT/NGT, dysphagia. Bedside swallow screen before oral intake post-stroke. Speech pathology consult for failed screen. GERD: LES dysfunction → acid reflux → heartburn, regurgitation, esophagitis, Barrett (premalignant). Treat with PPI, lifestyle. Achalasia: LES fails to relax → progressive dysphagia, regurgitation. Treat with pneumatic dilation, Heller myotomy, or POEM.

    DRAW IT

    Draw a swallow sequence in 3 frames: oral, pharyngeal (note airway protection), esophageal. Label UES, LES, epiglottis, soft palate.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which enzyme in saliva begins digestion of starch?
    2. DOK 3. A post-stroke patient coughs during the oral phase. What's the safest immediate action?
    Show answers
    1. Salivary amylase.
    2. Stop oral intake (NPO), suction airway if needed, position upright, consult speech pathology for formal swallow evaluation. Aspiration is a leading cause of post-stroke morbidity and mortality.

    GI · Concept 5 of 14

    Stomach + gastric phases of digestion A&P

    The stomach is a reservoir and acid bath. It mixes food into chyme, kills most pathogens, starts protein digestion, and releases nutrients into the duodenum at a manageable rate.

    Notes

    • Regions: cardia (entrance), fundus (dome above LES), body (main mixing area), antrum (distal grinder), pylorus (gate to duodenum).
    • Three muscular layers (extra oblique layer beyond standard 2): allow churning/mixing.
    • Rugae: folds in mucosa, flatten when stomach fills (allows ~4× expansion).
    • Gastric pit cells (in glands):
      • Mucous cells: bicarbonate-rich mucus → protect lining from acid.
      • Parietal cells: HCl (pH 1-2) + intrinsic factor (essential for B12 absorption in ileum).
      • Chief cells: pepsinogen → pepsin (activated by acid; begins protein digestion).
      • G cells (antrum): gastrin → stimulates parietal acid secretion.
      • D cells: somatostatin → inhibits acid secretion.
      • Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells: histamine → stimulate parietal cells (H2 receptor).
    • Three phases of gastric secretion:
      1. Cephalic (~30%): sight, smell, thought of food → vagus → ACh on parietal cells → acid + gastrin release.
      2. Gastric (~60%): stomach distention + protein/peptides → local + vagal reflexes → maximum gastrin and acid. Lasts hours.
      3. Intestinal (~10%): chyme entering duodenum initially briefly stimulates, then inhibits gastric activity via enterogastric reflex + CCK, secretin (matches stomach output to small intestine capacity).
    • Functions of gastric acid:
      • Denature proteins.
      • Activate pepsinogen → pepsin.
      • Kill most ingested microbes.
      • Solubilize iron (Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ for absorption).
      • Release B12 from food proteins.
    • Mucosal protection: mucus-bicarbonate barrier, rapid epithelial turnover, prostaglandins maintain blood flow. NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandins → ulcer risk.

    Summary

    CellSecretesFunction
    ParietalHCl + IFAcid + B12 absorption
    ChiefPepsinogenProtein digestion
    G cellGastrin↑ Acid
    D cellSomatostatin↓ Acid
    ECL cellHistamine↑ Acid (H2)
    MucousMucus + HCO₃⁻Protection

    CLINICAL THREAD

    PUD (peptic ulcer disease): imbalance of acid vs mucosal defense. Two main causes: H. pylori (~70-90% of duodenal, ~50-70% of gastric) and NSAIDs. Test and treat H. pylori (triple or quadruple therapy). PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole): irreversibly inhibit parietal cell H⁺/K⁺ ATPase. H2 blockers (famotidine): block histamine effect on parietal cells. Pernicious anemia: autoimmune attack on parietal cells → no intrinsic factor → no B12 absorption → megaloblastic anemia + neuro symptoms.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the gastric gland with all six cell types. Then draw the three regulators of parietal acid secretion (vagus → ACh, gastrin from G cells, histamine from ECL cells) converging on the H⁺/K⁺ ATPase.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which cell makes intrinsic factor, and what is it needed for?
    2. DOK 3. Why might chronic PPI use cause iron deficiency?
    Show answers
    1. Parietal cell. Intrinsic factor binds vitamin B12 in stomach → IF-B12 complex absorbed in terminal ileum. Without IF, B12 deficiency develops (megaloblastic anemia + neuropathy).
    2. Gastric acid solubilizes dietary iron (Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ for absorption in duodenum). Chronic acid suppression → less Fe²⁺ → iron deficiency anemia, especially in already at-risk patients.

    GI · Concept 6 of 14

    Small intestine: anatomy + absorption A&P

    The small intestine is where almost all nutrient absorption happens. Six meters long, optimized for surface area, and partnered with the pancreas and liver for chemistry.

    Notes

    • Three regions:
      • Duodenum (~25 cm): receives chyme + pancreatic enzymes + bile. Mostly retroperitoneal. Brunner glands secrete alkaline mucus.
      • Jejunum (~2.5 m): main absorption site. Tall villi, many circular folds.
      • Ileum (~3.5 m): completes absorption. Peyer's patches (MALT). Terminal ileum reabsorbs bile salts (enterohepatic circulation) and absorbs vitamin B12.
    • Surface area amplification (review):
      • Plicae (3×) → villi (10×) → microvilli (20×) ≈ 600× total amplification.
      • Each villus has: enterocytes (absorption), goblet cells (mucus), enteroendocrine cells, a central lacteal (lymph), capillary network.
      • Brush border enzymes (on enterocyte microvilli): lactase, sucrase, maltase, peptidases, etc.
    • Crypts of Lieberkühn: tubular glands between villi. Stem cells migrate up villus, mature, then slough off (~5-day turnover). Paneth cells at base secrete antimicrobial peptides.
    • Absorption mechanisms:
      • Passive diffusion: lipid-soluble substances, water.
      • Facilitated diffusion: fructose (GLUT-5).
      • Secondary active transport: glucose, galactose, amino acids (Na-cotransport via SGLT, etc.).
      • Active transport: ions.
      • Endocytosis: lipids in chylomicrons (into lacteals).
    • Hormones from the small intestine: CCK, secretin, GIP, GLP-1, motilin, ghrelin (mostly stomach but related), serotonin.

    Summary

    RegionDistinguishing featureKey absorption
    DuodenumBrunner glands, receives bile/pancreasIron, Ca
    JejunumTall villi, max surfaceCarbs, AA, water-soluble vitamins
    IleumPeyer's patchesB12, bile salts, fat-soluble vitamins

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Celiac disease: gluten triggers autoimmune attack on small intestinal villi → villous atrophy in proximal small intestine → malabsorption (especially iron, folate). Anti-tTG IgA + endoscopic biopsy confirms. Treat: lifelong gluten-free diet. Crohn disease often involves terminal ileum → B12 and bile salt malabsorption. Short bowel syndrome: after extensive resection. Diarrhea, malabsorption, often need TPN.

    DRAW IT

    Draw a villus with its enterocytes, capillary network, and central lacteal. Indicate where carbs/AAs absorb (into capillary) vs fat (into lacteal).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Where is vitamin B12 absorbed?
    2. DOK 3. A patient with Crohn disease involving the terminal ileum develops diarrhea after fatty meals. Mechanism?
    Show answers
    1. Terminal ileum (requires intrinsic factor from stomach).
    2. Bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum. With ileal disease, bile salts pass into the colon, where they irritate the mucosa and stimulate secretion → bile-acid diarrhea after fatty meals. Cholestyramine (bile-acid binder) can help.

    GI · Concept 7 of 14

    Large intestine, microbiome, defecation A&P

    The colon's main job is water reclamation and feces formation. The microbiome ferments fiber, makes vitamin K, and competes with pathogens. Loss of those bacteria (antibiotics) is its own disease.

    Notes

    • Segments: cecum (with appendix) → ascending colon → transverse → descending → sigmoid → rectum → anal canal.
    • Unique features:
      • Taeniae coli: three longitudinal muscle bands (instead of complete longitudinal layer).
      • Haustra: sac-like pouches between bands.
      • Epiploic appendages: small fat tags on serosa.
      • Mucosa: simple columnar with goblet cells (lots of mucus); no villi.
    • Functions:
      • Reabsorb water (~1.5 L/day in vs ~100 mL in stool).
      • Reabsorb electrolytes (Na, Cl).
      • Bacterial fermentation: ferment indigestible fiber → short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, acetate, propionate) → colonocyte fuel, anti-inflammatory.
      • Produce vitamin K and some B vitamins (bacterial).
      • Form and store feces.
    • Microbiome:
      • ~100 trillion bacteria, >1,000 species. Mostly anaerobic (Bacteroides, Firmicutes).
      • Roles: barrier, immune training, fermentation, vitamin production, drug metabolism.
      • Disruption (antibiotics, illness) → dysbiosis. Worst-case: C. difficile colitis after broad-spectrum antibiotics.
    • Defecation:
      • Mass movements (3-4× daily) push feces into rectum.
      • Stretch receptors in rectal wall → defecation reflex (S2-S4 parasympathetic).
      • Internal anal sphincter (smooth, involuntary) relaxes; external anal sphincter (skeletal, voluntary) under cortical control.
      • Valsalva maneuver assists.

    Summary

    FeatureSmall intestineLarge intestine
    Length~6 m~1.5 m
    Surface modificationsPlicae, villi, microvilliNone (just mucus + crypts)
    Main jobDigestion + absorptionWater reabsorption, microbiome, feces
    Unique muscleStandard 2 layersTaeniae coli

    CLINICAL THREAD

    C. difficile colitis: often after antibiotics disrupt normal flora. Watery diarrhea, pseudomembranes, leukocytosis, ↑ lactate in severe. Treat with oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin; fecal microbiota transplant in recurrent. Diverticulosis: outpouchings of mucosa through muscular wall, common in sigmoid. Often asymptomatic; diverticulitis if inflamed. Colorectal cancer: 3rd leading cancer death. Screening with colonoscopy q10y starting age 45 (USPSTF).

    DRAW IT

    Draw the colon with taeniae coli and haustra. Add the appendix at the cecum. Mark sigmoid (common diverticulosis site) and rectum.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which vitamin is largely produced by colonic bacteria?
    2. DOK 4. Why is broad-spectrum antibiotic use a major risk factor for C. difficile?
    Show answers
    1. Vitamin K (used in clotting factor synthesis). Newborns get a vitamin K shot at birth because their colon hasn't been colonized yet.
    2. Antibiotics kill commensal anaerobes that normally outcompete C. diff. With the niche cleared, C. diff overgrows and produces toxins (TcdA, TcdB) that destroy colonic epithelium → pseudomembranous colitis.

    GI · Concept 8 of 14

    Liver: anatomy + functions A&P

    The liver is the body's central chemistry lab. It processes nutrients from the portal vein, detoxifies, makes plasma proteins, stores energy, produces bile, and regulates blood glucose between meals.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: ~1.5 kg, right upper quadrant under diaphragm. Four lobes (right, left, caudate, quadrate). Capsule (Glisson's).
    • Dual blood supply:
      • Hepatic artery (~25%): oxygenated blood.
      • Portal vein (~75%): nutrient-rich, deoxygenated blood from GI tract and spleen.
      • Both drain into hepatic sinusoids → hepatic veins → IVC.
    • Microscopic unit: hepatic lobule. Hexagonal; central vein in middle, portal triads at corners (hepatic artery, portal vein, bile duct). Hepatocytes arranged in plates.
    • Bile flow: opposite direction from blood — from central vein toward portal triad bile ducts → common hepatic duct → cystic duct (to gallbladder) or common bile duct (to duodenum).
    • Functions (a lot):
      • Metabolic: gluconeogenesis, glycogen storage, glycogenolysis, lipogenesis, ketogenesis, urea synthesis (from ammonia), cholesterol synthesis.
      • Synthetic: plasma proteins (albumin, clotting factors except VIII, complement proteins, transport proteins).
      • Detoxification: Phase I (cytochrome P450) + Phase II (conjugation). Drugs, hormones, toxins.
      • Bile production: ~600-1,200 mL/day.
      • Storage: glycogen, iron (ferritin), fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), B12.
      • Immune: Kupffer cells (resident macrophages) filter portal blood.
      • Activation: vitamin D 25-hydroxylation; thyroid T4 → T3 (partly).
    • Lab markers:
      • AST/ALT: hepatocellular injury (ALT more liver-specific).
      • ALP / GGT: cholestasis or biliary obstruction.
      • Bilirubin: hepatic processing.
      • Albumin, PT/INR: synthetic function.
      • Ammonia: detox capacity.

    Summary: liver failure consequences

    Lost functionClinical consequence
    Detox of ammoniaHepatic encephalopathy
    Albumin synthesisEdema, ascites
    Clotting factor synthesis↑ INR, bleeding
    Bilirubin processingJaundice
    Estrogen metabolismGynecomastia, spider angiomata, palmar erythema (in cirrhotic males)
    Portal flowPortal HTN → varices, splenomegaly, ascites

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Cirrhosis: fibrosis + nodular regeneration replaces normal architecture. Causes: alcohol, viral hepatitis (B, C), NAFLD/NASH, autoimmune. Decompensation: ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome. Acetaminophen toxicity: overdose depletes glutathione → toxic metabolite (NAPQI) destroys hepatocytes. Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC), most effective within 8-10 hours. Hepatic encephalopathy: precipitated by infection, GI bleed, constipation, electrolytes. Treat with lactulose (traps ammonia in colon) and rifaximin.

    DRAW IT

    Draw a hepatic lobule: hexagon with central vein, six portal triads at corners. Show plates of hepatocytes between them. Add arrows for blood flow (toward center) and bile flow (toward periphery).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which lab elevation is most specific for hepatocellular injury?
    2. DOK 4. Why does cirrhosis cause both ascites and bleeding?
    Show answers
    1. ALT (alanine aminotransferase). AST is also released from heart, muscle, RBC. AST > ALT 2:1 suggests alcoholic liver disease.
    2. Reduced albumin synthesis → ↓ oncotic pressure → fluid leaves vessels (ascites, edema). Portal hypertension drives more fluid out of splanchnic capillaries. Reduced clotting factor synthesis (II, VII, IX, X) → ↑ INR → bleeding tendency. Both reflect loss of synthetic function. Splenomegaly from portal HTN also sequesters platelets, compounding bleeding risk.

    GI · Concept 9 of 14

    Gallbladder + bile A&P

    The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile between meals. When fat enters the duodenum, CCK signals release, and bile emulsifies lipids for digestion.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: pear-shaped sac under the liver's right lobe. Cystic duct joins common hepatic duct → common bile duct → joins pancreatic duct at ampulla of Vater → enters duodenum via sphincter of Oddi.
    • Bile composition: bile salts (cholesterol-derived), cholesterol, phospholipids, bilirubin (waste from RBC breakdown), water, electrolytes.
    • Functions of bile:
      • Emulsify fats (mechanical breakup of fat globules into smaller droplets → ↑ surface area for lipase).
      • Form micelles (transport fat-soluble products to enterocyte).
      • Excrete bilirubin, cholesterol, and other waste.
      • Mild bactericidal activity.
    • Release:
      1. Fat (and protein) in duodenum → CCK from I cells.
      2. CCK contracts gallbladder + relaxes sphincter of Oddi.
      3. Bile flows into duodenum.
      4. Bile salts emulsify fat → absorbed in jejunum/ileum.
      5. ~95% of bile salts reabsorbed at terminal ileum → enterohepatic circulation back to liver (cycles 4-12× per meal).
    • Bilirubin pathway:
      • RBC breakdown → heme → biliverdin → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin (lipid-soluble, albumin-bound).
      • Liver conjugates bilirubin with glucuronic acid → conjugated (direct) bilirubin (water-soluble).
      • Conjugated bilirubin excreted in bile → gut bacteria reduce to urobilinogen → some reabsorbed (urine: urobilin = yellow); rest oxidized to stercobilin (stool: brown).

    Summary: causes of jaundice

    TypeMechanismBilirubin pattern
    Pre-hepaticHemolysis → ↑ unconjugated↑ Unconjugated
    HepaticHepatocyte dysfunctionMixed (uncon + con)
    Post-hepaticBiliary obstruction (stone, tumor)↑ Conjugated, ↑ ALP, ↑ GGT, dark urine, pale stool

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Cholelithiasis (gallstones): ~80% cholesterol, ~20% pigment. Risk factors: "4 F's" — fat, female, fertile, forty (plus rapid weight loss, OCP, ethnicity). Cholecystitis: stone obstructs cystic duct → gallbladder inflammation. RUQ pain, fever, Murphy's sign. Choledocholithiasis: stone in CBD → obstructive jaundice ± pancreatitis. ERCP for stone removal. Cholangitis: infected obstructed CBD. Charcot triad: RUQ pain + fever + jaundice. Reynolds pentad adds hypotension + AMS — septic shock.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the biliary tree: hepatic ducts → common hepatic → cystic → gallbladder; common bile duct + pancreatic duct meeting at the ampulla → duodenum. Mark sphincter of Oddi. Add arrows for CCK action.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What hormone triggers gallbladder contraction in response to a fatty meal?
    2. DOK 3. A patient with obstructive jaundice has pale stools and dark urine. Explain.
    Show answers
    1. CCK (cholecystokinin), from I cells in the duodenum.
    2. CBD obstruction prevents conjugated bilirubin from reaching the gut → no stercobilin → pale stools. The water-soluble conjugated bilirubin backs up into blood → kidney excretes it → dark (tea-colored) urine.

    GI · Concept 10 of 14

    Pancreas: exocrine function A&P

    The pancreas is two organs in one. The exocrine pancreas produces digestive enzymes and bicarbonate-rich fluid. The endocrine pancreas (islets) makes insulin and glucagon — covered in endocrine notes.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: retroperitoneal organ across L1-L2, behind stomach. Head (in C-loop of duodenum), neck, body, tail (extends to spleen).
    • Two outputs:
      • Main pancreatic duct (Wirsung) → joins CBD at ampulla of Vater.
      • Accessory duct (Santorini) in some people.
    • Acinar cells (~80% of pancreas): secrete digestive enzymes as inactive zymogens.
    • Duct cells: secrete bicarbonate-rich fluid (neutralizes acidic chyme to pH 7-8 in duodenum).
    • Pancreatic enzymes:
      • Proteases: trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase. All inactive until enterokinase (from duodenum) cleaves trypsinogen → trypsin, which activates the rest.
      • Pancreatic amylase: starch digestion.
      • Pancreatic lipase: triglyceride digestion (with bile salts).
      • Nucleases: DNase, RNase.
    • Control:
      • CCK (from I cells in duodenum, triggered by fat/protein) → acinar enzyme release.
      • Secretin (from S cells, triggered by acid in duodenum) → duct cell bicarbonate release.
      • Vagus stimulation (parasympathetic) augments both.

    Summary

    Cell / outputSubstanceFunction
    AcinarZymogens (trypsinogen, etc.) + amylase + lipaseDigest protein, carb, fat, nucleic acids
    Duct cellBicarbonate fluidNeutralize chyme
    Islet β / α / δInsulin / glucagon / somatostatinGlucose homeostasis (endocrine — see endocrine notes)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Acute pancreatitis: autodigestion. Most common causes: gallstones (cause obstruction at ampulla) and alcohol. Severe epigastric pain radiating to back, nausea, vomiting. Labs: ↑ lipase (more specific) and ↑ amylase. Management: NPO, IV fluids (aggressive), pain control, treat cause. Chronic pancreatitis: permanent damage → exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhea, weight loss) and eventually endocrine insufficiency (diabetes). Treat with pancreatic enzyme replacement. Pancreatic cancer: often presents late; Courvoisier sign (palpable non-tender gallbladder + jaundice) suggests pancreatic head mass obstructing CBD.

    Draw the pancreas with head in duodenal C-loop, body across L1-L2, tail to spleen. Add ducts and ampulla of Vater. Show CCK and secretin arrows from duodenum to pancreas with their effects.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Why are pancreatic enzymes secreted as zymogens?
    2. DOK 4. In pancreatitis, premature trypsinogen activation in the pancreas causes autodigestion. Explain.
    Show answers
    1. To prevent the pancreas from digesting itself. Zymogens are inactive precursors; activation happens only in the duodenum via enterokinase, where trypsin activates the rest in cascade.
    2. Normally trypsinogen is held inactive until reaching the duodenum. Disease (stone blocking ampulla, alcohol, etc.) can trigger premature intra-pancreatic activation. Activated trypsin then activates the entire cascade locally → autodigestion → inflammation, edema, hemorrhage, fat necrosis, and in severe cases systemic SIRS/ARDS.

    GI · Concept 11 of 14

    Carbohydrate digestion and absorption P

    Carbohydrates are broken down to monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) before absorption. The brush border does the last step. Lactose intolerance is the most common malabsorption you'll see clinically.

    Notes

    • Dietary carb forms:
      • Polysaccharides: starch, glycogen, fiber (indigestible).
      • Disaccharides: sucrose (glucose-fructose), lactose (glucose-galactose), maltose (glucose-glucose).
      • Monosaccharides: glucose, fructose, galactose.
    • Digestion pathway:
      1. Mouth: salivary amylase begins starch digestion.
      2. Stomach: amylase inactivated by acid.
      3. Duodenum: pancreatic amylase breaks starch to disaccharides + oligosaccharides.
      4. Brush border (enterocyte microvilli): lactase, sucrase, maltase split disaccharides into monosaccharides.
      5. Absorption: monosaccharides cross enterocyte → portal vein → liver.
    • Absorption mechanisms:
      • Glucose, galactose: secondary active transport via SGLT-1 (Na cotransport).
      • Fructose: facilitated diffusion via GLUT-5.
      • All exit basolateral side via GLUT-2 into portal blood.
    • Lactose intolerance: ↓ lactase activity (common in adults of African, Asian, Native American descent). Undigested lactose → osmotic diarrhea + bacterial fermentation (gas, bloating).
    • Fiber: indigestible. Soluble (oats, beans) → slows absorption, lowers cholesterol. Insoluble (wheat bran) → bulks stool, prevents constipation. Bacterial fermentation produces SCFAs.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) block renal glucose reabsorption — exploit the same transporter class to dump glucose in urine, lowering blood glucose and providing cardio-renal benefit in T2DM and HF.

    Trace one starch molecule from mouth to portal vein. Mark each digestion step and the transporter used at the enterocyte.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which transporter brings glucose into the enterocyte apical membrane?
    2. DOK 3. Why does undigested lactose cause diarrhea?
    Show answers
    1. SGLT-1 (sodium-glucose cotransporter, secondary active transport).
    2. Undigested lactose draws water into the gut lumen osmotically (osmotic diarrhea) and is fermented by colonic bacteria producing gas (bloating, flatulence) and short-chain fatty acids.

    GI · Concept 12 of 14

    Protein digestion and absorption P

    Protein digestion starts in the stomach with pepsin, finishes in the small intestine with pancreatic and brush border enzymes. Amino acids and small peptides are absorbed by Na-cotransport.

    Notes

    • Digestion pathway:
      1. Stomach: HCl denatures protein; pepsin (from chief cell pepsinogen) cleaves peptide bonds → polypeptides.
      2. Duodenum: enterokinase activates trypsinogen → trypsin → cascade activates chymotrypsin, elastase, carboxypeptidase A/B → smaller peptides + amino acids.
      3. Brush border peptidases (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases) finish the job.
      4. Absorption: amino acids, dipeptides, tripeptides taken up by enterocytes.
    • Absorption mechanisms:
      • Amino acids: multiple Na-cotransporters specific to AA class (neutral, acidic, basic, etc.).
      • Dipeptides and tripeptides: PepT1 transporter (H⁺-cotransport). Cleaved inside cell.
      • Exit basolateral side → portal vein → liver.
    • Neonates can absorb intact proteins briefly (gut permeability) → enables passive immunity from breast milk IgA. Closes within first months.
    • Essential amino acids (9; cannot be synthesized): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine.
    • Hartnup disease: defect in neutral AA transporter → ↓ tryptophan absorption → pellagra-like skin and neurologic symptoms (tryptophan is niacin precursor).
    • B12 absorption is unique:
      1. Pepsin releases B12 from food protein in stomach.
      2. B12 binds R-protein (salivary glands).
      3. Pancreatic enzymes cleave R-protein in duodenum.
      4. B12 binds intrinsic factor (from parietal cells).
      5. IF-B12 complex absorbed by specific receptor in terminal ileum.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pernicious anemia: autoimmune destruction of parietal cells → no intrinsic factor → no B12 absorption → megaloblastic anemia + neuro symptoms (subacute combined degeneration). Ileal resection or Crohn disease affecting the terminal ileum also causes B12 deficiency. Treat with IM or high-dose oral B12.

    DRAW IT

    Trace a dietary protein from stomach to amino acid in portal blood. Mark each enzyme. Then draw the B12 absorption path with all its handoffs.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What activates trypsinogen to trypsin?
    2. DOK 4. A vegan patient has megaloblastic anemia. Why is this expected?
    Show answers
    1. Enterokinase (enteropeptidase), released from duodenal mucosa. Trypsin then activates the rest of the protease cascade.
    2. B12 comes only from animal sources (or fortification). Strict vegans without B12 supplementation eventually deplete liver stores (~3-5 years' worth) and develop megaloblastic anemia + neurologic symptoms. Always supplement.

    GI · Concept 13 of 14

    Lipid digestion: micelles, chylomicrons, fat-soluble vitamins P

    Fats need extra steps because they don't dissolve in water. Bile emulsifies, lipase digests, micelles ferry products to the enterocyte, and chylomicrons carry absorbed fat into lymph rather than blood.

    Notes

    • Dietary lipid forms: triglycerides (most, ~90%), phospholipids, cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).
    • Digestion pathway:
      1. Mouth and stomach: minimal (lingual and gastric lipase contribute small amount).
      2. Duodenum: bile salts emulsify large fat globules into small droplets → ↑ surface area.
      3. Pancreatic lipase + colipase cleave triglycerides → 2 free fatty acids + monoglyceride.
      4. Products combine with bile salts to form micelles → ferry products through unstirred water layer to enterocyte microvilli.
      5. Inside enterocyte: re-esterified into triglycerides; packaged with cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoprotein B-48 into chylomicrons.
      6. Chylomicrons exit via lacteals → lymph → thoracic duct → left subclavian vein → systemic circulation. Bypass the liver initially.
    • Fat-soluble vitamins (ADEK): absorbed with fats. Stored in liver and adipose. Deficiency states reflect malabsorption (cholestasis, pancreatic insufficiency, celiac, CF).
    • Vitamin K specifically is also produced by colonic bacteria. Needed for clotting factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C, S. Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist.
    • Enterohepatic circulation: ~95% of bile salts reabsorbed in terminal ileum → portal vein → liver → recycled into bile. Liver only needs to synthesize ~5% of total bile pool per cycle.

    Summary

    StepWhereWhat happens
    EmulsificationDuodenumBile salts → small droplets
    HydrolysisDuodenum / jejunumPancreatic lipase → FFA + monoglyceride
    Micelle formationLumenBile salts + lipid products → ferries
    AbsorptionEnterocyteProducts diffuse into cell
    Chylomicron packagingInside enterocyteResyntehsized TGs + apoB-48
    TransportLacteal → thoracic ductLymph, not portal

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Steatorrhea (fatty, foul-smelling, floating stool): malabsorption of fat. Causes: pancreatic insufficiency (CF, chronic pancreatitis), bile insufficiency (biliary obstruction, ileal disease losing enterohepatic recycling), mucosal disease (celiac). Also lose fat-soluble vitamins (ADEK). Orlistat (anti-obesity drug): blocks pancreatic lipase → fat malabsorption → weight loss + steatorrhea side effect.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which lipoprotein carries dietary fat from gut to circulation?
    2. DOK 3. A patient with chronic pancreatitis develops easy bruising. Why?
    Show answers
    1. Chylomicrons.
    2. Chronic pancreatitis → ↓ pancreatic lipase → fat malabsorption → ↓ absorption of fat-soluble vitamin K → ↓ synthesis of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X → ↑ INR and bruising/bleeding. Replace with pancreatic enzymes + parenteral vitamin K.

    GI · Concept 14 of 14

    Common GI conditions CLIN

    Most GI presentations are one of these patterns: motility (GERD, IBS, gastroparesis), mucosal damage (PUD, IBD, celiac), obstruction (mechanical or ileus), inflammation (appendicitis, diverticulitis, pancreatitis, cholecystitis), bleeding (upper vs lower), or hepatobiliary (cirrhosis, hepatitis, cholestasis).

    Notes

    • GERD: LES dysfunction → heartburn, regurgitation, atypical (cough, hoarse, asthma). Complication: Barrett esophagus (premalignant). PPI + lifestyle.
    • PUD: H. pylori (~70-90% duodenal) and NSAIDs (most gastric). Epigastric pain, GI bleed, perforation. Test and treat H. pylori. PPI.
    • IBS: functional disorder. Abdominal pain related to defecation, altered bowel habits. No structural cause. Rome IV criteria. Manage diet (low FODMAP), antispasmodics, fiber, antidepressants.
    • IBD:
      • Crohn: anywhere mouth-to-anus, transmural, skip lesions, fistulas, terminal ileum favored. Smoking worsens.
      • UC: continuous from rectum, mucosa only, bloody diarrhea, ↑ colon cancer risk after years.
    • Appendicitis: obstruction of appendix lumen → inflammation. Periumbilical → RLQ pain, fever, anorexia, nausea. McBurney's point, Rovsing's, psoas, obturator signs. Surgical.
    • Diverticulitis: inflamed diverticulum (usually sigmoid). LLQ pain, fever, ↑ WBC. CT confirms. Bowel rest, antibiotics; surgery if complicated.
    • Hepatitis:
      • A: fecal-oral, acute, self-limited. Vaccine available.
      • B: blood/sex, can be chronic, hepatocellular cancer risk. Vaccine.
      • C: blood, often chronic, treatable now with DAAs. No vaccine.
      • D: only with B. E: like A, dangerous in pregnancy.
    • Cirrhosis (covered in liver concept): decompensation = ascites, varices, encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome.
    • Cholelithiasis / cholecystitis (covered in gallbladder concept).
    • Pancreatitis (covered in pancreas concept).
    • GI bleeding:
      • Upper (proximal to ligament of Treitz): hematemesis, melena. Causes: PUD, varices, Mallory-Weiss, malignancy.
      • Lower (distal): hematochezia. Causes: diverticulosis, AVM, hemorrhoids, neoplasm, ischemic colitis.
      • Resuscitate with IV fluids and blood; PPI for upper; octreotide for variceal; endoscopy.
    • Colorectal cancer: usually adenoma → adenocarcinoma sequence over years. Screening: colonoscopy q10y from age 45, or FIT yearly. Symptoms (late): change in habits, blood, weight loss, anemia.

    Summary

    PatternExamplesInitial action
    Acute abdomenAppendicitis, perforation, ischemiaSurgical consult, imaging
    Upper GI bleedPUD, varicesResuscitate, PPI, endoscopy
    Lower GI bleedDiverticular, AVMResuscitate, colonoscopy
    Chronic mucosal diseaseIBD, celiacEndoscopy + biopsy, specialty referral
    HepatobiliaryHepatitis, cirrhosis, stonesLFTs, imaging, hepatology

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Nursing assessment for acute abdomen: vital signs (sepsis screen), abdominal exam (tenderness, rebound, guarding, rigidity, bowel sounds), HEENT (jaundice, pallor), CV/lung (assess for shock), pain quality and location, last menses (always rule out pregnancy in reproductive-age females), medication review (anticoagulants, steroids, NSAIDs). NPO and IV access while workup proceeds.

    DRAW IT

    Build an "acute abdomen by quadrant" reference card. RUQ: cholecystitis, hepatitis. Epigastric: PUD, pancreatitis, MI. LUQ: splenic. RLQ: appendicitis. LLQ: diverticulitis. Diffuse: peritonitis, mesenteric ischemia, bowel obstruction.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient on long-term NSAIDs develops epigastric pain and melena. Most likely diagnosis?
    2. DOK 4. A cirrhotic patient with new altered mental status and asterixis. What's the diagnosis and three precipitants to look for?
    Show answers
    1. NSAID-induced gastric ulcer with upper GI bleed.
    2. Hepatic encephalopathy. Precipitants to investigate: infection (SBP, UTI, pneumonia), GI bleed (high protein load), constipation, electrolyte imbalance (hypokalemia, hyponatremia), medication (sedatives, opioids), dietary indiscretion. Treat the precipitant + lactulose + rifaximin.
