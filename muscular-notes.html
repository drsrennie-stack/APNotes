Concept Notes

Muscular System

Muscle has one job: turn chemical energy (ATP) into movement or force. Skeletal muscle moves bones. Smooth muscle moves substances through tubes. Cardiac muscle pumps blood (see cardiovascular notes Concept 4a). Use the View filter for Anatomy / Physiology / Combined and the Depth filter for Essentials (BIO 304) or Full (BIO 431).

    Muscular · Concept 1 of 12

    Three muscle tissue types A

    Skeletal, cardiac, smooth — three muscle tissues, three jobs. Each shares the basic contractile machinery (actin + myosin) but is built differently for its specific demand.

    Notes

    • Skeletal muscle:
      • Voluntary, attached to bones.
      • Long, cylindrical, multinucleated fibers.
      • Striated (sarcomere alignment visible).
      • Innervated by somatic motor neurons; one neuromuscular junction per fiber.
      • Action potential ~2 ms; can tetanize.
    • Cardiac muscle:
      • Involuntary; only in heart.
      • Branched, mono- or binucleated cells.
      • Striated; intercalated discs (gap junctions + desmosomes).
      • Long AP (~250 ms with plateau); cannot tetanize.
      • Detailed in cardiovascular notes Concept 4a.
    • Smooth muscle:
      • Involuntary; in walls of hollow organs (vessels, gut, airways, bladder, uterus).
      • Spindle-shaped, single nucleus.
      • Not striated (actin and myosin not in regular sarcomeres).
      • Slow, sustained contractions with less ATP.
      • Innervated by ANS, hormones, local factors.
    • Common to all three:
      • Excitability (respond to stimulus).
      • Contractility (shorten or generate force).
      • Extensibility (can be stretched).
      • Elasticity (return to resting length).
      • Actin + myosin as contractile machinery.

    Summary

    FeatureSkeletalCardiacSmooth
    ControlVoluntaryInvoluntaryInvoluntary
    Cell shapeLong, cylindrical, multinucleateBranched, 1-2 nucleiSpindle, 1 nucleus
    StriationsYesYesNo
    Intercalated discsNoYesNo
    TetanizeYesNo (long refractory)Variable
    SpeedFastModerateSlow

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which muscle type lacks striations?
    2. DOK 2. Why can't cardiac muscle tetanize?
    Show answers
    1. Smooth muscle.
    2. Long action potential plateau and long refractory period mean a second stimulus cannot fire until the first contraction is nearly complete. This is essential — the heart must relax between beats to fill.

    Muscular · Concept 2 of 12

    Skeletal muscle gross anatomy and fascia A

    A muscle is a hierarchy: whole muscle → bundles (fascicles) → fibers (cells) → myofibrils → myofilaments. Each level is wrapped in connective tissue that anchors to tendons.

    Notes

    • CT wrappings (outer to inner):
      • Epimysium: surrounds entire muscle.
      • Perimysium: surrounds each fascicle (bundle of fibers).
      • Endomysium: surrounds each muscle fiber (cell).
      • All three converge to form tendons that anchor to bone.
    • Muscle attachments:
      • Origin: stationary attachment (usually proximal).
      • Insertion: moves toward the origin during contraction (usually distal).
      • Some muscles cross multiple joints.
    • Functional roles in movement:
      • Agonist (prime mover): primary force generator.
      • Antagonist: opposes the agonist (relaxes when agonist contracts).
      • Synergist: assists agonist.
      • Fixator: stabilizes the origin or proximal joint.
    • Fascia:
      • Superficial fascia (hypodermis): adipose + loose CT.
      • Deep fascia: dense CT surrounding muscles, separating compartments.
      • Forms compartments — basis of compartment syndrome (rising pressure within a fascial compartment compromises perfusion).
    • Blood supply: rich, especially in active muscles. Capillaries between each fiber.
    • Innervation: each fiber has one NMJ from a somatic motor neuron.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Compartment syndrome (introduced in skeletal notes): pressure within a fascial compartment exceeds perfusion pressure → ischemia, necrosis. Pain out of proportion to injury, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia. Surgical fasciotomy is the only definitive treatment. Common in tibial fractures, crush injuries, reperfusion.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which CT layer surrounds a single muscle fiber?
    2. DOK 3. Why does compartment syndrome require urgent fasciotomy?
    Show answers
    1. Endomysium.
    2. The fascia surrounding a compartment is inelastic. Once pressure exceeds perfusion pressure, capillary flow stops → muscle and nerve ischemia → irreversible damage within hours. Fasciotomy releases the compartment, restoring flow.

    Muscular · Concept 3 of 12

    Muscle microanatomy: sarcomere, myofilaments, T-tubules, SR A&P

    The sarcomere is the functional unit. Two filaments (actin and myosin) slide past each other in a regulated way. The sarcoplasmic reticulum stores Ca²⁺ and the T-tubules deliver the electrical signal deep into the cell.

    Notes

    • Sarcolemma: muscle cell membrane. Has invaginations called T-tubules.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR): modified smooth ER. Stores Ca²⁺. Terminal cisternae flank each T-tubule (triad = T-tubule + 2 terminal cisternae).
    • Myofibrils: the contractile rods inside the fiber. Each is a chain of sarcomeres end-to-end.
    • Sarcomere (Z-disc to Z-disc):
      • Z disc: anchor point; defines the sarcomere boundary.
      • A band: full myosin (dark band). Includes overlap with actin.
      • I band: only actin (light band). Includes Z disc.
      • H zone: only myosin, no overlap (lighter region in middle of A band).
      • M line: anchors myosin in the middle of the sarcomere.
    • Thick filaments (myosin):
      • ~300 myosin molecules per thick filament.
      • Each myosin has a globular head (binds actin, hydrolyzes ATP) and a long tail.
    • Thin filaments (actin):
      • F-actin (filamentous, made of G-actin monomers).
      • Tropomyosin: covers myosin-binding sites on actin at rest.
      • Troponin complex (TnT, TnI, TnC): TnC binds Ca²⁺ → conformational change → tropomyosin shifts → myosin can bind.
    • Other structural proteins (deep dive):
      • Titin: huge spring-like protein from Z-disc to M-line; elastic recoil.
      • Nebulin: anchors actin.
      • Dystrophin: connects sarcomere to sarcolemma. Mutated in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

    Diagram: sarcomere bands

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD): X-linked recessive defect in dystrophin → muscle fibers tear during contraction → progressive weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy, wheelchair by ~12 yr, respiratory failure. Gower's sign (using hands to climb up legs to stand). Cardiac troponin: TnI and TnT are the most specific serum markers of myocardial cell injury (rise in MI).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which sarcomere region narrows during contraction but the A band does not?
    2. DOK 3. Why are cardiac troponins T and I used as MI markers?
    Show answers
    1. H zone (and I band). The A band reflects total myosin length, which is constant.
    2. They are unique to cardiac muscle (different isoforms than skeletal). When cardiac myocytes die during MI, troponins leak into blood and can be measured. Specificity for cardiac injury is high; sensitivity rises with high-sensitivity assays.

    Muscular · Concept 4 of 12

    Excitation-contraction coupling P

    Excitation (action potential) and contraction (cross-bridge cycling) are linked by Ca²⁺. The signal arrives at the NMJ; depolarization spreads along the sarcolemma and into T-tubules; the SR releases Ca²⁺; troponin says "go."

    Notes

    • The eight-step chain (skeletal muscle):
      1. Motor neuron AP arrives at axon terminal at NMJ.
      2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ enters terminal.
      3. ACh released into synaptic cleft.
      4. ACh binds nicotinic receptors on motor end plate → Na⁺ enters → end plate potential → triggers AP in sarcolemma.
      5. AP propagates along sarcolemma + down T-tubules.
      6. T-tubule depolarization opens voltage-gated DHP receptors → mechanically linked to ryanodine receptors (RyR1) on SR → SR releases Ca²⁺ (mechanical coupling, no extracellular Ca²⁺ needed in skeletal muscle).
      7. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C → tropomyosin shifts → myosin-binding sites on actin exposed.
      8. Cross-bridge cycling begins (next concept).
    • Contrast with cardiac muscle: cardiac muscle uses calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) and depends on extracellular Ca²⁺. See cardiovascular notes Concept 4a.
    • Relaxation:
      1. AP stops; ACh degraded by acetylcholinesterase.
      2. SR Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) pumps Ca²⁺ back into SR (active transport).
      3. Ca²⁺ falls → troponin releases tropomyosin → myosin-binding sites covered → cross-bridges detach → muscle relaxes.
    • Drug points:
      • AChE inhibitors (neostigmine, pyridostigmine, donepezil, organophosphates) ↑ ACh at NMJ → more contraction (used in myasthenia; poisoning with pesticides).
      • Succinylcholine: nicotinic agonist (depolarizing) → sustained depolarization → flaccid paralysis (after brief fasciculations) → used in rapid sequence intubation.
      • Vecuronium, rocuronium: non-depolarizing nicotinic antagonists → flaccid paralysis (for surgery).
      • Botulinum toxin: blocks ACh release at NMJ → flaccid paralysis. Therapeutic (Botox for dystonia, spasticity, migraines, cosmesis).
      • Dantrolene: blocks SR Ca²⁺ release → treats malignant hyperthermia.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Myasthenia gravis: autoantibodies against the nicotinic ACh receptor at NMJ → fewer functional receptors → fatigable weakness. Most prominent in eye muscles (ptosis, diplopia), face, and proximal limbs. Improves with rest; worsens at end of day. Treat with AChE inhibitors (boost ACh), immunosuppressants, thymectomy (often thymic hyperplasia or thymoma).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What ion binds troponin to start contraction?
    2. DOK 4. Why does succinylcholine cause brief fasciculations before paralysis?
    Show answers
    1. Calcium (Ca²⁺).
    2. Succinylcholine is a nicotinic AChR agonist that opens the channels and keeps them open. Initial channel opening triggers an AP and brief muscle contraction (fasciculation). Sustained depolarization then inactivates voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → the muscle cannot fire another AP → flaccid paralysis. Brief muscle fasciculations precede paralysis; can cause myalgia and hyperkalemia (a clinical concern).

    Muscular · Concept 5 of 12

    Sliding filament mechanism + cross-bridge cycle P

    Filaments don't shorten — they slide. Myosin heads pull actin toward the center of the sarcomere using ATP, one cycle at a time, until Ca²⁺ falls.

    Notes

    • Sliding filament theory: during contraction, actin filaments slide past myosin filaments → Z discs pulled closer → sarcomere shortens. The filaments themselves stay the same length.
    • What shortens / what doesn't:
      • Shortens: H zone, I band, sarcomere as a whole.
      • Stays the same: A band (= myosin filament length), individual actin or myosin filament lengths.
    • Cross-bridge cycle (4 steps, repeating):
      1. Cross-bridge formation: myosin head (energized, with ADP + Pi bound) attaches to exposed actin site.
      2. Power stroke: myosin head pivots, pulling actin toward sarcomere center. ADP + Pi released.
      3. Cross-bridge detachment: new ATP binds myosin → myosin releases from actin.
      4. Re-energizing: ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP + Pi → myosin head "cocks" back, ready for the next cycle.
    • Continues while: Ca²⁺ present AND ATP available. When Ca²⁺ falls (Concept 4 relaxation) → tropomyosin re-covers actin sites → cycle stops.
    • Rigor mortis:
      • At death, ATP synthesis stops.
      • SR can't pump Ca²⁺ back → Ca²⁺ stays elevated → cross-bridges stay attached.
      • Without ATP to detach myosin, muscles stay locked in contraction (rigor).
      • Resolves over hours-days as proteolysis breaks down filaments.
    • Twitch: single AP → single contraction (~50-100 ms). Has latent, contraction, relaxation phases.
    • Summation: AP arrives before relaxation complete → contractions sum.
    • Tetanus: AP frequency so high that no relaxation occurs between → sustained maximum contraction. Unfused (incomplete) → fused (complete) tetanus. Note: "tetanus" here means physiologic sustained contraction, not the disease.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Rigor mortis begins ~2-4 hours after death, peaks ~12 hours, resolves ~24-48 hours. Forensic time-of-death estimation. ATP and muscle: in extreme exercise, ATP supply temporarily lags demand; you don't get "out of ATP" entirely (would be rigor) but creatine phosphate buffers + lactic acid accumulates. After death, all production stops, leading to rigor.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which sarcomere band stays the same length during contraction?
    2. DOK 3. Why is ATP required for muscle relaxation, not just contraction?
    Show answers
    1. A band (= myosin filament length).
    2. ATP binds myosin to detach it from actin (cross-bridge detachment), and ATP powers SERCA to pump Ca²⁺ back into the SR. Without ATP, cross-bridges remain attached AND Ca²⁺ stays elevated → rigor. Both detachment and reuptake fail at the same time.

    Muscular · Concept 6 of 12

    Motor unit and NMJ A&P

    A motor unit is one motor neuron + all the fibers it innervates. Small motor units = fine control. Large units = power.

    Notes

    • Motor unit: one alpha motor neuron + all the muscle fibers it innervates (all-or-none — when the neuron fires, all its fibers contract together).
    • Size principle:
      • Small motor units (few fibers per neuron): fine control. Examples — extraocular muscles (~10 fibers per neuron), intrinsic hand muscles.
      • Large motor units (many fibers per neuron): power, not precision. Examples — quadriceps, gastrocnemius (~1,000 fibers per neuron).
      • Recruitment order: small (slow) units first, large (fast) later as force demand increases.
    • Force gradation:
      • Recruitment: more motor units activated → more force.
      • Frequency: higher AP frequency → temporal summation → up to tetanus.
    • Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) anatomy:
      • Axon terminal containing ACh-filled vesicles.
      • Synaptic cleft.
      • Motor end plate: specialized region of sarcolemma with junctional folds and densely packed nicotinic AChRs.
      • Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft degrades ACh after release.
    • NMJ disease:
      • Myasthenia gravis: anti-AChR antibodies → fewer functional receptors → fatigable weakness.
      • Lambert-Eaton syndrome: anti-presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel antibodies → ↓ ACh release. Often paraneoplastic (small cell lung cancer). Strength improves with use (opposite of MG).
      • Botulism: Clostridium botulinum toxin blocks ACh release → descending flaccid paralysis. "Floppy baby" in infants.
      • Tetanus (the disease, distinct from physiological tetanus): Clostridium tetani toxin blocks inhibitory NT release in CNS → sustained excitation → spastic paralysis ("lockjaw").

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Myasthenia vs Lambert-Eaton (key distinction): MG weakens with use ("eyelids droop as the day goes on"); LEMS strengthens with use ("first few squeezes weak, gets stronger"). Both involve NMJ but at different sites. MG: postsynaptic AChR; LEMS: presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel. Anti-AChR Abs in MG; anti-VGCC Abs in LEMS, often paraneoplastic.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Why do extraocular muscles have very small motor units?
    2. DOK 3. A patient has progressive descending paralysis after eating canned food. Mechanism?
    Show answers
    1. Fine motor control. Small motor units (few fibers per neuron) allow precise adjustment of muscle force — needed for tracking and visual control.
    2. Botulism. Botulinum toxin blocks ACh release at NMJ (and at parasympathetic synapses), causing descending flaccid paralysis. Treat with antitoxin + supportive care.

    Muscular · Concept 7 of 12

    Muscle metabolism: ATP sources and fatigue P

    Muscle has three ATP sources for three time scales: instant (creatine phosphate), short-term (anaerobic glycolysis), long-term (aerobic respiration). Fatigue is multi-factorial — not just lactic acid.

    Notes

    • Three ATP sources:
      1. Creatine phosphate (CP):
        • Immediate, but brief (~10 seconds).
        • CP + ADP → ATP + creatine (catalyzed by creatine kinase).
        • Replenished at rest from ATP + creatine.
      2. Anaerobic glycolysis:
        • Lasts ~30 seconds to ~2 minutes.
        • Glucose → pyruvate → lactate (in cytoplasm).
        • Net 2 ATP per glucose.
        • Produces H⁺ (acidosis) along with lactate; H⁺ contributes to fatigue and burning sensation, more than lactate itself.
      3. Aerobic respiration:
        • Sustained activity.
        • Glucose, fatty acids, amino acids → pyruvate → mitochondrial Krebs cycle + ETC.
        • Net ~30-32 ATP per glucose.
        • Requires O₂; needs blood flow and time.
    • Oxygen debt (EPOC): elevated post-exercise oxygen consumption to:
      • Restore CP and ATP stores.
      • Convert lactate back to glucose (Cori cycle in liver).
      • Replace O₂ in myoglobin and Hb.
      • Re-equilibrate temperature and hormones.
    • Muscle fatigue causes (multifactorial):
      • ↑ H⁺ (acidosis) interferes with contractile machinery.
      • K⁺ accumulation in T-tubules reduces excitability.
      • Depletion of glycogen, CP.
      • Inadequate Ca²⁺ release from SR.
      • Central fatigue (CNS-mediated, motivation, neurotransmitter changes).
    • Heat production: muscle is ~75% inefficient → much energy lost as heat. Major contributor to thermoregulation (shivering generates heat).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Rhabdomyolysis: muscle injury (crush, prolonged immobility, severe exertion, statins, drugs) → myocyte breakdown → myoglobin released → AKI (myoglobinuria), hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, ↑ CK. Treat: aggressive IV fluids, monitor electrolytes, address cause. Suspect in anyone with cola-colored urine after exertion or prolonged immobilization.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which ATP source is fastest but has the shortest duration?
    2. DOK 4. A marathon runner collapses with dark urine, K 6.5, Cr 2.5. Diagnosis and immediate intervention?
    Show answers
    1. Creatine phosphate (CP). Instant; lasts ~10 seconds.
    2. Rhabdomyolysis with AKI. Aggressive IV fluids (NS) to flush myoglobin and protect kidneys. Treat hyperkalemia (calcium gluconate for myocardial protection; insulin + glucose + albuterol to shift; consider bicarbonate to alkalinize urine and shift K). Hospitalize. Monitor CK trend and renal function.

    Muscular · Concept 8 of 12

    Muscle fiber types: Type I, IIa, IIx P

    Slow oxidative (Type I) fibers fatigue resistant. Fast glycolytic (Type IIx) fibers explosive. Type IIa fibers in between. Genetics, hormones, and training shift the mix.

    Notes

    • Type I (slow oxidative, "slow twitch", red):
      • High myoglobin (red color), high mitochondria, high capillary density.
      • Aerobic metabolism dominant.
      • Slow contraction, fatigue resistant.
      • Postural muscles (paraspinals, soleus, deep neck flexors).
      • Endurance athletes have proportionally more.
    • Type IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic, intermediate, pink):
      • Combination of oxidative + glycolytic capacity.
      • Faster than Type I; more fatigue-resistant than Type IIx.
      • Suited to moderate-intensity sustained activity (e.g., middle-distance running).
    • Type IIx (fast glycolytic, "fast twitch", white):
      • Low myoglobin, fewer mitochondria, fewer capillaries.
      • Anaerobic glycolysis dominant.
      • Fast, powerful, fatigue quickly.
      • Sprinters, weightlifters have more.
    • Training effects:
      • Endurance training: ↑ mitochondrial density, ↑ capillaries; shifts Type IIx → Type IIa.
      • Strength training: ↑ fiber cross-sectional area (hypertrophy), modest fiber-type shift.
      • Genetic ceiling on fiber type ratio.
    • Disuse / immobilization: rapid atrophy, especially of Type I (postural) muscles. Astronauts and bedbound patients lose mass quickly.

    Summary

    PropertyType IType IIaType IIx
    SpeedSlowFastFastest
    Fatigue resistanceHighModerateLow
    ColorRedPinkWhite
    MetabolismOxidativeBothGlycolytic
    Best forEndurance, postureMiddle distanceSprint, power

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which fiber type is best suited for marathon running?
    2. DOK 2. What change does endurance training cause within Type II fibers?
    Show answers
    1. Type I (slow oxidative).
    2. Shift from Type IIx toward Type IIa — more oxidative capacity, fatigue resistance.

    Muscular · Concept 9 of 12

    Length-tension and force-velocity relationships P

    Muscle generates maximum force when sarcomeres are at optimal overlap. Stretch too much or shorten too much, and force drops. Move faster, and you can't generate as much force.

    Notes

    • Length-tension curve:
      • Maximum tension at optimal sarcomere length (~2.0-2.2 µm in skeletal): myosin heads have full access to actin binding sites.
      • Overstretched: filaments don't overlap enough → fewer cross-bridges → less force.
      • Over-shortened: filaments collide; Z discs press into thick filaments → less force.
    • Why this matters:
      • Body keeps muscles near optimal length most of the time via reflex tone.
      • Joint geometry positions muscles for best force generation in expected range.
      • For cardiac muscle: Frank-Starling mechanism uses this principle — more preload (stretch) → more force, up to a point. See cardiovascular notes.
    • Force-velocity curve:
      • Concentric contraction (shortening): force ↓ as velocity ↑. Heavy loads can only be lifted slowly.
      • Isometric (no length change): peak force.
      • Eccentric (lengthening under load): force higher than isometric — explains why eccentric loading causes more soreness and microtrauma.
    • Isometric vs isotonic contractions:
      • Isometric: length constant, tension changes (holding a heavy object still).
      • Isotonic concentric: tension constant, muscle shortens (lifting).
      • Isotonic eccentric: tension constant, muscle lengthens (lowering).
    • Muscle tone: low-level continuous contraction even at rest. Maintains posture, joint stability. Lost in flaccid paralysis; exaggerated in spasticity.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Eccentric exercise (e.g., downhill running, lowering weights slowly) produces more microtrauma and delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) than concentric. Useful for tendon rehabilitation (eccentric loading promotes tendon remodeling). Spasticity (UMN lesion): velocity-dependent ↑ resistance to passive stretch. Treat with PT, baclofen, botulinum injections, dantrolene.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which contraction type generates the most force per cross-bridge?
    2. DOK 3. Why does an extremely stretched muscle generate less force?
    Show answers
    1. Eccentric (lengthening under load).
    2. At extreme stretch, actin and myosin filaments overlap less — fewer cross-bridges can form simultaneously, so total tension drops.

    Muscular · Concept 10 of 12

    Smooth muscle physiology P

    Smooth muscle works differently from skeletal — slower, more sustained, more efficient. It is regulated by ANS, hormones, and local factors, and can stay contracted for hours without fatigue.

    Notes

    • Structure:
      • Single-nucleus spindle cells, no sarcomeres (no striations).
      • Actin and myosin present, but in irregular networks, not sarcomere units.
      • Dense bodies (analogous to Z discs) anchor actin.
      • Found in walls of GI, vessels, bronchi, bladder, uterus, eye (iris, ciliary).
    • Two organizational types:
      • Single-unit (visceral): cells electrically coupled via gap junctions → contract as a sheet. Examples: gut, bladder, uterus, small vessels.
      • Multi-unit: each cell innervated individually → fine control. Examples: large arteries, iris muscles, arrector pili, vas deferens.
    • Activation (different from skeletal):
      • Ca²⁺ enters cytoplasm (from extracellular AND SR).
      • Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin (not troponin).
      • Ca-calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK).
      • MLCK phosphorylates myosin light chain → activates myosin head → cross-bridge cycling.
      • Relaxation: myosin light chain phosphatase removes the phosphate.
    • Properties:
      • Slow contractions (seconds rather than ms).
      • Latch state: can maintain force with minimal ATP — keeps vessels and sphincters tonically contracted.
      • Plasticity: can adapt length over time (stress relaxation) — bladder fills gradually without rising pressure.
      • Spontaneous contraction in some (pacemaker cells in gut, uterus).
    • Regulation:
      • ANS: sympathetic and parasympathetic act on different receptors per tissue (see nervous system ANS receptor primer).
      • Hormones: oxytocin → uterus; angiotensin II → vessels; histamine → vessels and bronchi.
      • Local: tissue hypoxia → vasodilation; stretch → contraction (myogenic).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Asthma medications work on smooth muscle of bronchi: β2 agonists (albuterol) relax via cAMP cascade; muscarinic antagonists (ipratropium) prevent constriction. Oxytocin (Pitocin) drives uterine smooth muscle contraction in labor. Constipation drugs: prokinetics like metoclopramide and erythromycin stimulate GI smooth muscle. Bladder overactivity: muscarinic antagonists (oxybutynin, tolterodine) reduce detrusor contraction.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. What protein replaces troponin in smooth muscle Ca²⁺ signaling?
    2. DOK 3. Why can smooth muscle maintain tone with minimal ATP?
    Show answers
    1. Calmodulin (binds Ca²⁺ → activates MLCK → phosphorylates myosin).
    2. Latch state: dephosphorylated cross-bridges remain attached for prolonged periods at low ATP cost. Vessels and sphincters can hold tone for hours.

    Muscular · Concept 11 of 12

    Muscle naming + major groups A

    Muscle names usually encode location, shape, size, action, or origin/insertion. Memorize the patterns and you can decode hundreds of muscle names.

    Notes

    • Naming conventions:
      • Location: brachii (arm), abdominis (abdomen), femoris (thigh), oris (mouth).
      • Shape: deltoid (triangular), trapezius (trapezoid), rhomboid (diamond), serratus (saw-toothed).
      • Size: maximus, minimus, longus, brevis.
      • Direction of fibers: rectus (straight), oblique, transverse.
      • Number of heads: biceps (2), triceps (3), quadriceps (4).
      • Action: flexor, extensor, abductor, adductor, levator, depressor.
      • Origin and insertion: sternocleidomastoid (origin: sternum + clavicle; insertion: mastoid).
    • Major muscle groups — head and neck:
      • Facial expression: orbicularis oculi/oris, zygomaticus, frontalis (CN VII).
      • Mastication: masseter, temporalis, pterygoids (CN V).
      • Neck: SCM (rotates head opposite, flexes neck; CN XI); trapezius (CN XI); platysma.
    • Trunk:
      • Anterior: pectoralis major and minor, rectus abdominis ("six pack"), external and internal obliques, transversus abdominis.
      • Posterior: trapezius, latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, erector spinae group.
      • Diaphragm: principal muscle of inspiration (C3-C5, phrenic nerve).
      • Intercostals: external (inspire), internal (forced expiration).
    • Upper limb:
      • Shoulder: deltoid, rotator cuff (SITS — Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis).
      • Arm: biceps brachii (flex elbow, supinate), triceps brachii (extend elbow), brachialis (flex elbow).
      • Forearm flexors (anterior): finger flexors, pronator teres.
      • Forearm extensors (posterior): finger extensors, supinator.
      • Hand: thenar (thumb), hypothenar (pinky), lumbricals, interossei.
    • Lower limb:
      • Gluteal: gluteus maximus (extend hip), gluteus medius/minimus (abduct, stabilize pelvis).
      • Thigh anterior: quadriceps femoris (4 heads — rectus femoris, vastus lateralis/medialis/intermedius). Extend knee.
      • Thigh medial: adductors.
      • Thigh posterior: hamstrings (biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus). Flex knee, extend hip.
      • Leg anterior: tibialis anterior (dorsiflexion).
      • Leg posterior: gastrocnemius, soleus (plantar flexion via Achilles tendon).
      • Foot intrinsics.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Rotator cuff tear: supraspinatus most commonly injured (impingement under acromion). Pain with overhead reach. Quadriceps weakness: classic sign of femoral nerve injury, advanced osteoarthritis, prolonged immobilization. Difficulty rising from a chair. Achilles rupture: sudden pain in calf, often during push-off. Thompson test positive (squeezing calf with patient prone doesn't produce plantar flexion).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which four muscles make up the rotator cuff?
    2. DOK 2. Which muscle's name tells you it has three heads?
    Show answers
    1. SITS: Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis.
    2. Triceps (brachii or surae).

    Muscular · Concept 12 of 12

    Common muscle conditions CLIN

    Muscle disease falls into a few patterns: inherited dystrophy, autoimmune myositis, drug/toxin-induced, neuromuscular junction disorder, simple injury, or rhabdomyolysis. Each has distinct lab + EMG patterns.

    Notes

    • Muscle injury patterns:
      • Strain: muscle/tendon overstretch or tear. Pain, swelling, decreased function. RICE.
      • Contusion: blunt trauma. Hematoma in muscle.
      • Cramp: involuntary painful contraction. Often dehydration, electrolyte imbalance.
      • DOMS: delayed-onset muscle soreness from eccentric work. Peaks 24-72 hours; self-limited.
    • Muscular dystrophies (inherited):
      • Duchenne (X-linked): dystrophin absent. Onset ~3-5 yr. Calf pseudohypertrophy. Gower's sign. Wheelchair by ~12. Cardiomyopathy + respiratory failure by 20s-30s. ↑ CK.
      • Becker (X-linked): dystrophin reduced/abnormal. Milder, later onset.
      • Myotonic dystrophy (autosomal dominant): myotonia (delayed relaxation), facial weakness, cataracts, cardiac, endocrine features.
    • Inflammatory myopathies (autoimmune):
      • Polymyositis: proximal muscle weakness, ↑ CK, anti-Jo1 antibody. T-cell mediated.
      • Dermatomyositis: same + skin findings (heliotrope rash on eyelids, Gottron papules on knuckles). Paraneoplastic associations.
      • Inclusion body myositis: older adults, distal and proximal involvement, slower course.
    • NMJ disorders (review):
      • Myasthenia gravis: anti-AChR Abs. Fatigable, especially ocular.
      • Lambert-Eaton: anti-VGCC Abs. Strengthens with use. Paraneoplastic.
      • Botulism: blocks ACh release. Descending paralysis.
    • Drug-induced muscle disorders:
      • Statin myopathy: myalgias, ↑ CK, occasionally rhabdomyolysis. Manage: stop drug, alternative, hydration.
      • Corticosteroid myopathy: chronic use → proximal weakness, atrophy of Type II fibers. Normal CK (distinguishes from polymyositis).
      • Colchicine, alcohol, fibrates: also implicated.
    • Rhabdomyolysis (review Concept 7): crush injury, severe exercise, statins, prolonged immobilization (often elderly fall + lying for hours), seizures, drugs (cocaine, heroin). Triad: myalgia, muscle weakness, dark urine. Labs: ↑↑ CK, K, phosphate; ↓ Ca; AKI. Aggressive IV fluids.

    • Compartment syndrome (review Concept 2): surgical emergency.
    • Fibromyalgia: chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, cognitive symptoms. Tender points; no inflammation, no muscle weakness on exam, normal CK. Treat with exercise, sleep hygiene, CBT, certain meds (duloxetine, milnacipran, pregabalin, amitriptyline).
    • Malignant hyperthermia (deep dive): rare autosomal dominant. RYR1 mutation. Exposure to volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine → uncontrolled SR Ca²⁺ release → muscle hypermetabolism, hyperthermia (often > 40 °C), rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, acidosis. Treat: dantrolene (blocks RYR1), stop trigger, cooling, supportive.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Nursing assessment of muscle complaints: distribution (proximal vs distal; symmetric vs not), onset (acute vs gradual), weakness vs pain, fatigability, associated systemic features (rash, organ involvement, family history), drug history (statins, steroids), creatine kinase as a screen for muscle injury. Hospitalize any patient with dark urine after exertion or prolonged immobilization until rhabdomyolysis is excluded.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A child has progressive proximal weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy, and uses his hands to push up his legs to stand. Most likely diagnosis?
    2. DOK 4. Why is corticosteroid myopathy distinguishable from polymyositis even though both cause proximal weakness?
    Show answers
    1. Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Gower's sign + calf pseudohypertrophy + onset in early childhood is classic.
    2. Corticosteroid myopathy: normal CK, normal EMG findings of inflammation. Polymyositis: ↑ CK, inflammatory infiltrate on biopsy, anti-Jo1 may be positive. Polymyositis is autoimmune; corticosteroid myopathy is drug-induced and reversible by reducing/stopping steroids.
