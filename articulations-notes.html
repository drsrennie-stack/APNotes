Concept Notes

Articulations (Joints)

Every junction between two bones is a joint. Classify them two ways: by what holds them together (structural: fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial) and by how much they move (functional: synarthrosis, amphiarthrosis, diarthrosis). The mobility / stability trade-off shows up at every joint in the body.

    Articulations · Concept 1 of 10

    How joints are classified A

    There are two ways to classify a joint: structurally (what tissue connects the bones) and functionally (how much movement is allowed). They overlap but don't perfectly align, so know both.

    Structural classification (what holds it together)

    • Fibrous joints: bones joined by dense connective tissue. No joint cavity. Examples: skull sutures, syndesmosis (tibia-fibula), gomphosis (tooth-socket).
    • Cartilaginous joints: bones joined by cartilage. No joint cavity. Examples: epiphyseal plate (hyaline), pubic symphysis (fibrocartilage).
    • Synovial joints: bones separated by a fluid-filled joint cavity. Most movable, most clinically important. Examples: knee, shoulder, hip, IP joints.

    Functional classification (how much it moves)

    • Synarthrosis: immovable. Stability over mobility. (Most fibrous, some cartilaginous.)
    • Amphiarthrosis: slightly movable. Compromise between stability and motion. (Most cartilaginous, some fibrous.)
    • Diarthrosis: freely movable. Mobility over stability. (All synovial joints are diarthroses.)
    Big idea: structure predicts function. Fibrous & cartilaginous joints trade motion for stability and durability. Synovial joints add a fluid-filled cavity to maximize motion, but they pay for it with a more complex, injury-prone architecture.

    Articulations · Concept 2 of 10

    Fibrous joints: sutures, syndesmoses, gomphoses A

    Bones joined directly by dense fibrous connective tissue. No joint cavity. The length of the fibers between the bones predicts how much (if any) movement is possible.

    Sutures (skull)

    • Very short fibers; bones tightly interlocked along irregular margins.
    • Functional: synarthrosis (immovable in adults).
    • In infants: open (fontanels) to allow brain growth and birth canal passage. Sutures close by adulthood. In old age, sutures often ossify into synostoses (bony fusion).
    • Examples: sagittal, coronal, lambdoid, squamous.

    Syndesmoses

    • Bones connected by a ligament or interosseous membrane; fibers are longer than sutures, so a small amount of give exists.
    • Functional class depends on fiber length:
      • Short fibers (distal tibiofibular joint): amphiarthrosis — slight movement only.
      • Longer fibers (interosseous membrane between radius & ulna, or tibia & fibula along the shaft): allows more give. Permits pronation/supination of the forearm and transmits forces between paired bones.
    • Examples: distal tibiofibular joint; interosseous membranes of forearm and leg.

    Gomphoses ("peg-in-socket")

    • A cone-shaped peg (tooth root) embedded in a bony socket (alveolus), bound by the periodontal ligament.
    • Functional: synarthrosis.
    • Periodontal ligament permits slight tooth movement under load (chewing, orthodontics) and houses mechanoreceptors that give us bite awareness.
    • Only example in the body: tooth in alveolar socket.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Periodontitis: bacterial inflammation of the gomphosis structures → periodontal ligament destruction → tooth loosening and eventual loss. High ankle sprain (syndesmotic injury): tearing of the distal tibiofibular ligaments — slower to heal than a typical lateral ankle sprain, often requires immobilization or surgery. Premature suture fusion (craniosynostosis): skull deformity in infancy because brain growth can't push the bones apart; needs surgical release.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Name the only example of a gomphosis in the human body.
    2. DOK 2. Why does a "high ankle sprain" take longer to heal than a typical inversion sprain?
    Show answers
    1. Tooth held in alveolar socket by the periodontal ligament.
    2. It tears the syndesmosis (distal tibiofibular ligaments and/or interosseous membrane). These tissues are load-bearing at every step and have limited blood supply, so they heal slowly and often require surgical fixation.

    Articulations · Concept 3 of 10

    Cartilaginous joints: synchondroses & symphyses A

    Bones joined directly by cartilage. The type of cartilage decides which subtype and how much it moves. Hyaline cartilage = synchondrosis (rigid). Fibrocartilage = symphysis (compressible).

    Synchondroses (hyaline cartilage)

    • Hyaline cartilage rigidly connects the bones. No joint cavity.
    • Functional: synarthrosis.
    • Many are temporary: the epiphyseal plate connects the diaphysis and epiphysis during growth, then ossifies into a synostosis at skeletal maturity (epiphyseal line).
    • Other examples: first sternocostal joint (rib 1 to sternum) is a permanent synchondrosis; this is why rib 1 doesn't move with breathing the way ribs 2–7 do at their synovial sternocostal joints.

    Symphyses (fibrocartilage)

    • A pad of fibrocartilage sits between the bones; thin hyaline plates cover each bony surface.
    • Fibrocartilage absorbs compression and tolerates shear — gives slight movement.
    • Functional: amphiarthrosis.
    • Examples: pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs (between vertebral bodies, with nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus), manubriosternal joint (often calcifies in adults).
    Mnemonic: “Synchondrosis = hyaline; Symphysis = fibrocartilage”. Synchondroses are rigid; symphyses give slightly.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Salter-Harris fractures: pediatric fractures involving the epiphyseal plate (synchondrosis). Damage can cause premature closure and growth arrest; orthopedic follow-up is essential. Intervertebral disc herniation: nucleus pulposus extrudes through a tear in the annulus fibrosus, compressing a spinal nerve. Most common at L4–L5 and L5–S1. Pubic symphysis diastasis: pregnancy hormones (relaxin) soften the fibrocartilage to allow birth; excessive widening causes pelvic girdle pain.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What kind of cartilage is in a symphysis?
    2. DOK 2. The epiphyseal plate is what kind of joint while it's still open? What does it become when growth is complete?
    Show answers
    1. Fibrocartilage.
    2. A synchondrosis (hyaline cartilage, synarthrosis). When ossified at the end of growth it becomes a synostosis (bone-to-bone fusion) marked by the epiphyseal line.

    Articulations · Concept 4 of 10

    Synovial joint anatomy A

    All synovial joints share the same toolkit: a fluid-filled cavity, slick cartilage on the bone ends, a fibrous capsule lined with synovial membrane, and reinforcing ligaments. Accessory structures (menisci, bursae, fat pads, labra) tune each joint for its specific demands.

    The six required parts

    1. Articular (joint) cavity: a small space separating the bones; contains synovial fluid.
    2. Articular cartilage: hyaline cartilage on the bone ends. Smooth, slick, avascular. Reduces friction, absorbs shock. Damage doesn't heal well — the basis of osteoarthritis.
    3. Articular capsule: two layers:
      • Fibrous capsule (outer): dense irregular connective tissue; continuous with periosteum. Strong, restrains motion.
      • Synovial membrane (inner): loose connective tissue with specialized synoviocytes that produce synovial fluid.
    4. Synovial fluid: viscous, egg-white-like (Greek synovia). Lubricates, distributes load, and nourishes the avascular articular cartilage. Contains hyaluronic acid + lubricin.
    5. Reinforcing ligaments:
      • Capsular: thickening of the fibrous capsule itself.
      • Extracapsular: outside the capsule (e.g., MCL, LCL of knee).
      • Intracapsular: inside the capsule but outside the synovial cavity (e.g., ACL, PCL of knee).
    6. Nerves & blood vessels: innervate the capsule and ligaments (proprioception, pain). Cartilage itself is avascular and aneural.

    Accessory structures (joint-specific)

    • Menisci (articular discs): wedges of fibrocartilage that deepen the joint and improve load distribution (knee, TMJ, sternoclavicular, distal radioulnar).
    • Labra: fibrocartilaginous rims that deepen a shallow socket (glenoid labrum of shoulder, acetabular labrum of hip).
    • Bursae: flat sacs of synovial fluid where tendons cross bones or ligaments. Reduce friction (e.g., subacromial bursa of shoulder, prepatellar bursa of knee).
    • Tendon sheaths: elongated bursae wrapping a tendon as it passes through a tight space (e.g., long head of biceps; flexor tendons of the hand).
    • Fat pads: cushions inside the joint (infrapatellar fat pad).
    Why this matters: when a joint is "swollen," the synovial membrane is overproducing fluid in response to injury or inflammation. Arthrocentesis (joint tap) samples the fluid: clear & viscous = normal; cloudy with high WBC = infection (septic arthritis); bloody = hemarthrosis (trauma, ACL tear, bleeding disorder); crystals = gout (negatively birefringent needles) or pseudogout (positively birefringent rhomboids).

    Articulations · Concept 5 of 10

    Six types of synovial joints A

    All synovial joints are diarthroses, but they're not equally free. Joint shape determines the number of axes of motion: nonaxial → uniaxial → biaxial → multiaxial. More axes = more freedom = less stability.

    The six types

    TypeAxesExamplePermitted movements
    Plane (gliding)NonaxialIntercarpal, intertarsal, AC, facet (zygapophyseal) jointsShort gliding only; no rotation around an axis
    HingeUniaxialElbow (humeroulnar), knee, IP joints, ankle (talocrural)Flexion / extension only (around one axis)
    PivotUniaxialAtlantoaxial (dens of C2 within C1 ring), proximal & distal radioulnarRotation around the long axis only
    Condylar (ellipsoid)BiaxialRadiocarpal (wrist), MCP joints (knuckles), atlanto-occipitalFlex/extend AND abduct/adduct
    SaddleBiaxial1st CMC of thumb, sternoclavicularFlex/extend AND abduct/adduct; thumb saddle also allows opposition
    Ball-and-socketMultiaxialGlenohumeral (shoulder), hip (acetabulofemoral)Flex/extend, abduct/adduct, rotation, circumduction
    Stability vs mobility: as axes increase, motion increases and stability decreases. Plane joints (nonaxial) are stable; ball-and-sockets (multiaxial) sacrifice stability for range. The hip cheats this rule by having a deep socket. The shoulder doesn't — that's why it's the most-dislocated joint in the body.

    Articulations · Concept 6 of 10

    Movements at synovial joints A

    Four categories of movement: gliding, angular, rotation, and special. Master the vocabulary and you can describe any motion at any joint precisely.

    1. Gliding

    Bones slide past each other in linear or curved paths. No rotation around an axis. Limited range. Examples: plane joints (intercarpal, intertarsal, facet joints).

    2. Angular movements (change the angle between two bones)

    • Flexion / extension: sagittal plane, around a frontal axis. Flexion decreases the angle (elbow bends), extension increases it.
    • Hyperextension: extension beyond anatomical position (head tilted back, wrist bent dorsally past straight).
    • Abduction / adduction: frontal plane, around a sagittal axis. AB-duct = away from midline (arm out to side). AD-duct = toward midline.
    • Circumduction: the distal end of the bone traces a cone; the proximal end stays put. Combination of flex + abd + ext + add. Possible at biaxial and multiaxial joints (shoulder, hip).

    3. Rotation (around the long axis of a bone)

    • Medial (internal) rotation: anterior surface of bone turns toward midline.
    • Lateral (external) rotation: turns away from midline.
    • Possible at pivot, condylar, and ball-and-socket joints. Rotation of the head happens primarily at the atlantoaxial pivot (C1–C2).

    4. Special movements (named for unique joints)

    • Pronation / supination: forearm rotation. Supination = palm forward / up (anatomical position; "holding a bowl of soup"). Pronation = palm backward / down.
    • Dorsiflexion / plantarflexion: ankle. Dorsiflexion = toes up. Plantarflexion = toes down (standing on tiptoes).
    • Inversion / eversion: foot. Inversion = sole turns inward. Eversion = sole turns outward.
    • Protraction / retraction: anterior & posterior gliding. Mandible (jaw forward / back), scapulae (shoulders forward / back).
    • Elevation / depression: superior / inferior. Shoulders shrugged (elevation) vs. dropped (depression). Mandible closing / opening.
    • Opposition / reposition: only at the 1st CMC of the thumb. Opposition: thumb pad to fingertip.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Goniometry: measuring joint range of motion with a goniometer. Normal values exist for each joint movement; deficits trend toward stiffness or laxity. Active ROM tests muscle + joint; passive ROM tests joint alone (examiner moves the limb). When passive ROM exceeds active ROM, suspect muscle/tendon weakness or pain inhibition rather than joint contracture.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which joint and movement get tested when you ask a patient to do "thumbs up"?
    2. DOK 2. What movement combines flexion, abduction, extension, and adduction in sequence?
    Show answers
    1. Extension at the carpometacarpal and metacarpophalangeal joints of the thumb (with the wrist held neutral).
    2. Circumduction.

    Articulations · Concept 7 of 10

    Knee joint: structure, ligaments, menisci A

    The largest joint in the body and the most clinically tested. The knee is a modified hinge that combines two functional joints in one capsule (tibiofemoral + patellofemoral), depends on muscles for stability, and is held together by four critical ligaments plus two crescent-shaped fibrocartilage menisci.

    The two joints in one capsule

    • Tibiofemoral joint: femoral condyles roll/glide on the tibial plateau. Modified hinge: flexion / extension + small "screw-home" rotation at terminal extension.
    • Patellofemoral joint: patella tracks in the trochlear groove of the femur. Plane / sellar joint. Patella acts as a lever to increase quadriceps mechanical advantage.

    The four major ligaments

    LigamentTypeFunctionTest
    ACL (anterior cruciate)Intracapsular, intra-synovial; runs from anterior tibia to posterolateral femoral condylePrevents anterior translation of tibia on femur and hyperextensionAnterior drawer; Lachman test (most sensitive)
    PCL (posterior cruciate)Intracapsular; from posterior tibia to anteromedial femoral condylePrevents posterior translation of tibia on femurPosterior drawer; "sag sign"
    MCL (medial collateral / tibial collateral)Extracapsular, broad; from medial femoral epicondyle to medial tibia. Attached to the medial meniscus.Resists valgus stress (lateral blow that pushes knee inward)Valgus stress test
    LCL (lateral collateral / fibular collateral)Extracapsular, cord-like; from lateral femoral epicondyle to head of fibulaResists varus stress (medial blow that pushes knee outward)Varus stress test

    The menisci

    • Medial meniscus: C-shaped (open larger); firmly attached to the medial tibial plateau AND to the MCL — less mobile, more vulnerable to tears. Bears most weight on the medial side.
    • Lateral meniscus: nearly circular (O-shaped); not attached to the LCL, more mobile, less injury-prone.
    • Function: deepen the tibial articular surface, improve congruence with the femoral condyles, distribute load, absorb shock.
    • Blood supply: only outer ~1/3 ("red zone") receives blood. Tears in the inner "white zone" don't heal and often need trimming (partial meniscectomy).

    Other knee features

    • Bursae: prepatellar (in front of patella; bursitis = "housemaid's knee"), infrapatellar, suprapatellar, semimembranosus (a tear here forms a Baker's cyst in the popliteal fossa).
    • Locking mechanism (screw-home): at terminal extension the femur medially rotates on a fixed tibia (or tibia laterally rotates on a fixed femur), locking the joint for stable weight-bearing. The popliteus muscle "unlocks" it to initiate flexion.
    • Iliotibial (IT) band: thickened fascia along the lateral thigh; helps stabilize the lateral knee. IT band friction syndrome is common in runners.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    ACL tear: classically a non-contact pivoting injury with planted foot (skiing, soccer, basketball). Sudden pop, immediate hemarthrosis (bloody effusion within hours), instability. Positive Lachman & anterior drawer. Often needs surgical reconstruction in active patients. "Unhappy triad": lateral blow to a planted knee → valgus force → tears MCL + ACL + medial meniscus. PCL tear: "dashboard injury" — posterior tibial force in MVA. Meniscus tear: twisting injury, locking or catching, joint-line tenderness, positive McMurray test. Patellar tendon rupture vs quadriceps tendon rupture: patellar tendon rupture in younger patients (jumping athletes); quad tendon rupture more common in older patients.

    Articulations · Concept 8 of 10

    Shoulder & hip: stability vs mobility trade-off A

    Both are ball-and-socket joints. They solve the stability/mobility trade-off in opposite directions: the shoulder bets on motion (and dislocates), the hip bets on stability (and rarely dislocates without major trauma).

    Glenohumeral (shoulder) joint

    • Geometry: shallow glenoid fossa receives a large humeral head — "golf ball on a tee." Maximum mobility, minimum bony stability.
    • Glenoid labrum: fibrocartilage rim deepens the socket ~50% and resists translation.
    • Capsule and ligaments: loose inferior capsule (allows full abduction); reinforced by glenohumeral ligaments (superior, middle, inferior) and the coracohumeral ligament.
    • Rotator cuff (SITS): primary active stabilizer. Pulls humeral head into the glenoid during arm motion.
    • Bursae: subacromial-subdeltoid bursa (key in impingement); subscapular bursa.

    Common injuries: anterior dislocation (most common, axillary nerve at risk), Bankart lesion (anterior labral tear), Hill-Sachs lesion (humeral head impaction), SLAP tears (superior labrum anterior-to-posterior), rotator cuff tears (supraspinatus most often), adhesive capsulitis ("frozen shoulder").

    Hip (acetabulofemoral) joint

    • Geometry: deep acetabulum holds a smaller femoral head — "deep cup." Less motion, far more bony stability than the shoulder.
    • Acetabular labrum: fibrocartilage rim deepens the socket further and creates a partial seal.
    • Capsular ligaments (three big ones):
      • Iliofemoral ligament (Y-ligament of Bigelow): anterior, strongest ligament in the body. Resists hyperextension — lets us stand upright with minimal muscle effort.
      • Pubofemoral ligament: anteroinferior, resists abduction.
      • Ischiofemoral ligament: posterior, resists internal rotation.
    • Ligamentum teres (ligament of the head of femur): intracapsular ligament running from acetabular notch to the fovea on the femoral head. Carries a small artery to the femoral head in childhood; minor mechanical role.
    • Blood supply: medial & lateral circumflex femoral arteries (branches of profunda femoris) wrap the femoral neck. Intracapsular femoral neck fracture disrupts these vessels → risk of avascular necrosis of the femoral head.

    Common injuries: femoral neck fracture (shortened, externally rotated leg), posterior hip dislocation (often from MVA with knee against dashboard — sciatic nerve at risk), labral tears (often from femoroacetabular impingement), hip osteoarthritis (one of the most common reasons for joint replacement).

    Compare: shoulder = shallow socket + loose capsule + small labrum → huge ROM, frequent dislocation. Hip = deep socket + large labrum + thick ligaments (iliofemoral is the body's strongest) → restricted ROM, rare dislocation outside of major trauma.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Anterior shoulder dislocation: humeral head pushed anteroinferior; classic Bankart + Hill-Sachs lesions; axillary nerve sensation (lateral deltoid) tested before and after reduction. Posterior hip dislocation: leg appears shortened, adducted, and internally rotated. Check sciatic nerve function (foot drop, hamstring weakness). Femoral neck fracture in elderly: leg appears shortened and externally rotated. Surgical fixation (or hip replacement if AVN risk is high).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What is the strongest ligament in the human body, and what does it resist?
    2. DOK 2. Why does an intracapsular femoral neck fracture carry a high risk of avascular necrosis?
    3. DOK 2. Why is the shoulder more prone to dislocation than the hip?
    Show answers
    1. The iliofemoral ligament (Y-ligament of Bigelow). It resists hip hyperextension and supports upright standing.
    2. The medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries wrap the femoral neck; an intracapsular fracture tears them. The femoral head loses its main blood supply (the ligamentum teres artery contributes very little in adulthood).
    3. Shallow glenoid + loose capsule + small labrum → the shoulder traded stability for motion. The hip has a deep socket and thick capsular ligaments and trades motion for stability.

    Articulations · Concept 9 of 10

    Selected synovial joints: TMJ, elbow, wrist, ankle A

    Quick anatomy of the other clinically important synovial joints. Each illustrates the same architecture (capsule, cartilage, ligaments) tuned for a different job.

    Temporomandibular joint (TMJ)

    • Condylar joint with a complete articular disc dividing the joint into two compartments.
    • Upper compartment: gliding (protraction, retraction, lateral excursion). Lower compartment: hinge (elevation, depression).
    • Lateral, sphenomandibular, and stylomandibular ligaments stabilize.
    • Innervation: auriculotemporal branch of CN V3.

    Elbow complex

    • Three joints share one capsule:
      • Humeroulnar: hinge. Carries most flex/extend load.
      • Humeroradial: condylar; allows radius to rotate AND flex/extend.
      • Proximal radioulnar: pivot; radial head spins in the annular ligament for pronation/supination.
    • Collateral ligaments: medial (ulnar) collateral ligament resists valgus; lateral (radial) collateral resists varus.
    • Annular ligament: encircles radial head; "nursemaid's elbow" = radial head subluxation when a child's arm is yanked (the annular ligament slips off the radial head).

    Wrist (radiocarpal) and intercarpal joints

    • Radiocarpal joint: condylar between distal radius and the proximal row of carpals (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum). Flex/extend + radial/ulnar deviation.
    • The ulna does not directly articulate with the carpals — an articular disc of the distal radioulnar joint intervenes.
    • Intercarpal joints: plane; allow gliding between carpal rows.
    • Scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal (fall on outstretched hand → snuffbox tenderness). Blood supply enters distally, so proximal pole fractures risk avascular necrosis.

    Ankle (talocrural) and subtalar joints

    • Talocrural: hinge between distal tibia/fibula "mortise" and the talus. Dorsiflexion/plantarflexion only.
    • Subtalar: plane between talus and calcaneus. Inversion/eversion.
    • Lateral ligament complex (the one that gets sprained):
      • Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL): weakest, most commonly torn.
      • Calcaneofibular (CFL): second most often torn.
      • Posterior talofibular (PTFL): rarely torn.
    • Medial (deltoid) ligament: thick, fan-shaped, strong. Inversion sprains are far more common than eversion (deltoid ligament is harder to tear).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    TMJ disorder: clicking, locking, pain with chewing; often related to disc displacement or bruxism. Nursemaid's elbow: radial head subluxation in toddlers; reduce by supinating + flexing the elbow. Lateral ankle sprain (inversion injury): ATFL torn first; grade I — III based on severity. High ankle sprain: syndesmotic injury (tibiofibular ligaments); longer recovery.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Name the most commonly torn ligament in a typical (inversion) ankle sprain.
    2. DOK 2. A child is in tears after being lifted by one arm; the elbow is held slightly flexed and pronated, and the child won't move it. What is the diagnosis and reduction maneuver?
    Show answers
    1. Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL).
    2. Nursemaid's elbow (radial head subluxation). Reduce by supinating the forearm and flexing the elbow, often feeling a click as the annular ligament slips back over the radial head.

    Articulations · Concept 10 of 10

    Joint disorders: sprains, dislocations, arthritis CLIN

    A short clinical taxonomy of the conditions that affect joints. Distinguish sprain (ligament) from strain (muscle/tendon), dislocation from subluxation, and the major arthropathies (OA vs RA vs gout vs septic).

    Sprain vs strain vs contusion

    • Sprain: ligament injury. Graded I (stretch), II (partial tear), III (complete tear).
    • Strain: muscle or tendon injury.
    • Contusion: blunt soft-tissue injury (bruise).
    • Initial management for all three: RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation), often plus NSAIDs and progressive rehab.

    Dislocation and subluxation

    • Dislocation (luxation): complete loss of contact between articular surfaces. Often deformity, severe pain, loss of motion. Reduce promptly (after assessing neurovascular status).
    • Subluxation: partial dislocation; articular surfaces still partly in contact.
    • Repeated dislocations → capsular and labral laxity → chronic instability that may need surgical repair.

    Major arthropathies

    DiseaseMechanismPatternKey features
    Osteoarthritis (OA)Mechanical wear of articular cartilage → bone exposure, osteophytesAsymmetric, weight-bearing joints (knees, hips, lumbar spine), DIPs (Heberden), PIPs (Bouchard)Pain worse with use, morning stiffness < 30 min, no systemic features
    Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)Autoimmune synovitis (anti-CCP, RF); pannus erodes cartilage and boneSymmetric, small joints (MCP, PIP — spares DIP), morning stiffness > 1 hourUlnar deviation, swan neck, boutonnière deformities; systemic illness
    GoutMonosodium urate crystals in joint → intense inflammationAcute, monoarticular; classically first MTP ("podagra"); also midfoot, ankle, kneeNegatively birefringent needle-shaped crystals; treat acute with NSAID/colchicine/steroid; long-term urate lowering (allopurinol)
    Pseudogout (CPPD)Calcium pyrophosphate crystalsKnees and wrists more typical than goutPositively birefringent rhomboids; chondrocalcinosis on X-ray
    Septic arthritisBacterial infection (often S. aureus); hematogenous or direct inoculationAcute monoarticular; hot, swollen, fever; refuses to bear weightArthrocentesis WBC often > 50,000 (frequently > 100,000); empiric antibiotics + surgical washout; cartilage destroyed in hours; mortality 10–15%

    Bursitis & tendinopathy

    • Bursitis: inflammation of a bursa (prepatellar = "housemaid's knee"; subacromial = shoulder impingement; trochanteric = lateral hip pain).
    • Tendinopathy: overuse-related degeneration of a tendon (lateral epicondylitis = "tennis elbow"; rotator cuff tendinopathy).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Always assess neurovascular status before and after reducing a dislocation. Always consider septic arthritis in any acutely hot, swollen joint with fever or systemic symptoms; delay can destroy a joint in hours. Distinguish OA from RA in five questions: which joints? symmetric? morning stiffness duration? worse with use or rest? any systemic illness?

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Define sprain vs strain.
    2. DOK 2. A patient has a hot, swollen, painful first MTP. Arthrocentesis shows negatively birefringent needle-shaped crystals. Diagnosis and acute treatment?
    3. DOK 2. Which arthritis spares the DIP joints?
    Show answers
    1. Sprain = ligament injury. Strain = muscle or tendon injury.
    2. Acute gout. Acute treatment: NSAID, colchicine, or corticosteroid (oral or intra-articular). Long-term urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol) after the acute attack settles.
    3. Rheumatoid arthritis (it favors MCP and PIP joints; OA classically involves DIP).
