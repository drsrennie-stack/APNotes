    Nervous · Concept 1 of 16

    Nervous system organization A

    The nervous system splits in two ways: anatomically (CNS vs PNS) and functionally (somatic vs autonomic). Knowing which compartment a problem lives in is half of the diagnosis.

    Notes

    • Anatomic divisions:
      • CNS (central): brain + spinal cord. Wrapped in meninges, bathed in CSF.
      • PNS (peripheral): cranial nerves (12 pairs) + spinal nerves (31 pairs) + ganglia.
    • Functional divisions (within the PNS):
      • Somatic: voluntary motor to skeletal muscle + sensory from skin/joints.
      • Autonomic (ANS): involuntary motor to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands. Two branches: sympathetic and parasympathetic.
      • Enteric (often called a "third division"): GI tract intrinsic neurons. Can function autonomously.
    • Signal types:
      • Afferent (sensory): toward CNS.
      • Efferent (motor): away from CNS.
    • Three core functions:
      • Sensory input → integration → motor output.
      • The brain layers higher functions (cognition, emotion, memory) on top of this loop.

    Summary

    DivisionDirectionEffectorConscious?
    Somatic motorEfferentSkeletal muscleVoluntary
    Somatic sensoryAfferentSkin, joints, special sensesConscious
    Autonomic motorEfferentSmooth muscle, cardiac, glandsInvoluntary
    Visceral sensoryAfferentFrom visceraMostly unconscious

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Localizing a lesion starts with: is this CNS or PNS? UMN (upper motor neuron, CNS) lesions show spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign. LMN (lower motor neuron, PNS) lesions show flaccid weakness, hyporeflexia, fasciculations, atrophy. ALS is rare in that both UMN and LMN are involved.

    Nervous · Concept 2 of 16

    Neurons and glial cells A

    Neurons signal. Glial cells support, insulate, defend, and recycle. Both matter — most CNS tumors are glial, not neuronal.

    Notes

    • Neuron parts:
      • Soma (cell body): contains nucleus, organelles.
      • Dendrites: receive input. Many per neuron.
      • Axon hillock: integration zone; where the action potential is initiated.
      • Axon: conducts AP. One per neuron, can branch at ends (telodendria).
      • Axon terminal (synaptic bouton): releases neurotransmitter.
    • Neuron functional types:
      • Sensory (afferent): toward CNS.
      • Motor (efferent): away from CNS.
      • Interneuron: between neurons in CNS. Most neurons in the body are interneurons.
    • Glial cells (CNS, 4 types):
      • Astrocyte: most abundant. Blood-brain barrier, ion buffering, structural support, glutamate cycling.
      • Oligodendrocyte: myelinates CNS axons (one cell wraps multiple axons).
      • Microglia: resident immune cells. Phagocytose debris, present antigen.
      • Ependymal cells: line ventricles, make CSF (choroid plexus).
    • Glial cells (PNS, 2 types):
      • Schwann cell: myelinates PNS axons (one cell, one segment of one axon).
      • Satellite cell: surrounds neuron cell bodies in ganglia.
    • Myelin: insulating wrapping. Speeds AP via saltatory conduction (jumps between nodes of Ranvier). Loss of myelin (MS, Guillain-Barré) slows or blocks conduction.

    Summary

    CellWhereMain job
    AstrocyteCNSBBB, ion balance, support
    OligodendrocyteCNSMyelin (multi-axon)
    MicrogliaCNSImmune defense
    EpendymalCNS ventriclesCSF production + flow
    SchwannPNSMyelin (one segment)
    SatellitePNS gangliaNeuron cell-body support

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Most primary CNS tumors are glial (gliomas — glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma). Neurons rarely divide in the adult brain; gliomas come from cells that still divide. MS: autoimmune attack on CNS myelin (oligodendrocytes). Guillain-Barré: autoimmune attack on PNS myelin (Schwann cells) after infection. Both cause weakness and sensory loss but at different anatomic locations.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which glial cell forms the blood-brain barrier?
    2. DOK 3. Why does demyelination slow or stop nerve conduction?
    Show answers
    1. Astrocytes (their end-feet wrap brain capillaries).
    2. Saltatory conduction depends on AP jumping between nodes of Ranvier — the myelin insulates the in-between segments. Loss of myelin → AP must propagate continuously like in unmyelinated fibers (much slower), or fails altogether if Na⁺ channels are not present in the formerly myelinated segments.

    Nervous · Concept 3 of 16

    Resting + action potentials P

    A resting neuron is polarized at ≈ −70 mV because of selective permeability and the Na/K ATPase. An action potential is a fast, all-or-none reversal of that polarity that propagates down the axon.

    Notes

    • Resting membrane potential: ~ −70 mV inside (relative to outside).
      • Na/K ATPase: pumps 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in. Maintains gradients.
      • Resting membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺ (K⁺ leak channels). K⁺ exits → inside becomes negative.
      • Nernst / Goldman equations describe the equilibrium quantitatively.
    • Action potential phases:
      • Threshold (~ −55 mV): if depolarization reaches it, AP fires.
      • Depolarization: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ rushes in → membrane swings to ~ +30 mV.
      • Repolarization: Na⁺ channels inactivate, voltage-gated K⁺ channels open → K⁺ exits.
      • Hyperpolarization: K⁺ channels close slowly → brief overshoot below −70 mV.
      • Return to rest: Na/K ATPase restores ion gradients gradually.
    • All-or-none: once threshold is reached, AP magnitude is fixed. Stronger stimuli → more frequent APs, not bigger ones.
    • Refractory periods:
      • Absolute: Na⁺ channels inactivated; cannot fire again. Sets max firing rate.
      • Relative: K⁺ still leaving; need stronger stimulus.
    • Conduction:
      • Unmyelinated: continuous propagation. Slow (~1 m/s).
      • Myelinated: saltatory. Jumps between nodes of Ranvier. Fast (up to ~120 m/s).
      • Speed also depends on axon diameter (larger = faster).
    • Local anesthetics (lidocaine) block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → no AP → no signal. Tetrodotoxin (pufferfish) does the same with extreme potency.

    Diagram: action potential phases

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Hyperkalemia shifts resting membrane potential toward threshold initially (cells more excitable, peaked T waves on ECG), but at high levels inactivates Na⁺ channels → conduction failure (wide QRS, asystole). Hypocalcemia lowers threshold → easier firing → tetany and seizures. Demyelinating disease slows or stops saltatory conduction → weakness, sensory deficits.

    Reproduce the AP curve. Label resting, threshold, depolarization, repolarization, hyperpolarization. Mark which ion channel state corresponds to each phase. Add the refractory periods.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which ion drives depolarization in a neuron?
    2. DOK 3. Why does myelination dramatically increase conduction speed?
    Show answers
    1. Na⁺ (sodium) influx through voltage-gated channels.
    2. Myelin insulates internodal segments, so the AP only "regenerates" at nodes of Ranvier where Na⁺ channels are clustered. The AP effectively jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction) instead of propagating continuously, multiplying speed without requiring a larger axon.

    Nervous · Concept 4 of 16

    Synaptic transmission and neurotransmitters P

    The action potential ends at the synapse. Calcium drives neurotransmitter release. The neurotransmitter determines whether the postsynaptic cell fires next or stays quiet.

    Notes

    • Chemical synapse steps:
      1. AP arrives at axon terminal.
      2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ enters terminal.
      3. Ca²⁺ triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with presynaptic membrane.
      4. Neurotransmitter (NT) released into synaptic cleft.
      5. NT binds postsynaptic receptors.
      6. Postsynaptic ion channels open → EPSP (excitatory) or IPSP (inhibitory).
      7. NT cleared by reuptake, enzymatic degradation, or diffusion.
    • Summation (how single synapses combine):
      • Temporal: rapid repeated input from one synapse.
      • Spatial: simultaneous input from multiple synapses.
      • If EPSPs raise membrane to threshold at the axon hillock → AP fires.
    • Major neurotransmitters:
      • Glutamate: main excitatory CNS NT. Implicated in stroke, seizures.
      • GABA: main inhibitory CNS NT. Target of benzos, alcohol, barbiturates.
      • Glycine: inhibitory in spinal cord and brainstem.
      • Acetylcholine (ACh): NMJ, all autonomic ganglia, parasympathetic postganglionic, CNS cognition. Lost in Alzheimer.
      • Norepinephrine (NE): sympathetic postganglionic, locus coeruleus. Mood, arousal.
      • Dopamine (DA): reward, motor (substantia nigra → striatum, lost in Parkinson). Schizophrenia involves dysregulation.
      • Serotonin (5-HT): mood, sleep, appetite. SSRI target.
      • Histamine: wakefulness. H1 antagonists cause sedation.
      • Endorphins / enkephalins: endogenous opioids. Pain modulation.
    • Electrical synapses (gap junctions): direct cytoplasmic continuity, bidirectional, very fast. Found in cardiac muscle and some CNS regions.

    Summary

    NTEffectWhereClinical link
    GlutamateExcitatoryCNSExcitotoxicity in stroke
    GABAInhibitoryCNSBenzos, alcohol, seizures
    AChVariableNMJ, ANS, CNSAlzheimer, myasthenia, organophosphates
    NEVariableSympathetic, CNSDepression, panic
    DopamineVariableBasal ganglia, mesolimbicParkinson, schizophrenia, addiction
    SerotoninVariableRaphe nuclei → CNSDepression, anxiety, GI motility

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Many psychiatric meds target NT systems: SSRIs (block 5-HT reuptake), SNRIs (5-HT + NE), benzos (potentiate GABA), antipsychotics (D2 antagonists). Drugs of abuse all funnel through the mesolimbic dopamine reward pathway. Serotonin syndrome (too much serotonergic activity, e.g., SSRI + MAOI + tramadol): hyperthermia, agitation, clonus, autonomic instability. Medical emergency.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the synapse with the seven steps numbered. Show one excitatory and one inhibitory postsynaptic potential summing at the axon hillock with threshold marked.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which ion triggers neurotransmitter release at the axon terminal?
    2. DOK 3. Why does an SSRI take weeks to work despite blocking reuptake immediately?
    Show answers
    1. Ca²⁺ (calcium).
    2. Immediate effect is more synaptic serotonin, but receptor adaptation (down-regulation of presynaptic auto-receptors, gene expression changes, neuroplasticity) takes 2-6 weeks to translate into clinical mood improvement.

    Nervous · Concept 5 of 16

    Reflexes P

    A reflex is the simplest unit of neural behavior: a hard-wired sensor-to-effector loop that runs without conscious control. Knowing the loop tells you what each clinical reflex actually tests.

    Notes

    • Five components of a reflex arc:
      1. Receptor (e.g., muscle spindle).
      2. Afferent (sensory) neuron.
      3. Integration center (CNS — often just one synapse in cord, or interneuron).
      4. Efferent (motor) neuron.
      5. Effector (muscle or gland).
    • Monosynaptic reflex: one synapse between afferent and efferent. Stretch reflex (knee jerk). Tests integrity of L2-L4 (patellar).
    • Polysynaptic reflex: includes interneurons. Withdrawal reflex (pull hand from hot stove). Often with crossed extensor (opposite leg extends when one withdraws).
    • Reciprocal inhibition: when one muscle contracts, its antagonist is reflexively inhibited via interneuron.
    • Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) tested clinically:
      • Biceps: C5-C6.
      • Brachioradialis: C5-C6.
      • Triceps: C7-C8.
      • Patellar: L2-L4.
      • Achilles: S1-S2.
    • Babinski sign: stroking the lateral plantar surface → fanning of toes. Normal in infants (incomplete myelination). After ~2 yr: pathologic, suggests UMN lesion.
    • UMN vs LMN reflex pattern:
      • UMN: hyperreflexia, spasticity, positive Babinski.
      • LMN: hyporeflexia, flaccid weakness, fasciculations.

    Summary: DTR scale

    GradeFinding
    0Absent
    1+Diminished
    2+Normal
    3+Brisk (could be normal or early UMN)
    4+Hyperactive with clonus → UMN lesion

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Brain death exam includes loss of all brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, gag, vestibulo-ocular, cough). Spinal shock (acute spinal cord injury): areflexia below the lesion temporarily, then hyperreflexia as UMN pattern emerges weeks later.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the patellar reflex arc with all five components labeled. Then draw a withdrawal reflex (foot on tack) with the polysynaptic interneurons + the crossed extensor in the opposite leg.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which spinal levels does the patellar reflex test?
    2. DOK 3. Why does Babinski's sign appear in a UMN lesion?
    Show answers
    1. L2-L4.
    2. Normally, descending corticospinal input from the motor cortex suppresses the primitive plantar flexion reflex pattern → adult shows toe flexion (downgoing toes). When the UMN tract is damaged, that suppression is lost and the primitive pattern returns: toes fan upward (positive Babinski).

    Nervous · Concept 6 of 16

    Brain anatomy A

    The brain has four major regions: cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem, cerebellum. Each lobe of the cerebrum has signature functions and a signature deficit when injured.

    Notes

    • Cerebrum (left + right hemispheres, four lobes each):
      • Frontal: motor cortex (primary, premotor, supplementary), Broca's area (left, speech production), executive function, personality.
      • Parietal: somatosensory cortex, body schema, spatial orientation.
      • Temporal: auditory cortex, Wernicke's area (left, language comprehension), memory (hippocampus), olfactory cortex.
      • Occipital: visual cortex.
    • Deep cerebral structures:
      • Basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, plus substantia nigra and subthalamic nucleus): motor program selection, habit learning. Lost in Parkinson, Huntington.
      • Hippocampus: declarative memory consolidation. Damaged in Alzheimer.
      • Amygdala: emotional salience, fear.
      • Corpus callosum: connects hemispheres.
    • Diencephalon:
      • Thalamus: relay station for almost all sensory input to cortex.
      • Hypothalamus: homeostasis (temperature, thirst, hunger, circadian, ANS, neuroendocrine).
      • Epithalamus: pineal (melatonin).
    • Brainstem (caudal to rostral: medulla, pons, midbrain):
      • Medulla: cardiorespiratory centers, decussation of corticospinal pyramids.
      • Pons: relay between cerebrum and cerebellum, sleep.
      • Midbrain: substantia nigra, red nucleus, ocular motor nuclei.
      • Cranial nerves III-XII emerge here.
    • Cerebellum: motor coordination, balance, motor learning. Lesion → ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor.
    • Functional lateralization (in most right-handed people):
      • Left hemisphere: language, math, analytic.
      • Right hemisphere: spatial, music, holistic.
      • Each hemisphere controls the contralateral side of the body.

    Summary: lobe deficits

    LobeFunctionDeficit if injured
    FrontalMotor, executive, BrocaContralateral weakness, expressive aphasia, personality change
    ParietalSensory, spatialSensory loss, neglect (often R hemisphere)
    TemporalHearing, Wernicke, memoryHearing loss, receptive aphasia, memory deficit
    OccipitalVisionVisual field cuts, cortical blindness
    CerebellumCoordination, balanceAtaxia, intention tremor
    Basal gangliaMotor programmingMovement disorders (Parkinson, Huntington)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Stroke localization is mostly about MCA territory (most common stroke): contralateral motor and sensory deficits, often face and arm worse than leg, plus aphasia if dominant (usually left) hemisphere. ACA strokes hit leg more than face/arm. PCA strokes affect vision and memory.

    Sketch a lateral view of the brain. Label four lobes, Broca's and Wernicke's areas, primary motor cortex (homunculus orientation), primary sensory cortex, cerebellum, brainstem. Add the basal ganglia underneath as deep structures.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient cannot speak fluently but understands commands. Which area is likely damaged?
    2. DOK 3. Which structure relays nearly all sensory input to the cortex?
    Show answers
    1. Broca's area (left frontal lobe). Expressive (non-fluent) aphasia.
    2. Thalamus.

    Nervous · Concept 7 of 16

    Cranial nerves I-XII A&P

    Twelve pairs emerge directly from the brain. Each has a function (sensory, motor, or both) and a specific testable behavior at the bedside.

    Notes — the twelve, with bedside test

    • I — Olfactory: smell (sensory). Test: smell each nostril separately.
    • II — Optic: vision (sensory). Test: acuity, visual fields, pupillary light reflex.
    • III — Oculomotor: most extraocular muscles, eyelid (LPS), pupillary constriction (parasympathetic). Test: H-pattern eye movement, pupil response, ptosis check.
    • IV — Trochlear: superior oblique (eye looks down-and-in). Test: H-pattern; diplopia going down stairs is classic.
    • V — Trigeminal: facial sensation (3 branches: V1, V2, V3), muscles of mastication. Test: light touch + pinprick on three branches; clench jaw; corneal reflex (V afferent, VII efferent).
    • VI — Abducens: lateral rectus (eye abducts). Test: lateral gaze.
    • VII — Facial: facial expression muscles, taste anterior 2/3 tongue, lacrimation/salivation. Test: raise eyebrows, smile, close eyes against resistance. Bell's palsy = LMN VII (whole side); central lesion spares forehead.
    • VIII — Vestibulocochlear: hearing + balance. Test: Weber and Rinne, head impulse test.
    • IX — Glossopharyngeal: taste posterior 1/3 tongue, swallowing, gag reflex (afferent). Test: gag reflex.
    • X — Vagus: swallowing, voice, parasympathetic to thoracic and abdominal viscera. Test: uvula midline on "ahh," gag (efferent), voice quality.
    • XI — Accessory: trapezius, SCM. Test: shoulder shrug, head turn against resistance.
    • XII — Hypoglossal: tongue muscles. Test: stick out tongue; deviates toward side of LMN lesion.

    Summary mnemonics

    Mnemonic lineUse
    "On Old Olympus' Towering Tops A Finn And German Viewed Some Hops"Names of the 12 in order
    "Some Say Marry Money But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More"Sensory / motor / both: S S M M B M B S B B M M (for I-XII)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pupillary light reflex: afferent CN II, efferent CN III. An unreactive dilated pupil with new headache and altered consciousness = CN III compression from uncal herniation (medical emergency). Diplopia patterns: CN IV → down-and-in difficulty (going down stairs); CN VI → lateral gaze difficulty (cannot abduct affected eye). Bell's palsy is a peripheral CN VII lesion → entire ipsilateral facial weakness including forehead. Central VII lesion → forehead spared because forehead has bilateral cortical input.

    DRAW IT

    Build a 12-row table: nerve, function (S/M/B), bedside test, classic lesion finding. Use this as your bedside reference card.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which cranial nerve innervates the lateral rectus?
    2. DOK 4. A patient has new right-sided facial droop including the forehead. Central or peripheral lesion, and which nerve?
    Show answers
    1. CN VI (abducens).
    2. Peripheral (LMN) lesion of CN VII (Bell's palsy on the right). Central lesions spare the forehead because the forehead motor cortex has bilateral projections.

    Nervous · Concept 8 of 16

    Spinal cord, meninges, CSF, ventricles A

    The CNS lives inside protective layers, suspended in fluid. CSF cushions, removes waste, and circulates from ventricles through subarachnoid space.

    Notes

    • Spinal cord:
      • Extends foramen magnum → L1-L2 (ends as conus medullaris).
      • Below L2 = cauda equina (lumbar puncture safe here).
      • 31 spinal nerve pairs: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal.
      • Cross-section: gray matter (H-shaped, contains cell bodies) surrounded by white matter (tracts).
      • Anterior horn: LMN cell bodies. Posterior horn: sensory entry. Lateral horn (T1-L2): sympathetic preganglionic cell bodies.
    • Meninges (3 layers, outer to inner):
      • Dura mater: tough, fibrous. Two layers (periosteal, meningeal) in skull, one layer in spine.
      • Arachnoid mater: spider-web-like, no vessels.
      • Pia mater: delicate, hugs CNS surface, vascular.
    • Three spaces:
      • Epidural: outside dura (in spine, contains fat and venous plexus; in skull, only with pathology — epidural hematoma from MMA tear).
      • Subdural: between dura and arachnoid (bridging veins → subdural hematoma).
      • Subarachnoid: between arachnoid and pia. Contains CSF and major vessels (ruptured aneurysm → SAH).
    • Ventricular system + CSF flow:
      1. Lateral ventricles (2) → interventricular foramen of Monro →
      2. 3rd ventricle → cerebral aqueduct →
      3. 4th ventricle → exits via foramina of Luschka/Magendie →
      4. Subarachnoid space → reabsorbed at arachnoid granulations into dural venous sinuses.
    • CSF: ~150 mL total. Produced by choroid plexus (ependymal cells) at ~500 mL/day. Clear, low protein, normal glucose ~⅔ serum. Functions: buoyancy, protection, waste removal.

    Summary

    HemorrhageLocationSourceCT appearance
    EpiduralBetween skull and duraMiddle meningeal artery (often after temporal bone fx)Lens-shaped, doesn't cross sutures
    SubduralBetween dura and arachnoidBridging veins (elderly, alcoholics, infants)Crescent-shaped, crosses sutures
    SubarachnoidBetween arachnoid and piaRuptured aneurysmBlood in sulci/basal cisterns
    IntracerebralWithin brain parenchymaHTN, AVM, tumorFocal hyperdensity

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Lumbar puncture at L3-L4 or L4-L5 — below conus, no cord to hit. Hydrocephalus: obstructed CSF flow (e.g., aqueductal stenosis) or impaired reabsorption → ↑ ventricular pressure → headache, vomiting, papilledema, AMS. Bacterial meningitis CSF: ↑ WBC (PMNs), ↑ protein, ↓ glucose. Viral: ↑ WBC (lymphocytes), normal glucose.

    DRAW IT

    Sketch the ventricles in sequence with arrows. Add the choroid plexus locations, the three meningeal layers, and label where each hemorrhage type sits.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Why is LP performed below L2?
    2. DOK 3. A "lucid interval" after head trauma followed by rapid decline is classic for what hemorrhage?
    Show answers
    1. The spinal cord ends around L1-L2. Below that is cauda equina (loose nerve roots that float aside). Safer to enter than risking the cord.
    2. Epidural hematoma — arterial bleed (middle meningeal artery) expands rapidly; "lucid interval" before mass effect overwhelms compensation.

    Nervous · Concept 9 of 16

    Sensory and motor pathways P

    Two big sensory tracts and two big motor tracts. Each crosses (decussates) at a specific level, so the deficit predicts the lesion side.

    Notes

    • Two main ascending sensory pathways:
      • Dorsal column-medial lemniscus (DCML): fine touch, vibration, proprioception.
        • First-order neuron enters dorsal column → ascends ipsilaterally to medulla → synapses → decussates → ascends medial lemniscus → thalamus → primary sensory cortex.
        • Decussation: medulla.
      • Spinothalamic tract: pain, temperature, crude touch.
        • First-order neuron enters → synapses in dorsal horn → second-order decussates at that level → ascends in lateral spinothalamic → thalamus → cortex.
        • Decussation: spinal cord (immediately at level of entry).
    • Two main descending motor pathways:
      • Corticospinal (pyramidal): voluntary movement, esp. distal limbs.
        • Cortex → internal capsule → midbrain → pons → ~85% decussate at pyramids of medulla → lateral corticospinal tract → synapse on LMN → muscle.
        • ~15% don't decussate (ventral corticospinal tract, decussate at level of action).
      • Extrapyramidal tracts (rubrospinal, reticulospinal, vestibulospinal, tectospinal): posture, balance, gross movement. Modulate the corticospinal output.
    • UMN vs LMN signs revisited:
      • UMN: spasticity, hyperreflexia, positive Babinski, no atrophy (until late).
      • LMN: flaccid weakness, hyporeflexia, fasciculations, atrophy.
    • Brown-Séquard syndrome (cord hemisection): ipsilateral loss of motor and DCML (vibration/proprioception) below lesion; contralateral loss of pain/temperature starting one or two levels below the lesion (because spinothalamic decussates at level of entry).

    Summary

    PathwayWhat it carriesDecussates at
    DCMLFine touch, vibration, proprioceptionMedulla
    SpinothalamicPain, temperature, crude touchSpinal cord at entry
    Corticospinal (lateral)Voluntary motorPyramids of medulla
    Corticospinal (ventral)Voluntary motor (axial)At level of action

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Stroke pattern reflects pathway decussation. A right cortical stroke (above medulla) produces left-sided weakness and left-sided sensory loss because the tracts have not yet crossed (motor) or have already crossed (sensory). Anterior cord syndrome (often from anterior spinal artery infarct): bilateral motor + spinothalamic loss; DCML preserved (different blood supply).

    Draw a spinal cord cross-section. Color the dorsal columns (DCML), the lateral spinothalamic tract, and the lateral corticospinal tract. Mark where each decussates.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Where does the spinothalamic tract decussate?
    2. DOK 4. A right T6 hemisection causes which deficits below the lesion?
    Show answers
    1. At the spinal cord level of entry (the second-order neuron crosses immediately).
    2. Brown-Séquard pattern: ipsilateral motor and vibration/proprioception loss (DCML and lateral corticospinal haven't decussated yet at T6), contralateral pain/temperature loss starting one or two levels below (spinothalamic already crossed at entry).

    Nervous · Concept 10 of 16

    Autonomic nervous system: sympathetic vs parasympathetic A&P

    Two opposing branches, each with a two-neuron path from CNS to effector. Sympathetic is fight-or-flight (thoracolumbar, short pre / long post). Parasympathetic is rest-and-digest (craniosacral, long pre / short post).

    Notes

    • Two-neuron pathway in both branches:
      1. Preganglionic neuron: cell body in CNS → axon to autonomic ganglion (releases ACh on nicotinic receptors).
      2. Postganglionic neuron: cell body in ganglion → axon to effector (releases NE in sympathetic, ACh in parasympathetic).
    • Sympathetic (thoracolumbar):
      • Preganglionic cell bodies in lateral horn T1-L2.
      • Short preganglionic axons → sympathetic chain ganglia (paravertebral) or prevertebral ganglia (celiac, SMG, IMG).
      • Long postganglionic axons → effectors throughout body.
      • Exception: adrenal medulla. Preganglionic fiber synapses directly on chromaffin cells (no postganglionic neuron) → systemic release of E and NE.
    • Parasympathetic (craniosacral):
      • Cranial outflow: CN III, VII, IX, X (vagus carries ~75% of all parasympathetic fibers).
      • Sacral outflow: S2-S4 (pelvic splanchnic nerves to pelvic organs).
      • Long preganglionic → ganglia located in or near target organ.
      • Short postganglionic → effector.
    • Dual innervation: most organs receive both branches with opposing effects. Notable exceptions:
      • Adrenal medulla: sympathetic only.
      • Sweat glands: sympathetic only (but uses ACh — unique).
      • Vascular smooth muscle: mostly sympathetic only.
      • Pilomotor muscles: sympathetic only.

Medical emergency.

    Nervous · Concept 11 of 16

    ANS receptor pharmacology primer P

    Every autonomic drug works on one of a small set of receptors. Memorize the receptor map once and the pharmacology of antihypertensives, asthma drugs, GI motility drugs, mydriatics, urinary drugs, and shock pressors becomes one consistent system.

    Notes — the receptor map

    • Cholinergic receptors (respond to ACh):
      • Nicotinic (N): at all autonomic ganglia (both sympathetic and parasympathetic) and at the neuromuscular junction. Ionotropic (fast).
      • Muscarinic (M1-M5): at parasympathetic targets (and the unique sweat gland sympathetic outflow). Metabotropic.
        • M1 — CNS, gastric parietal cells.
        • M2 — heart (↓ HR, ↓ AV conduction).
        • M3 — smooth muscle (bronchoconstriction, GI/bladder contraction, glands).
    • Adrenergic receptors (respond to NE / E):
      • α1: vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction; iris dilator → mydriasis; bladder neck → contraction (urinary retention).
      • α2: presynaptic on sympathetic terminals → negative feedback on NE release; CNS sympathetic dampening (clonidine).
      • β1: heart → ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, ↑ conduction; kidney JG cells → ↑ renin.
      • β2: bronchial smooth muscle → bronchodilation; vascular smooth muscle (skeletal muscle) → vasodilation; liver → glycogenolysis; uterus → relaxation.
      • β3: bladder detrusor → relaxation; adipose → lipolysis.

    ReceptorAgonist (mimic)Antagonist (block)Therapeutic use

    Putting it together — what does each drug do at the bedside?

    • Albuterol (β2 agonist): bronchodilation in asthma. Side effects: tremor, tachycardia (some β1 cross-reactivity).
    • Metoprolol (β1 antagonist): ↓ HR, ↓ contractility, ↓ renin. Used in HF, HTN, post-MI, AFib rate control.
    • Atropine (M antagonist): ↑ HR, ↓ secretions, mydriasis. Used in symptomatic bradycardia and as antidote to AChE inhibitor poisoning.
    • Phenylephrine (α1 agonist): vasoconstriction → ↑ BP. Used in distributive shock and as a nasal decongestant.
    • Tamsulosin (α1A antagonist): relaxes bladder neck → improves urine flow in BPH.
    • Clonidine (α2 central agonist): ↓ central sympathetic outflow → ↓ BP, sedation.
    • Pyridostigmine (AChE inhibitor): ↑ ACh at NMJ → improves myasthenia gravis weakness.
    • Succinylcholine (nicotinic NMJ agonist, depolarizing): rapid muscle paralysis for intubation; brief duration.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Organophosphate poisoning (pesticide exposure) = AChE inhibition → too much ACh everywhere. Symptoms = "SLUDGE-M": Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis, Miosis. Treat with atropine (block muscarinic) + pralidoxime (regenerate AChE).

    Anticholinergic toxicity ("hot, dry, blind, mad, full") — diphenhydramine, antipsychotics, TCAs, scopolamine: hyperthermia, dry skin, mydriasis, confusion, urinary retention. Treat with supportive care; physostigmine in severe cases.

    β-blocker overdose: bradycardia, hypotension, hypoglycemia. Treat with glucagon (bypasses β receptor, uses cAMP independently).

    Vision: eye anatomy and visual pathway A&P

    Light is focused by cornea + lens, converted to electrical signal by photoreceptors, and processed by retinal neurons before traveling through the optic nerve to the brain. Where the visual pathway is interrupted predicts the visual field cut.

    Notes

    • Eye layers (outside in):
      • Fibrous tunic: sclera (white), cornea (anterior, refractive).
      • Vascular tunic (uvea): choroid, ciliary body, iris.
      • Neural tunic: retina.
    • Refractive elements (in order light passes):
      • Cornea (~⅔ of refraction).
      • Aqueous humor (anterior chamber).
      • Pupil (size controlled by iris).
      • Lens (accommodation by ciliary muscle: contraction → lens rounds for near vision).
      • Vitreous humor.
    • Retina cells (light comes through outer layers first to reach photoreceptors):
      • Photoreceptors:
        • Rods: dim light, peripheral, no color.
        • Cones: bright light, color (3 types: S/M/L wavelength), central vision (fovea is all cones).
      • Bipolar cells: relay.
      • Ganglion cells: their axons form the optic nerve.
      • Horizontal and amacrine cells: lateral processing.
    • Phototransduction (light onto retinal):
      • Rhodopsin (rods) or photopsin (cones) absorbs photon → 11-cis-retinal → all-trans-retinal → G-protein cascade → ↓ cGMP → close Na⁺ channels → hyperpolarization → ↓ NT release.
      • Counterintuitive: photoreceptors hyperpolarize in light (they're depolarized in dark).
    • Visual pathway:
      • Retina → optic nerve (CN II) → optic chiasm → optic tract → lateral geniculate nucleus (thalamus) → optic radiations → primary visual cortex (occipital lobe).
      • At the chiasm: nasal retinal fibers cross; temporal fibers stay ipsilateral.

    Summary: visual field deficits

    Lesion siteVisual field defectClassic cause
    Optic nerveIpsilateral monocular blindnessOptic neuritis (MS), trauma
    Optic chiasm (center)Bitemporal hemianopiaPituitary adenoma
    Optic tractContralateral homonymous hemianopiaStroke, tumor
    Optic radiation (temporal)Contralateral upper quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")Temporal lobe lesion
    Optic radiation (parietal)Contralateral lower quadrantanopiaParietal lobe lesion
    Occipital cortexHomonymous hemianopia, macular sparingPCA stroke

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Glaucoma: ↑ intraocular pressure (often impaired aqueous humor outflow) → optic nerve damage. Open-angle (chronic, asymptomatic until late) and closed-angle (acute emergency: red painful eye, fixed dilated pupil, halos around lights). Macular degeneration: central vision loss with intact peripheral. Retinal detachment: sudden "curtain" over visual field, flashes, floaters.

    Nervous · Concept 13 of 16

    Hearing: ear anatomy and auditory pathway A&P

    Sound is air pressure waves → mechanical bone vibration → fluid wave in the cochlea → bending of hair cell stereocilia → electrical signal to brain. Where in this chain the problem sits determines what kind of hearing loss the patient has.

    Notes

    • Three compartments:
      • External ear: pinna (auricle), external auditory canal, tympanic membrane.
      • Middle ear: air-filled cavity with three ossicles (malleus → incus → stapes). Eustachian tube to nasopharynx equalizes pressure.
      • Inner ear: bony labyrinth filled with perilymph; membranous labyrinth filled with endolymph. Contains cochlea (hearing) and vestibular apparatus (balance, Concept 14).
    • Sound transmission pathway:
      1. Sound wave → tympanic membrane vibrates.
      2. Malleus → incus → stapes amplifies and transmits.
      3. Stapes pushes on oval window → fluid wave in cochlea (scala vestibuli).
      4. Fluid wave travels along basilar membrane → bends hair cell stereocilia in organ of Corti.
      5. Hair cell mechanotransduction → opens K⁺ channels → depolarization → release glutamate onto auditory nerve (CN VIII).
      6. Pressure dissipates at round window.
    • Tonotopic organization: basilar membrane is stiff and narrow at base, floppy and wide at apex. High-frequency sounds peak at base; low-frequency sounds peak at apex. Maps onto primary auditory cortex.
    • Auditory pathway: CN VIII → cochlear nuclei (medulla) → superior olivary nucleus (bilateral) → inferior colliculus (midbrain) → medial geniculate nucleus (thalamus) → primary auditory cortex (temporal lobe).
    • Bilateral pathway means unilateral cortical lesions rarely cause complete deafness.
    • Hearing loss types:
      • Conductive: problem in external or middle ear (wax, otitis media, otosclerosis, perforated TM). Sound can't reach the inner ear normally.
      • Sensorineural: problem in inner ear or auditory nerve (noise damage, presbycusis, ototoxic drugs, Ménière, acoustic neuroma).

    Summary: Weber and Rinne tests

    TestNormalConductive loss (one ear)Sensorineural loss (one ear)
    Weber (tuning fork on forehead)Equal both sidesLateralizes to affected earLateralizes to unaffected ear
    Rinne (mastoid then ear)AC > BC bilaterallyBC > AC on affected sideAC > BC on affected side (both reduced)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Ototoxic drugs: aminoglycosides (gentamicin), loop diuretics (high-dose furosemide), cisplatin, salicylates (reversible at high doses). Mostly damage cochlear hair cells → sensorineural loss. Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma): tumor on CN VIII at cerebellopontine angle → progressive unilateral SNHL, tinnitus, balance issues. Presbycusis: age-related high-frequency SNHL → trouble in noisy rooms before quiet conversation.

    Nervous · Concept 14 of 16

    Balance: vestibular apparatus and pathways A&P

    The vestibular system tells the brain how the head is oriented and moving. Two structures: semicircular canals (rotation) and otolith organs (linear acceleration + gravity). Mismatch among vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive input = vertigo or motion sickness.

    Notes

    • Two functional units:
      • Semicircular canals (3 per side: anterior, posterior, lateral): detect angular (rotational) acceleration. Endolymph movement bends cupula → bends hair cells in ampulla.
      • Otolith organs (utricle, saccule): detect linear acceleration and head tilt relative to gravity. Otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) sit on a gelatinous membrane over hair cells; gravity shifts them.
    • Hair cell transduction: stereocilia bend → mechanotransduction channels open → K⁺ enters from endolymph → depolarization → release glutamate onto vestibular nerve (CN VIII).
    • Central pathway:
      • Vestibular nerve → vestibular nuclei (pons/medulla) → outputs to:
        • Cerebellum (coordination).
        • Spinal cord via vestibulospinal tract (posture).
        • Cranial nerve nuclei III, IV, VI via medial longitudinal fasciculus (eye movements — vestibulo-ocular reflex, VOR).
        • Cerebral cortex (conscious perception of motion).
    • Vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR): head turns one way → eyes reflexively move the opposite way at the same speed → image stays stable on the retina. Tested with head impulse test.
    • Three-way integration: balance requires agreement among vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive (joint position) input. Conflict produces vertigo or motion sickness.

    Summary: peripheral vs central vertigo

    FeaturePeripheral (inner ear / CN VIII)Central (brainstem / cerebellum)
    OnsetSudden, severeGradual, less severe
    NystagmusHorizontal, unidirectional, fatigableVertical or multi-directional, non-fatigable
    HearingOften affectedUsually normal
    Other neuro signsAbsentOften present (diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia)
    Common causesBPPV, vestibular neuritis, Ménière, acoustic neuromaStroke, MS, tumor

    CLINICAL THREAD

    BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo): otoconia displaced into a semicircular canal (usually posterior). Brief vertigo with head position changes. Dix-Hallpike test confirms; Epley maneuver treats. Ménière disease: endolymphatic hydrops → episodic triad of vertigo, fluctuating SNHL, tinnitus, with aural fullness. Vestibular neuritis: viral inflammation of vestibular nerve → severe vertigo days to weeks, no hearing loss.

    Nervous · Concept 15 of 16

    Olfaction and gustation A&P

    Smell and taste are chemical senses. Smell uses CN I (olfactory) and is the only sense that does not relay through the thalamus first. Taste uses three cranial nerves and relies on five basic tastes plus enormous input from smell.

    Notes

    • Olfaction:
      • Olfactory epithelium in the roof of the nasal cavity. Bipolar olfactory neurons with cilia extending into mucus.
      • Odorant binds GPCR on cilia → G-protein → ↑ cAMP → opens cation channel → depolarization.
      • Axons (CN I fibers) pass through cribriform plate → synapse at olfactory bulb → olfactory tract → directly to olfactory cortex (piriform), amygdala, hippocampus. Bypasses thalamus.
      • Adaptation is rapid — you stop noticing your own perfume in minutes.
      • Anosmia (loss of smell): viral URI, COVID-19, head trauma (cribriform fracture shears olfactory fibers), neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer, Parkinson — often early sign).
    • Gustation:
      • Taste buds in tongue papillae (mainly fungiform, foliate, circumvallate), plus palate, pharynx, epiglottis.
      • Each taste bud has 50-150 taste receptor cells (with microvilli) + supporting cells.
      • Five basic tastes:
        • Sweet: GPCR (T1R2/T1R3).
        • Bitter: GPCR (T2R family — many).
        • Umami: GPCR (T1R1/T1R3), responds to glutamate.
        • Salty: Na⁺ enters epithelial Na⁺ channels directly.
        • Sour: H⁺ blocks K⁺ channels (and other mechanisms) → depolarization.
      • Three cranial nerves carry taste:
        • CN VII (facial): anterior 2/3 tongue (chorda tympani branch).
        • CN IX (glossopharyngeal): posterior 1/3 tongue.
        • CN X (vagus): epiglottis and pharynx.
      • Pathway: cranial nerve → nucleus solitarius (medulla) → thalamus → primary gustatory cortex (insula).
      • Most "flavor" is actually smell (retronasal olfaction) — that's why food tastes bland when you have a cold.

    Summary

    SenseCranial nerve(s)Pathway shortcut
    SmellCN IDirect to olfactory cortex (no thalamic relay)
    Taste (anterior 2/3)CN VII→ nucleus solitarius → thalamus → insula
    Taste (posterior 1/3)CN IX→ nucleus solitarius → thalamus → insula
    Taste (epiglottis/pharynx)CN X→ nucleus solitarius → thalamus → insula

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Sudden anosmia was the most reliable early symptom of COVID-19 infection during the pandemic. Anosmia is often the earliest sign of neurodegenerative disease: olfactory bulbs sit close to entry points of α-synuclein (Parkinson) and amyloid (Alzheimer) pathology. Loss of taste with stroke is rare — bilateral pathway provides redundancy.

    Nervous · Concept 16 of 16

    Common neurological conditions CLIN

    Most neuro pathology is one of four patterns: vascular (stroke), demyelinating, neurodegenerative, or seizure. Localizing the lesion and identifying the pattern narrows the diagnosis quickly.

    Notes

    • Stroke:
      • Ischemic (~80%): thrombotic, embolic, lacunar. tPA window ≤ 4.5 h (some up to 9 h with imaging). Mechanical thrombectomy up to 24 h in selected.
      • Hemorrhagic (~20%): intracerebral or subarachnoid. Often from HTN (deep, basal ganglia) or aneurysm (SAH).
      • BE FAST: Balance, Eyes (vision change), Face droop, Arm drift, Speech, Time.
      • Localization: MCA (contralateral arm/face weakness, aphasia if dominant); ACA (leg); PCA (visual field cut).
    • Multiple sclerosis (MS):
      • Autoimmune demyelination of CNS (oligodendrocytes).
      • Lesions disseminated in space and time on MRI.
      • Sx: optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, sensory disturbance, fatigue, bladder dysfunction.
      • Treatment: corticosteroids for acute exacerbations; disease-modifying therapies long-term.
    • Parkinson disease:
      • Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra → ↓ dopamine to striatum.
      • TRAP: Tremor (at rest, "pill-rolling"), Rigidity (cogwheel), Akinesia/bradykinesia, Postural instability.
      • Treatment: levodopa/carbidopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, deep brain stimulation.
    • Alzheimer disease:
      • Most common dementia. Amyloid plaques + tau neurofibrillary tangles → hippocampal and cortical atrophy.
      • Progressive memory loss, then executive dysfunction, then personality change and global decline.
      • Treatment: AChE inhibitors (donepezil), NMDA modulator (memantine), anti-amyloid antibodies in select cases.
    • Seizures and epilepsy:
      • Seizure = abnormal synchronous neuronal firing. Epilepsy = recurrent unprovoked seizures.
      • Types: focal (one area, with or without awareness) vs generalized (both hemispheres). Tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic.
      • Status epilepticus: seizure > 5 min or recurrent without recovery between → emergency.
      • First-line acute: benzodiazepines (lorazepam IV).
    • Other key conditions:
      • Migraine: throbbing, often unilateral, photophobia, phonophobia, ± aura.
      • Meningitis: fever + neck stiffness + altered mental status (classic triad). LP confirms.
      • Myasthenia gravis: anti-AChR antibodies at NMJ → fatigable weakness, especially ocular. Improves with AChE inhibitors.
      • Guillain-Barré: ascending flaccid paralysis post-infection; albuminocytologic dissociation on LP; IVIG or plasmapheresis.

    Hemorrhagic strokeSudden severe headache, deficitBP control, neurosurgery
