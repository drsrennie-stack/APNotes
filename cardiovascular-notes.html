    Heart location, pericardium, wall layers A

    The heart sits in the middle mediastinum, wrapped in a tough fibrous sac with a slick serous lining that lets it beat without friction.

    Notes

    • Location: middle mediastinum, between the lungs, posterior to the sternum. About two-thirds lies left of midline.
    • Orientation: base (top) points to the right shoulder, apex points anterior-inferior to the left (5th intercostal space, midclavicular line). PMI (point of maximal impulse) is felt here.
    • Pericardium (3 layers from outside in):
      • Fibrous pericardium: tough, inelastic, anchors heart to diaphragm and great vessels.
      • Parietal serous pericardium: lines the inside of the fibrous layer.
      • Visceral serous pericardium (epicardium): adheres to the heart surface.
    • Pericardial cavity: the slit between parietal and visceral serous layers. Contains ~15-50 mL serous fluid for friction reduction.
    • Heart wall (3 layers from outside in):
      • Epicardium: visceral serous pericardium.
      • Myocardium: cardiac muscle. The pump. Thickness varies by chamber.
      • Endocardium: endothelium continuous with the lining of the great vessels.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Cardiac tamponade: rapid fluid accumulation in the pericardial cavity (blood from trauma, effusion) compresses the heart because the fibrous pericardium will not stretch. Beck's triad: hypotension, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds. Pulsus paradoxus on assessment. Pericarditis: inflammation of serous layers. Sharp chest pain that worsens lying flat, eases sitting forward. Pericardial friction rub on auscultation.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 2 of 19

    Chambers, valves, great vessels, and internal landmarks A

    Four chambers, four valves, and one rule: blood always flows from high pressure to low. Each chamber has signature internal landmarks. Knowing those landmarks is how you orient on a cadaver, an echo, or a CT.

    Notes

    • Four chambers (wall thickness tracks workload):
      • Right atrium (RA): receives deoxygenated blood from SVC, IVC, coronary sinus.
      • Right ventricle (RV): pumps to the lungs through the pulmonary trunk. Wall ~4 mm.
      • Left atrium (LA): receives oxygenated blood from four pulmonary veins.
      • Left ventricle (LV): pumps to the systemic circulation through the aorta. Wall ~10-15 mm, 3 to 4 times thicker than RV.
    • Great vessels:
      • Into the heart: SVC, IVC, coronary sinus (into RA); four pulmonary veins (into LA).
      • Out of the heart: pulmonary trunk (from RV); aorta (from LV).
    • Right atrium internal landmarks:
      • Sinus venarum: smooth-walled posterior region. Receives the openings of the SVC, IVC, and coronary sinus.
      • Pectinate muscles: parallel ridges on the anterior wall and inside the right auricle (atrial appendage).
      • Crista terminalis: vertical muscular ridge that separates the smooth sinus venarum from the trabecular (pectinate) region.
      • Fossa ovalis: oval depression on the interatrial septum. Closed remnant of the fetal foramen ovale.
      • SA (sinoatrial) node: embedded in the wall of the sinus venarum near the SVC opening. The natural pacemaker.
      • AV (atrioventricular) node: in the floor of the RA, in the interatrial septum near the coronary sinus opening (within Koch's triangle).
      • Tricuspid orifice: entrance into the RV.
    • Right ventricle internal landmarks:
      • Trabeculae carneae: irregular muscular ridges on the inner wall.
      • Papillary muscles (3): anterior, posterior, septal. Project into the chamber.
      • Chordae tendineae: tendinous cords running from papillary muscle tips to the free edges of the tricuspid cusps. Prevent valve prolapse during systole.
      • Moderator band (septomarginal trabecula): muscular bridge from the interventricular septum to the anterior papillary muscle. Carries the right bundle branch.
      • Conus arteriosus (infundibulum): smooth-walled outflow region leading to the pulmonary valve.
    • Left atrium internal landmarks:
      • Smooth main body: derived embryologically from incorporated pulmonary vein tissue.
      • Four pulmonary vein openings: two superior, two inferior. Posterior wall.
      • Pectinate muscles confined to the auricle (left atrial appendage).
      • Left atrial appendage: a small ear-shaped pouch. Clinically critical — most common site of thrombus formation in atrial fibrillation.
      • Valve of fossa ovalis: seen on the septal side; pushes against the right side and closes the fetal foramen.
      • Mitral orifice: entrance into the LV.
    • Left ventricle internal landmarks:
      • Trabeculae carneae: finer and more numerous than in the RV.
      • Papillary muscles (2): anterior (anterolateral) and posterior (posteromedial). Larger than the RV papillaries.
      • Chordae tendineae from each papillary muscle anchor both cusps of the mitral valve.
      • Aortic vestibule: smooth-walled outflow region leading to the aortic valve.
      • Wall 3 to 4 times thicker than the RV because of systemic afterload.
    • Atrioventricular (AV) valves:
      • Tricuspid (right): three cusps — anterior, posterior, septal. Between RA and RV.
      • Mitral / bicuspid (left): two cusps — anterior, posterior. Between LA and LV.
      • Both anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles. The papillary muscles contract just before the rest of the ventricle to tension the chordae and prevent valve prolapse.
    • Semilunar valves:
      • Pulmonary semilunar: between RV and pulmonary trunk.
      • Aortic semilunar: between LV and aorta. Aortic sinuses behind two of the cusps give rise to the coronary arteries.
      • Three cusps each, no chordae. Snap shut from backflow pressure.
    • Cardiac skeleton: dense fibrous rings around all four valves. Anchor valves, electrically insulate atria from ventricles (only the AV bundle pierces it).

    Summary

    ValveLocationOpen whenAnchored by

    MitralLA → LVVentricular filling (diastole)Chordae + papillary muscles
    Pulmonary semilunarRV → pulm trunkVentricular ejection (systole)Three cusps, no chordae

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Stenosis (valve will not open fully): increases workload upstream. Mitral stenosis → LA pressure rises → pulmonary congestion. Regurgitation (valve will not close fully): blood leaks backward. Mitral regurgitation → blood pushed back into LA during systole → murmur, reduced forward output. Heart murmurs: turbulent flow heard on auscultation. Listen at the valve's auscultation point, not its anatomical location.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 3 of 19

    Coronary circulation A

    The heart cannot use the blood inside its chambers as its own supply. It feeds itself through the coronary arteries, and almost all of that feeding happens during diastole.

    Notes

    • Origin: right and left coronary arteries arise from the aortic sinuses just above the aortic valve.
    • Left coronary artery (LCA):
      • Left anterior descending (LAD) in the anterior interventricular sulcus. Supplies anterior LV, anterior 2/3 of septum, apex.
      • Circumflex (LCx) in the coronary sulcus. Supplies LA and lateral LV.
    • Right coronary artery (RCA):
      • Right marginal: supplies RA and lateral RV.
      • Posterior interventricular (posterior descending, PDA): inferior LV and posterior 1/3 of septum. In about 85% of people the PDA branches from the RCA ("right-dominant" circulation).
      • In most people, supplies SA node and AV node.
    • Venous drainage: coronary veins → coronary sinus on the posterior heart → right atrium.
    • Timing: coronary arteries fill during diastole. Systole compresses the intramural vessels.

    Summary

    VesselSuppliesClinical association
    LADAnterior LV, anterior septum, apex"Widow maker" — large anterior MI
    CircumflexLateral LV, LALateral wall MI
    RCARA, RV, often SA/AV nodesInferior MI, bradyarrhythmias, heart blocks
    PDA (usually from RCA)Inferior LV, posterior septumInferior wall MI

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Tachycardia shortens diastole more than systole. That cuts coronary perfusion time. In a patient with stable angina, exercise (which raises HR) can tip them into ischemia even without new plaque. This is why we use rate control (beta blockers) and reduce demand. ECG changes localize the lesion: anterior ST elevation in V1 to V4 points to LAD; inferior ST elevation in II, III, aVF points to RCA; lateral changes in I, aVL, V5, V6 point to circumflex.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 4 of 19

    Conduction system: SA, AV, bundle of His, Purkinje A&P

    The heart's electrical system is built so that the atria contract first, the AV node deliberately delays, and then the ventricles fire from apex upward in a coordinated wave.

    Notes

    • SA (sinoatrial) node: in the right atrium near the SVC opening. The natural pacemaker. Intrinsic rate 60-100 bpm.
    • AV (atrioventricular) node: floor of right atrium near the tricuspid valve. Introduces a deliberate ~0.1 second delay so atria finish emptying before ventricles contract ("atrial kick"). Intrinsic rate 40-60 bpm if SA fails.
    • Bundle of His (AV bundle): only electrical bridge through the fibrous skeleton between atria and ventricles.
    • Right and left bundle branches: run down the interventricular septum.
    • Purkinje fibers: spread through the ventricular myocardium from apex upward. Cause coordinated contraction. Intrinsic rate 20-40 bpm.
    • Pacemaker hierarchy: SA > AV > ventricles. The fastest pacemaker wins. If SA fails, AV takes over (junctional rhythm). If AV fails, ventricles take over (slow, unreliable).

    Summary

    StructureFunctionIntrinsic rate
    SA nodePrimary pacemaker60-100 bpm
    AV nodeDelay, backup pacemaker40-60 bpm
    Bundle of HisAtria-to-ventricle conductionConducts, does not pace
    Bundle branchesSeptum conductionConducts
    Purkinje fibersVentricular spread, apex up20-40 bpm (escape rhythm)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Heart blocks are AV node or below.

    • 1st degree: prolonged PR, all beats conduct.
    • 2nd degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach): progressive PR lengthening, then a dropped beat.
    • 2nd degree Mobitz II: PR constant, sudden dropped beats. Higher risk.
    • 3rd degree (complete): atria and ventricles fire independently. Needs pacemaker.

    Atropine works on AV node by blocking parasympathetic input. Pacemakers replace failed nodes.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Cardiac myocyte physiology

    Cardiac muscle contraction: how it differs from other muscle P

    Cardiac muscle uses the same sliding-filament machinery as skeletal muscle, but it solves a different problem: it must contract rhythmically, never tetanize, and recruit every cell every beat. Three features make that possible — intercalated discs, calcium-induced calcium release, and a long plateau in the action potential.

    Notes

    • Structural differences from skeletal muscle:
      • Branched, mononucleated (or binucleated) cells, not long multinucleated fibers.
      • Intercalated discs connect cell ends. Two components: desmosomes (mechanical anchoring so cells don't pull apart) and gap junctions (electrical connections so the whole myocardium fires as one functional syncytium).
      • Abundant mitochondria, ~30% of cell volume (vs ~2% in skeletal). The heart is obligately aerobic.
      • T-tubules align with Z-discs (in cardiac, they are wider and at the Z-line rather than at the A-I junction as in skeletal).
      • Sarcoplasmic reticulum less developed than skeletal; depends on extracellular Ca++ as well as stored.
    • Excitation-contraction coupling: calcium-induced calcium release (CICR).
      • Action potential opens voltage-gated L-type Ca++ channels (DHP receptors) in the T-tubule.
      • A small influx of extracellular Ca++ triggers the ryanodine receptors (RyR2) on the SR to release a much larger Ca++ load.
      • Ca++ binds troponin C → tropomyosin moves → cross-bridge cycling → contraction.
      • Relaxation requires Ca++ to be pumped back into SR (SERCA, blocked by phospholamban) and out of the cell (Na/Ca exchanger, Ca-ATPase).
      • Key contrast: skeletal muscle uses direct mechanical coupling (DHP-RyR1 physical link), no extracellular Ca++ needed. Cardiac muscle requires extracellular Ca++.
    • Action potential differences (working myocyte):
      • Resting membrane potential: ~ −90 mV (similar to skeletal).
      • Phase 0: rapid Na+ influx → depolarization to ~ +20 mV.
      • Phase 1: brief partial repolarization (transient K+ out).
      • Phase 2: plateau ~200 ms. Ca++ in balances K+ out. This is the signature feature.
      • Phase 3: K+ efflux dominates → rapid repolarization.
      • Phase 4: resting potential restored.
      • Total duration ~250-300 ms (vs ~2 ms in skeletal muscle).
    • Pacemaker cell physiology (SA / AV nodes):
      • No stable resting potential. They drift upward in Phase 4 ("funny current," If, Na+ inward).
      • Threshold reached → Phase 0 driven by slow Ca++ (not Na+).
      • Phase 3: K+ efflux repolarizes.
      • The drift rate sets the heart rate. Sympathetic ↑ slope (faster), parasympathetic ↓ slope (slower).
    • Why no tetanus: the long absolute refractory period spans almost the entire contraction. A second stimulus cannot fire until the first has nearly finished. The heart physically cannot tetanize. This is what allows it to fill between beats.
    • Functional syncytium: atrial syncytium and ventricular syncytium contract as units. Action potentials spread cell-to-cell through gap junctions. Either the chamber contracts together or it doesn't (all-or-none at the chamber level).
    • Length-tension relationship: cardiac sarcomeres rest at less than optimal overlap. Stretching them (increased preload) increases force. This is the molecular basis of the Frank-Starling mechanism. Cardiac muscle is also more sensitive to length-induced changes in calcium sensitivity than skeletal muscle.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    • Calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem): block L-type Ca++ channels → reduce Ca++ influx → ↓ contractility and slow AV nodal conduction. Cardiac muscle's dependence on extracellular Ca++ is exactly why these drugs work and why they can precipitate heart failure in vulnerable patients.
    • Digoxin: blocks the Na/K ATPase → ↑ intracellular Na+ → less Ca++ extrusion via Na/Ca exchange → ↑ intracellular Ca++ → ↑ contractility (positive inotrope). It also enhances vagal tone, slowing AV conduction.
    • Hyperkalemia: shifts the resting membrane potential closer to threshold initially (mild K+ elevation), but at higher levels inactivates Na+ channels → reduced AP upstroke → conduction slowing, widened QRS, peaked T waves, eventual asystole.
    • Hypocalcemia: prolongs phase 2 → prolonged QT interval → risk of torsades.
    • Ischemia: ATP depletion impairs SERCA and the Na/K ATPase → calcium overload → contraction band necrosis and arrhythmogenicity.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. What does CICR stand for, and which two channels are the key players?
    2. DOK 4. Explain in mechanistic terms why digoxin increases the force of cardiac contraction without directly opening calcium channels.
    Show answers
    1. Calcium-induced calcium release. Voltage-gated L-type Ca++ channels (DHP receptors) on the T-tubule and ryanodine receptors (RyR2) on the SR.
    2. Digoxin inhibits the sarcolemmal Na/K ATPase. Intracellular Na+ rises. The Na/Ca exchanger normally uses the Na+ gradient to pump Ca++ out of the cell, three Na+ in for one Ca++ out. The next CICR cycle releases a larger Ca++ load → stronger contraction. It is an indirect inotrope.

    ECG basics: P, QRS, T, and intervals P

    The ECG is just a recording of electrical events. Each wave is depolarization or repolarization of a specific chamber. Once you know which event is which, you can read what the heart is doing.

    Notes

    • P wave: atrial depolarization. Just before atrial contraction.
    • PR interval: start of P to start of QRS. Reflects AV node delay. Normal 0.12 to 0.20 s (3 to 5 small boxes).
    • QRS complex: ventricular depolarization. Atrial repolarization happens here but is buried under QRS. Normal < 0.12 s.
    • ST segment: ventricles fully depolarized. Should be at baseline (isoelectric).
    • T wave: ventricular repolarization.
    • QT interval: start of QRS to end of T. Total time of ventricular depolarization plus repolarization. Varies with HR (corrected QT, QTc).
    • Standard paper: 25 mm/s. Small box = 0.04 s. Large box = 0.20 s. Five large boxes = 1 second.
    • Rate calculation: 300 / number of large boxes between R waves.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    • ST elevation: acute injury (STEMI). Time-critical.
    • ST depression / T inversion: ischemia.
    • Prolonged PR: 1st degree AV block.
    • Widened QRS: bundle branch block or ventricular origin beat.
    • Long QT: risk of torsades de pointes. Drug-induced (some antibiotics, antiemetics, antipsychotics) or congenital.
    • Peaked T waves: hyperkalemia.
    • U waves: hypokalemia.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 6 of 19

    Cardiac cycle: systole, diastole, heart sounds P

    One heartbeat is a pressure story. Track which chamber has the higher pressure at each phase and the valve behavior, sounds, and blood movement follow automatically.

    Notes

    • One cycle at 75 bpm is about 0.8 s: ~0.3 s systole, ~0.5 s diastole.
    • Phases (one cycle):
      • Atrial systole: atria contract, finish filling ventricles (atrial kick = last 20-30% of EDV).
      • Isovolumetric ventricular contraction: ventricles begin to contract; AV valves close (S1); pressure rises with no volume change because semilunar valves are still closed.
      • Ventricular ejection: ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/pulmonary pressure → semilunar valves open → blood ejected. Stroke volume leaves.
      • Isovolumetric relaxation: ventricles relax; aortic and pulmonary pressures now exceed ventricular pressure → semilunar valves snap shut (S2); volume unchanged because AV valves still closed.
      • Ventricular filling: ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure → AV valves open → passive filling (most of EDV).
    • Heart sounds:
      • S1 ("lub"): AV valves close. Marks beginning of ventricular systole. Loudest at apex.
      • S2 ("dub"): semilunar valves close. Marks end of ventricular systole. Loudest at base.
      • S3 (ventricular gallop): early diastole, rapid filling. Normal in young athletes and pregnancy. Pathologic in adults with heart failure.
      • S4 (atrial gallop): late diastole, atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle. Pathologic (LVH, HTN, MI).
    • Murmurs: turbulent flow. Timed against S1 and S2 (systolic vs diastolic).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    S3 in an adult is a soft early diastolic sound often heard in volume overload from heart failure (the ventricle is full, then more blood slams into a stretched chamber). S4 reflects a stiff ventricle. New onset S3 or S4 in an adult is a finding, not a noise to ignore. Split S2 on inspiration is physiologic (delayed pulmonary valve closure from increased venous return); fixed splitting suggests atrial septal defect.

    Reading the PV loop in disease:

    • Aortic stenosis: loop becomes taller (high LV pressure required to open the stenotic valve).
    • Aortic regurgitation: loop widens (extra volume regurgitates back in during diastole).
    • Mitral regurgitation: loop loses its isovolumetric contraction phase (blood escapes back to LA as soon as LV pressure rises).
    • HFrEF: ESPVR shifts down-right (less force at any volume); loop shifts right with larger EDV and ESV, narrower SV.
    • HFpEF: EDPVR shifts up-left (stiff ventricle); same EDV requires much higher filling pressure.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 7 of 19

    Cardiac output (CO = HR × SV) P

    Cardiac output equals heart rate times stroke volume. Almost every cardiac intervention is changing one of those two numbers, or both.

    Notes

    • CO = heart rate (bpm) × stroke volume (mL/beat). Normal resting CO ~5 L/min. Equals total blood volume per minute.
    • Stroke volume (SV): volume ejected per beat. Normal ~70 mL.
      • SV = EDV − ESV.
      • EDV: end-diastolic volume (~120 mL). ESV: end-systolic volume (~50 mL).
    • Ejection fraction (EF): SV / EDV × 100. Normal ≥ 55%. Single most important number for systolic function.
    • Cardiac index (CI): CO / body surface area (m²). Normalizes across body sizes. Normal 2.5 to 4.0 L/min/m².
    • Determinants of SV (Concept 8): preload, afterload, contractility.
    • Determinants of HR: intrinsic SA rate modulated by ANS (sympathetic ↑, parasympathetic ↓), hormones (epinephrine, thyroid), temperature, electrolytes.

    Summary

    Worked calculations

    1. Stroke volume: Patient's echo shows EDV 130 mL, ESV 55 mL.
      • SV = EDV − ESV = 130 − 55 = 75 mL/beat.
      • Interpretation: within the normal range (60-100 mL).
    2. Cardiac output: Same patient, HR 80 bpm.
      • CO = HR × SV = 80 × 75 = 6,000 mL/min = 6.0 L/min.
      • Interpretation: normal resting CO (4-8 L/min).
    3. Ejection fraction: Same patient.
      • EF = SV / EDV × 100 = 75/130 × 100 = 57.7%.
      • Interpretation: preserved systolic function (≥ 55%). If this number had been 35%, you would be looking at HFrEF.
    4. Cardiac index: Same patient, BSA 1.8 m².
      • CI = CO / BSA = 6.0 / 1.8 = 3.3 L/min/m².
      • Interpretation: normal (2.5-4.0). A CI < 2.2 typically indicates cardiogenic shock and triggers escalation of care.
    5. Worked exam-style case: 68-year-old with HFrEF arrives in clinic. Echo: EDV 200 mL, ESV 140 mL. HR 90 bpm. BSA 1.9 m². Compute SV, CO, EF, CI and interpret.
      • SV = 200 − 140 = 60 mL.
      • CO = 90 × 60 = 5,400 mL/min = 5.4 L/min.
      • EF = 60/200 × 100 = 30%.
      • CI = 5.4 / 1.9 = 2.8 L/min/m².

Exercise tolerance and prognosis are poor without medical therapy targeting remodeling (ACE-I/ARB/ARNI, beta blocker, MRA, SGLT2 inhibitor).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Heart failure with reduced EF (HFrEF): EF < 40%. Systolic dysfunction. Classic dilated ventricle. Heart failure with preserved EF (HFpEF): EF ≥ 50%. Diastolic dysfunction — the ventricle cannot relax and fill properly. Shock: inadequate tissue perfusion. Often a CO problem (cardiogenic, hypovolemic) but distributive shock (sepsis) is a TPR problem with usually high CO.

    Preload, afterload, contractility P

    Three independent levers move stroke volume. Preload is the stretch you start with, afterload is the pressure you have to push against, contractility is how hard the muscle squeezes regardless of either.

    Notes

    • Preload: the wall stretch on the ventricle at the end of diastole. Best clinical proxy: EDV. Depends mostly on venous return.
      • Increased by: increased venous return (volume infusion, lying down, exercise), bradycardia (longer fill time).
      • Decreased by: hemorrhage, vasodilators (venodilators like nitroglycerin), tachycardia.
    • Afterload: the resistance the ventricle must push against to eject. For the LV, best proxy: aortic pressure (or systemic vascular resistance).
      • Increased by: hypertension, aortic stenosis, vasoconstriction.
      • Decreased by: vasodilators (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, hydralazine), reduced TPR.
    • Contractility (inotropy): the intrinsic strength of contraction independent of preload and afterload.
      • Increased by: sympathetic stimulation (β1), positive inotropes (dobutamine, digoxin, milrinone), calcium.
      • Decreased by: β blockers, calcium channel blockers (non-dihydropyridines), acidosis, ischemia, hypoxia.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Concept 9 of 19

    Frank-Starling mechanism P

    The healthy heart pumps out whatever venous return delivers. Stretch the muscle and it contracts harder. This is how the two sides of the heart stay matched without external control.

    Notes

    • Definition: within physiologic limits, ↑ EDV (preload) → ↑ stretch → ↑ force of contraction → ↑ SV.
    • Mechanism: the length-tension relationship of cardiac sarcomeres. Stretching brings actin and myosin into more optimal overlap and increases calcium sensitivity.
    • Why it matters:
      • Beat-to-beat balance: if RV pumps a little more, LV preload rises and LV matches output.
      • Allows the heart to respond to changes in venous return without nervous system input.
    • Limits:
      • At very high preload, sarcomeres overstretch and force falls (descending limb).
      • In a failing heart, the curve is flatter and shifted down. Adding more preload buys less benefit and risks pulmonary edema.

    ANS control of the heart P

    Sympathetic input speeds up, strengthens, and conducts faster through the heart. Parasympathetic input does the opposite, but mostly just slows the rate. The medulla coordinates it through baroreceptor feedback.

    Notes

    • Sympathetic:
      • Cardiac accelerator nerves from T1-T4 spinal cord.
      • Postganglionic NE → β1 receptors on SA, AV, atrial and ventricular myocytes.
      • Effects: ↑ HR (chronotropy), ↑ contractility (inotropy), ↑ conduction velocity (dromotropy), ↑ rate of relaxation (lusitropy).
    • Parasympathetic:
      • Vagus nerve (CN X). Releases ACh onto muscarinic (M2) receptors.
      • Densest innervation on SA node, AV node, atria. Minimal on ventricles.
      • Effects: ↓ HR, ↓ AV conduction, modest ↓ atrial contractility.
    • Baseline tone: resting heart is parasympathetic-dominant. Intrinsic SA rate ~100 bpm, vagal tone drops resting HR to ~70.
    • Baroreceptor reflex:
      • Receptors in carotid sinus (CN IX) and aortic arch (CN X) detect stretch (pressure).
      • Send to medulla. If BP rises → ↑ vagal, ↓ sympathetic → ↓ HR, ↓ contractility, vasodilation.
      • If BP falls → ↓ vagal, ↑ sympathetic → ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, vasoconstriction.

    Cardiovascular · Heart · Acute MI in depth

    Acute myocardial infarction: pathophysiology and management CLIN

    An MI is muscle death from prolonged ischemia. The trigger is almost always a plaque rupture inside a coronary artery, and the clock starts the instant flow stops. Time is muscle. Time is mortality.

    Notes

    • Three things have to be present for an MI: a coronary supply, a metabolic demand the supply cannot meet, and time. Remove flow long enough and the muscle dies.
    • ACS spectrum:
      • Stable angina: predictable, exertional, relieved by rest or nitrates. No troponin rise. ECG may be normal at rest.
      • Unstable angina: new, at rest, crescendo, or with less exertion than usual. No troponin rise. ECG may show ischemic changes.
      • NSTEMI: troponin rises (cardiac muscle is dying) without ST elevation. Often ST depression or T inversion.
      • STEMI: ST elevation in two contiguous leads (or new LBBB) plus troponin rise. Transmural infarct.
    • Symptoms: classic crushing substernal chest pain or pressure, often radiating to left arm or jaw, diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea. Atypical presentations in women, older adults, and patients with diabetes — fatigue, dyspnea, epigastric pain, syncope. Do not dismiss atypical symptoms.
    • First-line investigations at the bedside: 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of arrival, IV access, troponin, basic chemistries, chest X-ray.
    • Bedside management priorities (immediate, all patients):
      • Oxygen if SpO₂ < 90% (no routine oxygen if normal).
      • Aspirin 162-325 mg chewed (antiplatelet).
      • Nitroglycerin sublingual if SBP > 90 and not RV infarct.
      • Morphine for unrelieved pain (used cautiously; signals reperfusion failure).
      • Anticoagulation: heparin or LMWH per protocol.
      • P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel, ticagrelor) per cath plan.
      • Beta blocker if no contraindications (no HF, no shock, no bradycardia).
      • High-intensity statin.
    • Reperfusion (STEMI):
      • PCI (preferred): door-to-balloon < 90 min if available.
      • Thrombolytics: if PCI is not available within 120 min from first medical contact; door-to-needle < 30 min.
    • Lead grouping for localization:
      • Anterior: V1-V4 (LAD).
      • Lateral: I, aVL, V5, V6 (circumflex or diagonal LAD).
      • Inferior: II, III, aVF (RCA in most; circumflex in left-dominant).
      • Posterior: tall R + ST depression in V1-V2 (often with inferior MI).
      • RV: ST elevation in V4R (always check in inferior MI — nitrates contraindicated).

    MOLECULAR AND CELLULAR PATHOGENESIS

    • Plaque rupture: the fibrous cap of an atherosclerotic plaque tears. Often a "vulnerable" plaque with thin cap, lipid-rich core, inflammatory infiltrate. Exposed subendothelial collagen and tissue factor activate platelets and the clotting cascade. A thrombus forms over the rupture site.
    • From thrombus to infarct:
      • Coronary flow drops below metabolic demand. ATP production fails within minutes.
      • Loss of Na/K ATPase activity → intracellular Na+ and Ca++ rise → membrane dysfunction.
      • Anaerobic glycolysis raises intracellular lactate and H+ → contractile failure (within seconds).
      • By 20-30 minutes of complete occlusion, irreversible injury begins at the subendocardium (the most vulnerable region — furthest from epicardial coronary supply, highest tension).
      • Wave of cell death spreads from endocardium to epicardium over hours ("wavefront phenomenon"). This is why early reperfusion saves muscle.
    • Reperfusion injury: restoring flow paradoxically harms some already-stressed cells via free radical generation, Ca++ overload, and mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. Net benefit still strongly favors reperfusion, but explains why some early benefit is offset.
    • Inflammatory phase (days 1-7): neutrophils, then macrophages clear debris. The wall is mechanically weakest at days 5-10 — peak risk of free wall rupture, papillary muscle rupture, VSD.
    • Healing phase (weeks 2-8): granulation tissue, then fibrosis. The scar is non-contractile. If large, the LV remodels — chamber dilates, eventual HFrEF.
    • Biomarker kinetics:
      • Troponin I/T: rises within 2-4 h, peaks ~24 h, persists 7-10 days. The standard.
      • CK-MB: rises within 4-6 h, peaks ~24 h, normalizes within 2-3 days. Useful to detect re-infarction during the troponin tail.
      • Myoglobin: rises within 1-2 h but non-specific.

    Most cardiac pathology is one of three patterns: a coronary supply problem, a pump-mechanics problem, or an electrical problem. Place each diagnosis into one of those buckets and the rest of the picture follows.

    Cardiovascular · Vessels · Concept 12 of 19

    Blood vessel histology A

    Every vessel has three tunics, but the thickness and composition of each layer reflects whether the vessel is a pressure reservoir, a distributor, an exchange site, or a return path.

    Notes

    • Three tunics (inside to outside):
      • Tunica intima: endothelium + basement membrane + internal elastic lamina. Continuous with endocardium.
      • Tunica media: smooth muscle ± elastic fibers + external elastic lamina. Controls diameter.
      • Tunica externa (adventitia): connective tissue. Anchors and contains vasa vasorum in large vessels.
    • Elastic (conducting) arteries: aorta and major branches. Thick media rich in elastic laminae. Stretch during systole, recoil during diastole — keeps blood moving forward when ventricle is filling.
    • Muscular (distributing) arteries: brachial, radial, femoral, splenic. Thick media of smooth muscle. Direct blood to specific organs.
    • Arterioles: small. Mostly smooth muscle. Major site of total peripheral resistance. "Stopcocks" of the circulation.
    • Capillaries: single layer of endothelium + basement membrane. Three types:
      • Continuous: tight, most tissues (skin, muscle, lungs, CNS — tightest).
      • Fenestrated: pores. Sites of filtration or fast exchange (kidney glomerulus, intestinal villi, endocrine glands).
      • Sinusoidal: wide, leaky. Liver, bone marrow, spleen. Pass cells and proteins.
    • Venules: drain capillaries. Postcapillary venules are leukocyte exit sites in inflammation.
    • Veins: thinner walls than arteries, larger lumen, valves (esp. in limbs). Hold ~60-70% of total blood volume — capacitance vessels.

    Pulmonary circulation A

    Pulmonary circulation is its own world: low pressure, low resistance, the only place where arteries carry deoxygenated blood and veins carry oxygenated blood.

    Notes

    • Pathway:
      • Deoxygenated blood: RV → pulmonary trunk → right and left pulmonary arteries → progressively smaller arteries → pulmonary capillaries (around alveoli).
      • Oxygenated blood: pulmonary capillaries → pulmonary venules → four pulmonary veins → LA.
    • Pressure profile: systolic ~25 mmHg, diastolic ~10 mmHg, mean pulmonary arterial pressure ~15 mmHg. Roughly 1/6th of systemic pressures.
    • Resistance: low. Pulmonary vessels are short, wide, distensible.
    • Volume: same as systemic — whatever the RV puts out, the LV gets back.
    • Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction: opposite of systemic — alveolar hypoxia causes the local pulmonary arteriole to constrict, shunting blood to better-ventilated alveoli. Optimizes V/Q matching.
    • Pulmonary vs bronchial circulation:
      • Pulmonary: gas exchange, low pressure.
      • Bronchial: from systemic circulation (off thoracic aorta), supplies lung tissue itself with oxygenated arterial blood.

    ResistanceLowHigh
    Volume per minute= systemic CO= systemic CO

    Veins carryOxygenatedDeoxygenated

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pulmonary embolism (PE): a clot (often from a DVT) lodges in the pulmonary arterial tree. Sudden ↑ pulmonary resistance → RV strain. Classic signs: sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxemia. Pulmonary hypertension: chronically elevated pulmonary pressure → RV hypertrophy → cor pulmonale (right heart failure from lung pathology). Common in COPD, chronic PE, idiopathic PAH.

    Hemodynamics: pressure, flow, resistance, Poiseuille P

    Flow equals pressure difference divided by resistance. Resistance is dominated by vessel radius to the fourth power, which is why arterioles control almost everything.

    Notes

    • Core equation: Q = ΔP / R.
      • Q = flow.
      • ΔP = pressure gradient driving flow (e.g., MAP − right atrial pressure for systemic circulation).
      • R = resistance.
    • Poiseuille's law: R ∝ (η × L) / r⁴.
      • η = viscosity (mostly determined by hematocrit).
      • L = length.
      • r = radius. The fourth power makes radius the dominant lever.
    • Halving radius increases resistance 16×. Doubling radius drops it 16×. This is why arteriolar tone is the master control of TPR.
    • Combining resistances:
      • In series (sequential vessels): R total = R₁ + R₂ + ...
      • In parallel (capillary beds): 1/R total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... → adding parallel paths reduces total resistance.
    • Where is the biggest pressure drop? Across arterioles. They are the site of maximum resistance.
    • Velocity vs flow: flow rate (mL/min) is conserved across the circuit. Velocity (cm/s) is slowest in capillaries because total cross-sectional area is largest there. This is essential for exchange time.

    Summary

    VariableDirectionEffect on resistance
    Vessel radius↓ (vasoconstriction)↑↑↑↑ (r⁴ effect)
    Vessel length↑ (rare clinically; obesity ↑ length)↑
    Viscosity↑ (polycythemia, dehydration)↑

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Most BP-lowering meds work by reducing TPR (radius) or reducing CO. CCBs and ACE-I/ARBs drop afterload by relaxing arterioles. IV fluid resuscitation in shock exploits Q = ΔP/R: raising venous return raises preload, CO, then MAP. Polycythemia (high hematocrit) raises viscosity, slows flow, increases workload on the heart, and raises clot risk.

    2. Flow (Q) is conserved, but Q = velocity × cross-sectional area. Capillaries have the greatest total cross-sectional area of any vascular segment (billions of capillaries in parallel), so velocity must be lowest. This maximizes time for diffusion and exchange.

    Capillary exchange: Starling forces P

    Fluid leaves capillaries on the arteriolar end because hydrostatic pressure wins, and returns on the venular end because oncotic pressure wins. Anything that changes that balance produces edema.

    Notes

    • Four Starling forces:
      • Pc: capillary hydrostatic pressure — pushes fluid out.
      • πc: capillary oncotic pressure (mostly albumin) — pulls fluid in.
      • Pi: interstitial hydrostatic pressure — small.
      • πi: interstitial oncotic pressure — small.
    • Net filtration pressure = (Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi).
    • Arteriolar end: Pc is high (~35 mmHg) → net filtration outward.
    • Venular end: Pc has dropped (~15 mmHg); πc has risen slightly → net reabsorption inward.
    • Lymphatic system returns the small net excess of filtrate (~3 L/day) back to circulation. Critical safety valve.
    • Causes of edema (mechanism → example):
      • ↑ Pc — heart failure, venous obstruction.
      • ↓ πc — low albumin (liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition).
      • ↑ permeability — inflammation, sepsis, burns.
      • Lymphatic obstruction — post-mastectomy lymphedema, filariasis.

    Summary

    ForceDirectionMagnitude
    Pc (capillary hydrostatic)Out of capillary~35 mmHg arteriolar → ~15 mmHg venular
    πc (capillary oncotic)Into capillary~25 mmHg (mostly albumin)
    Pi (interstitial hydrostatic)Into capillarySmall, near zero
    πi (interstitial oncotic)Out of capillarySmall, ~3 mmHg

    Diagram: Starling forces across a capillary

    Worked calculation

    1. Net filtration at the arteriolar end (healthy):
      • Pc = 35, πc = 25, Pi ≈ 0, πi ≈ 3.
      • NFP = (Pc − Pi) − (πc − πi) = (35 − 0) − (25 − 3) = 35 − 22 = +13 mmHg outward.
      • Interpretation: fluid leaves the capillary at the arteriolar end.
    2. Net filtration at the venular end (healthy):
      • Pc = 15, πc = 25, Pi ≈ 0, πi ≈ 3.
      • NFP = (15 − 0) − (25 − 3) = 15 − 22 = −7 mmHg (inward).
      • Interpretation: fluid returns to the capillary at the venular end.
    3. Heart failure causing pulmonary edema: Pc rises to 25 at the alveolar capillaries (normally ~7-10).
      • If πc remains 25, NFP = 25 − 22 = +3 mmHg outward across the whole length.
      • Interpretation: there is now no net reabsorption. Fluid accumulates in alveolar interstitium and floods the alveoli. This is acute pulmonary edema.
    4. Hypoalbuminemia (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome): πc falls to 15 (low albumin).
      • Arteriolar end NFP: (35) − (15 − 3) = 35 − 12 = +23 mmHg outward.
      • Venular end NFP: (15) − (15 − 3) = 15 − 12 = +3 mmHg outward (no reabsorption).
      • Interpretation: generalized edema and ascites. Nursing implication: monitor albumin trends; replacing volume with crystalloid in a low-albumin patient further dilutes πc.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pulmonary edema in left HF: LA pressure rises → pulmonary venous pressure rises → pulmonary capillary Pc rises → fluid filters into alveoli → crackles, dyspnea, hypoxemia. Peripheral edema in right HF: systemic venous pressure rises → leg capillary Pc rises → fluid filters into interstitium → ankle and shin edema. Low-albumin edema: πc falls, fluid balance shifts outward → ascites in cirrhosis, generalized edema in nephrotic syndrome.

    Blood pressure regulation: short-term and long-term P

    BP equals CO times TPR. Short-term, the baroreflex moves both within seconds. Long-term, the kidney owns BP through volume control with RAAS at the center.

    Notes

    • BP = CO × TPR.
    • MAP estimate = DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP). Normal MAP 70-100 mmHg.
    • Short-term regulation (seconds to minutes):
      • Baroreceptors: carotid sinus (CN IX) and aortic arch (CN X). Stretch receptors. Send afferent signals to the cardiovascular center in the medulla.
      • BP ↑ → baroreceptors fire more → medulla ↑ vagal, ↓ sympathetic → HR ↓, contractility ↓, vasodilation.
      • BP ↓ → baroreceptors fire less → ↑ sympathetic, ↓ vagal → HR ↑, contractility ↑, vasoconstriction.
      • Chemoreceptors: peripheral (carotid and aortic bodies) and central (medulla). Respond to ↓ O₂, ↑ CO₂, ↓ pH. Contribute to BP support in extremes.
    • Long-term regulation (hours to days): RAAS.
      • Renin from juxtaglomerular cells in the kidney (triggered by low renal perfusion, ↑ sympathetic, ↓ Na+ to macula densa).
      • Renin cleaves angiotensinogen (from liver) → angiotensin I.
      • ACE (mostly pulmonary endothelium) converts AT-I → angiotensin II.
      • AT-II: potent vasoconstrictor; ↑ aldosterone from adrenal cortex; ↑ ADH from posterior pituitary; ↑ thirst; ↑ proximal tubule Na+ reabsorption.
      • Aldosterone: principal cells of cortical collecting duct reabsorb Na+ (water follows), secrete K+ and H+.
    • ADH (vasopressin): water reabsorption at collecting duct (V2 receptors) + vasoconstriction (V1).
    • ANP / BNP: released from atrial and ventricular myocytes when stretched. Promote Na+ and water loss, vasodilation. Counterbalance to RAAS.

    Baroreflex (vagal side)SecondsLowers BP—

    Worked calculations: MAP, pulse pressure, hemodynamics

    1. MAP (mean arterial pressure): Patient BP 120/80.
      • MAP = DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP) = 80 + ⅓(120 − 80) = 80 + 13.3 = 93 mmHg.
      • Interpretation: normal (70-100). MAP < 65 is the typical perfusion threshold for organ failure risk in shock.
    2. Pulse pressure: Same patient.
      • PP = SBP − DBP = 120 − 80 = 40 mmHg.
      • Interpretation: normal. Reflects stroke volume and arterial compliance. Wide PP (≥ 60) suggests aortic regurgitation, hyperthyroidism, or stiff arteries (older adults). Narrow PP (≤ 25) suggests cardiogenic shock, cardiac tamponade, or severe AS.
    3. Shock case: ICU patient. BP 78/52, HR 118. Compute MAP and PP. Interpret.
      • MAP = 52 + ⅓(78 − 52) = 52 + 8.7 = ~61 mmHg.
      • PP = 78 − 52 = 26 mmHg (narrow).
      • Interpretation: MAP below 65 → inadequate perfusion. Narrow PP with hypotension and tachycardia suggests low CO. Suspect cardiogenic or hypovolemic shock.
    4. Vascular resistance (clinical estimate): SVR = 80 × (MAP − CVP) / CO, where SVR is in dyn·s·cm⁻⁵ and CVP is central venous pressure. Patient: MAP 60, CVP 4, CO 4 L/min.
      • SVR = 80 × (60 − 4) / 4 = 80 × 14 = 1,120 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵.
      • Interpretation: normal range 800-1,200. If SVR were < 800 with low MAP, suspect distributive (septic) shock. If SVR > 1,400 with low CO, suspect cardiogenic shock.
    5. Poiseuille's law: A vasodilator doubles the arteriolar radius. By what factor does resistance change?
      • R ∝ 1/r⁴, so doubling r reduces R by factor of 2⁴ = 16×.
      • Interpretation: small changes in arteriolar diameter produce massive changes in resistance. This is why arteriolar tone is the master regulator of TPR.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Antihypertensive drug classes map cleanly onto this physiology:

    • ACE inhibitors / ARBs: block AT-II → vasodilation, less aldosterone.
    • Beta blockers: block β1 → lower HR, contractility, and renin release.
    • Calcium channel blockers: arteriolar vasodilation (dihydropyridines) or also rate control (non-dihydropyridines).
    • Diuretics: ↓ volume → ↓ preload → ↓ CO → ↓ BP. Loop, thiazide, K+-sparing.
    • Alpha blockers: block α1 → vasodilation.

    Common vascular conditions CLIN

    Most vascular pathology is one of three problems: the pipe is too narrow (occlusion or stenosis), the pipe is too wide or weak (aneurysm), or fluid is in the wrong compartment (edema or shock).

    Notes

    • Hypertension:
      • Primary (essential, ~95%): no identifiable cause.
      • Secondary (~5%): renovascular disease, primary aldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, OSA, drugs.
      • Stages (current guidelines): elevated 120-129/<80, stage 1 130-139/80-89, stage 2 ≥140/≥90.
    • Atherosclerosis: intimal plaque from chronic injury and lipid accumulation. Hard plaques narrow lumen → stable angina, PAD claudication. Soft plaques rupture → acute thrombosis (MI, stroke).
    • Aneurysm: abnormal dilation. AAA is the most common (infrarenal aorta). Risk factors: HTN, smoking, atherosclerosis, genetic disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos). Rupture is catastrophic.
    • Aortic dissection: tear in the intima, blood enters the media, splitting the wall. Tearing chest or back pain, BP difference between arms. Emergency.
    • Peripheral artery disease (PAD): atherosclerosis in limb arteries. Intermittent claudication, cool/pale limb, weak pulses, ulcers that do not heal.
    • DVT and PE: Virchow's triad — stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability. DVT in deep leg veins; PE if a clot embolizes to the pulmonary arteries.
    • Shock (four classic categories):
      • Hypovolemic: low volume (hemorrhage, severe dehydration).
      • Cardiogenic: pump failure (MI, severe HF, arrhythmias).
      • Distributive: low TPR (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic).
      • Obstructive: blocked flow (massive PE, tamponade, tension pneumothorax).
