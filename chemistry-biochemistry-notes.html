Chemistry & Biochemistry

All physiology is chemistry happening fast. Atoms make molecules; molecules make biomolecules; biomolecules make life. DNA stores the instructions, transcription rewrites them as mRNA, and translation builds the proteins that do nearly all the work.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 1 of 7

    Atoms, bonds, chemical reactions P

    Everything is made of atoms. Bonds are how atoms share or trade electrons. Reactions are how bonds break, form, or rearrange.

    Notes

    • Atom: protons (+) and neutrons in nucleus; electrons (−) orbit in shells.
      • Atomic number = protons.
      • Atomic mass = protons + neutrons.
      • Isotope: same element, different neutron count.
      • Ion: atom with unequal protons and electrons → net charge.
    • Major body elements (CHNOPS): C, H, N, O, P, S → ~96% of mass. Trace: Ca, P, K, S, Na, Cl, Mg, Fe, I, Zn, Cu, Mn, Co, Cr, Se, Mo, F.
    • Three chemical bonds:
      • Ionic: electrons transferred → cation (+) + anion (−). Strong in solid, dissociate in water (NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻).
      • Covalent: electrons shared.
        • Nonpolar: equal sharing (C-H in lipids).
        • Polar: unequal sharing → partial charges (O-H in water).
      • Hydrogen bond: weak attraction between slightly + H and slightly − atom (often O or N). Critical for water, DNA base pairing, protein folding.
    • Chemical reactions:
      • Synthesis (anabolic): A + B → AB. Builds. Uses energy.
      • Decomposition (catabolic): AB → A + B. Breaks down. Releases energy.
      • Exchange: AB + CD → AC + BD.
      • Reversible: A + B ⇌ AB (depends on conditions).
      • Oxidation-reduction (redox): electron transfer. Oxidation = lose electrons; reduction = gain electrons. Core of cellular respiration.
    • Reaction rate factors:
      • ↑ Temperature → ↑ rate (until protein denaturation).
      • ↑ Concentration → ↑ rate.
      • Catalyst (enzyme in biology) → ↓ activation energy → ↑ rate without being consumed.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What type of bond holds the two strands of DNA together?
    2. DOK 3. Why does very high fever (> 41 °C) become dangerous?
    Show answers
    1. Hydrogen bonds (between complementary bases A-T and C-G).
    2. High temperature disrupts the weak interactions holding protein structure together → enzymes and other proteins denature → cellular machinery fails. The same heat sensitivity that lets us cook eggs damages our own proteins above safe limits.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 2 of 7

    Water, pH, and buffers P

    The body is ~60% water. Water's polarity makes it a universal solvent and gives it thermal properties that protect us. pH determines how proteins fold and how reactions proceed.

    Notes

    • Water properties (and why each matters):
      • Polar molecule: universal solvent for ions and polar molecules.
      • High specific heat: resists temperature change → thermal stability.
      • High heat of vaporization: sweat evaporation cools effectively.
      • Cohesion + surface tension: blood flow through capillaries.
      • Cushion: CSF, amniotic fluid, synovial fluid.
      • Reactant: hydrolysis breaks bonds with water; dehydration synthesis forms bonds, releases water.
    • Solutions, mixtures, suspensions:
      • Solution: homogeneous mixture (saline).
      • Colloid: particles large enough to scatter light but stay suspended (cytoplasm, blood plasma proteins).
      • Suspension: large particles settle out (RBCs in plasma).
    • pH:
      • pH = −log[H⁺]. Each unit = 10× change.
      • Acid donates H⁺; base accepts H⁺.
      • Pure water: pH 7 (neutral).
      • Blood pH: 7.35-7.45 (slightly alkaline). Outside ~6.8-8.0 incompatible with life.
      • Stomach pH: 1-2 (very acidic).
      • Urine pH: 4.5-8.0 (variable).
    • Buffers: resist pH change by absorbing or releasing H⁺.
      • Bicarbonate (extracellular, regulated by lungs + kidneys): CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Main blood buffer.
      • Protein (intracellular + plasma): hemoglobin (huge buffer capacity), albumin. Histidine residues are the main acceptors.
      • Phosphate (intracellular + urinary): HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ H₂PO₄⁻.
      • Ammonia (urinary): NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ — kidney's way to excrete H⁺.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Acid-base disorders are introduced in renal Concept 13 and respiratory Concept 11. Briefly: respiratory acidosis (↑ CO₂ from hypoventilation), respiratory alkalosis (↓ CO₂ from hyperventilation), metabolic acidosis (↓ HCO₃⁻ from DKA, sepsis, diarrhea, kidney failure), metabolic alkalosis (↑ HCO₃⁻ from vomiting, diuretics).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which buffer system is the main one for blood pH?
    2. DOK 2. A pH drop from 7.4 to 6.4 represents how many times more H⁺?
    Show answers
    1. Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻/CO₂), regulated by both lungs (CO₂) and kidneys (HCO₃⁻).
    2. 10× more H⁺ (each pH unit = 10-fold change). pH is logarithmic.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 3 of 7

    Carbohydrates and lipids P

    Carbs are fast energy. Lipids are stored energy plus structural and signaling molecules. Both built from carbon backbones; both broken down to ATP.

    Notes

    • Carbohydrates:
      • Monosaccharides: simple sugars. Glucose (universal fuel), fructose, galactose.
      • Disaccharides: sucrose (glucose-fructose), lactose (glucose-galactose), maltose (glucose-glucose).
      • Polysaccharides:
        • Glycogen: animal storage form. In liver + muscle. ↑ by insulin.
        • Starch: plant storage (food source).
        • Cellulose: plant cell walls; indigestible to humans (fiber).
      • Function: ATP fuel (preferred by brain, RBC), short-term energy storage as glycogen, structural (glycoproteins on cell surfaces).
    • Lipids:
      • Triglycerides (most abundant): glycerol + 3 fatty acids. ~9 kcal/g (vs 4 for carbs/protein). Long-term energy storage in adipose tissue.
        • Saturated: no double bonds, solid at room temp, animal sources. Linked to ↑ LDL.
        • Unsaturated: one or more double bonds, liquid at room temp, plant sources.
        • Trans fats: industrial hydrogenation; strongly linked to CV disease.
        • Essential fatty acids (must eat): omega-3, omega-6.
      • Phospholipids: glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group. Amphipathic (polar head + nonpolar tails) → form bilayers of cell membranes.
      • Steroids: four-ring structure. Cholesterol is the parent.
        • Cholesterol: cell membrane component, precursor to all steroid hormones, bile salts, vitamin D.
        • Steroid hormones: cortisol, aldosterone, estrogens, androgens, vitamin D.
      • Eicosanoids: signaling lipids from arachidonic acid.
        • Prostaglandins: inflammation, pain, fever (NSAIDs block COX → ↓ prostaglandins).
        • Leukotrienes: bronchoconstriction (asthma).
        • Thromboxanes: platelet aggregation.
      • Fat-soluble vitamins: A, D, E, K. Absorbed with dietary fat.
    • Metabolic overview:
      • Glucose breakdown: glycolysis → pyruvate → acetyl CoA → Krebs (citric acid) cycle → electron transport chain → ~30-32 ATP per glucose aerobic; only 2 ATP anaerobic.
      • Fatty acid breakdown: β-oxidation → acetyl CoA → Krebs/ETC. Much more ATP per gram than carbs.
      • In starvation: ketone bodies from fatty acids fuel the brain (which can't use fat directly).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Statins block HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ cholesterol synthesis → ↓ LDL → ↓ CV events. NSAIDs block COX → ↓ prostaglandins. Side effects (GI ulcers, kidney injury) reflect the loss of protective prostaglandins. Aspirin's antiplatelet effect: irreversibly inhibits COX-1 in platelets → ↓ thromboxane → ↓ platelet aggregation for the platelet's lifespan (~7-10 days).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which biomolecule provides the most kcal per gram?
    2. DOK 3. Why is the brain primarily glucose-dependent but can use ketones during starvation?
    Show answers
    1. Lipids (~9 kcal/g vs ~4 for carbs and protein).
    2. Fatty acids can't cross the blood-brain barrier directly. During prolonged fasting, the liver converts fatty acids to ketone bodies (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate), which DO cross the BBB and can be metabolized by neurons. This is why ketogenic diets affect the brain.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 4 of 7

    Proteins, enzymes, and protein structure P

    Proteins do nearly everything in cells. Twenty amino acids combine in sequences that fold into precise 3D shapes. Shape determines function; loss of shape (denaturation) is loss of function.

    Notes

    • Amino acids: 20 types in humans. 9 essential (cannot synthesize). Each has the same backbone + a unique R-group (side chain) that gives properties.
    • Peptide bond: formed between amino acids by dehydration synthesis.
      • Dipeptide (2 AAs), oligopeptide (3-20), polypeptide (> 20). "Protein" usually means a functional polypeptide.
    • Four levels of protein structure:
      • Primary: linear sequence of amino acids (coded by gene).
      • Secondary: local folding by backbone H-bonds. Alpha helix, beta sheet.
      • Tertiary: overall 3D shape from R-group interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic clustering).
      • Quaternary: assembly of multiple polypeptide chains. Examples: hemoglobin (4 chains), antibodies (4 chains).
    • Denaturation: loss of shape (and function) without breaking peptide bonds.
      • Triggers: heat, extreme pH, heavy metals, chemicals.
      • Sometimes reversible if mild; usually irreversible if extreme.
      • Examples: cooking egg whites (clear → opaque), enzyme inactivation in severe fever, gastric acid denaturing dietary proteins for digestion.
    • Protein functions (huge variety):
      • Enzymes (catalyze reactions).
      • Structural (collagen, keratin).
      • Transport (hemoglobin, albumin, membrane carriers).
      • Motor (actin, myosin in muscle; dynein, kinesin in cytoskeleton).
      • Immune (antibodies, complement, MHC).
      • Hormones (insulin, GH, glucagon, PTH).
      • Receptors (membrane and intracellular).
      • Buffers (hemoglobin, albumin in blood).
      • Storage (ferritin for iron).
    • Enzymes:
      • Biological catalysts. ↓ activation energy without being consumed.
      • Substrate-specific (lock-and-key or induced fit).
      • Active site = where substrate binds and reaction happens.
      • Cofactors (often minerals: Zn, Mg, Fe) or coenzymes (often vitamins: B-complex) required by many.
      • Naming: usually ends in -ase (lactase, amylase, lipase, dehydrogenase).
      • Regulation:
        • Substrate concentration.
        • Allosteric activators / inhibitors (bind non-active site).
        • Competitive inhibitors (compete with substrate at active site).
        • Covalent modification (phosphorylation by kinases; reversed by phosphatases).
        • Feedback inhibition (end product inhibits earlier enzyme).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors: ACE inhibitors (block angiotensin converting enzyme), statins (block HMG-CoA reductase), PPIs (block H⁺/K⁺ ATPase), AChE inhibitors (block acetylcholinesterase), aspirin (blocks COX). Inborn errors of metabolism are enzyme deficiencies — PKU (phenylalanine hydroxylase), galactosemia (galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase), Tay-Sachs (hexosaminidase A). Newborn screens detect many.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which level of protein structure refers to the linear amino acid sequence?
    2. DOK 3. Why does denaturation cause loss of function but not loss of primary structure?
    Show answers
    1. Primary structure.
    2. Denaturation disrupts the weak interactions (H-bonds, ionic, hydrophobic) holding higher-order structure, but does not break the strong peptide bonds of the primary sequence. The amino acid order is preserved; the 3D shape is lost. Sometimes the protein can refold if conditions normalize.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 5 of 7

    Nucleic acids: DNA structure and replication P

    DNA stores every instruction the cell needs. Two strands wound into a double helix, paired by base complementarity. Replication is how cells copy the instructions before they divide.

    Notes

    • Nucleotide structure:
      • Phosphate group + 5-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) + nitrogenous base.
      • Five bases total:
        • Purines (double ring): Adenine (A), Guanine (G).
        • Pyrimidines (single ring): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T) in DNA only, Uracil (U) in RNA only.
    • DNA structure:
      • Double helix discovered by Watson, Crick, Franklin (1953).
      • Two antiparallel strands held by H-bonds between paired bases.
      • Base pairing rules: A-T (two H-bonds), C-G (three H-bonds).
      • Sugar-phosphate backbone on outside; bases on inside.
      • Each strand has direction: 5' to 3'.
    • DNA in the cell:
      • Wrapped around histone proteins → nucleosomes → chromatin → chromosomes.
      • 23 pairs of chromosomes in human somatic cells (46 total): 22 autosomal + 1 sex (XX or XY).
      • Each cell has ~3 billion base pairs of DNA, ~20,000 protein-coding genes.
    • DNA replication (during S phase of cell cycle):
      1. DNA helicase unwinds the double helix → "replication fork."
      2. Single-strand binding proteins keep strands apart.
      3. DNA polymerase reads the template strand 3'→5' and synthesizes the new strand 5'→3' by adding complementary nucleotides.
      4. Leading strand: synthesized continuously toward the fork.
      5. Lagging strand: synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments → joined by DNA ligase.
      6. Result: two identical daughter DNA molecules, each with one old and one new strand (semi-conservative).
    • Proofreading: DNA polymerase corrects mismatches as it goes. Additional repair enzymes fix errors after the fact. Error rate ~1 per 10⁹ bases.
    • Telomeres: repetitive sequences at chromosome ends that shorten with each division (because lagging strand synthesis can't fully copy the very end). Stem cells and germ cells use telomerase to maintain them; most somatic cells don't, contributing to cellular aging.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Chemotherapy often targets DNA replication: alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide), antimetabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU), topoisomerase inhibitors. Rapidly dividing cells (tumor + bone marrow + GI mucosa + hair follicles) are most affected → explains the side effects. UV radiation damages DNA (forms thymine dimers). Defective repair → xeroderma pigmentosum → severe sun sensitivity and skin cancer risk.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What base pairs with adenine in DNA?
    2. DOK 3. Why is DNA replication called "semi-conservative"?
    Show answers
    1. Thymine (A-T, two H-bonds). In RNA, adenine pairs with uracil.
    2. Each daughter DNA molecule has one original ("conserved") strand and one newly synthesized strand. "Semi-conservative" means half of each daughter comes from the parent.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 6 of 7

    Transcription and translation: DNA → mRNA → protein P

    A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein. Transcription rewrites the gene as mRNA. Translation reads the mRNA in three-letter codons and assembles the protein. The central dogma in two steps.

    Notes

    • Central dogma: DNA → mRNA → protein. Information flows from genome to working machinery.
    • Three types of RNA:
      • mRNA (messenger): carries the protein-coding message from nucleus to cytoplasm.
      • tRNA (transfer): brings amino acids to the ribosome. Each tRNA has an anticodon matching a specific codon.
      • rRNA (ribosomal): structural + catalytic core of the ribosome.
      • Plus miRNAs and others (regulation).
    • Transcription (in nucleus):
      1. RNA polymerase binds promoter region on DNA.
      2. DNA unwinds at the gene.
      3. RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3'→5' and synthesizes mRNA 5'→3', adding complementary RNA nucleotides (A-U, T-A, C-G, G-C).
      4. Termination at stop sequence.
      5. Pre-mRNA processing (in eukaryotes):
        • 5' cap added.
        • 3' poly-A tail added.
        • Introns spliced out; exons joined together.
      6. Mature mRNA exits nucleus through nuclear pores.
    • Genetic code:
      • Triplet code: each codon = 3 bases = 1 amino acid.
      • 64 possible codons → code for 20 amino acids + start + stop signals.
      • Redundant (degenerate): most amino acids have > 1 codon.
      • Start codon: AUG (codes for methionine).
      • Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA (no AA; terminate translation).
      • Universal: nearly the same code across all life forms.
    • Translation (in cytoplasm, at ribosome):
      1. Initiation: small ribosomal subunit binds mRNA's 5' cap, scans to start codon (AUG). Initiator tRNA (carrying methionine) binds. Large subunit joins.
      2. Elongation: ribosome moves along mRNA codon by codon. For each codon: matching tRNA brings AA → peptide bond forms with growing chain → ribosome shifts to next codon → empty tRNA released.
      3. Termination: stop codon reached → release factor binds → polypeptide released. Ribosomal subunits dissociate.
    • Post-translational modifications:
      • Folding (sometimes with chaperone proteins).
      • Cleavage (e.g., proinsulin → insulin).
      • Glycosylation, phosphorylation, methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination.
      • Targeting to specific destinations (nucleus, mitochondrion, membrane, secretion).
    • Mutations:
      • Silent: different codon, same AA → no effect (because of code redundancy).
      • Missense: different codon, different AA → may or may not affect function.
      • Nonsense: codon → stop → truncated protein.
      • Frameshift: insertion/deletion not a multiple of 3 → all downstream codons read incorrectly. Usually catastrophic.
      • Causes: spontaneous (replication errors), radiation, chemicals (mutagens), viruses.
    • Regulation of gene expression:
      • Most regulation is at the level of transcription (which genes are read).
      • Transcription factors bind regulatory DNA regions and turn genes on or off.
      • Steroid hormones, thyroid hormone, vitamin D all act as transcription factor regulators.
      • Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone modification) silence or activate regions without changing the DNA sequence.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Antibiotics that target translation: aminoglycosides (gentamicin), tetracyclines, macrolides (azithromycin), chloramphenicol, linezolid. They exploit differences between bacterial and human ribosomes. Side effect: side toxicity from mitochondrial ribosomes (which resemble bacterial ones) explains some adverse effects. mRNA vaccines (COVID-19): deliver synthetic mRNA encoding viral spike protein → host ribosomes translate it → immune response. The mRNA is degraded within days; doesn't enter the nucleus or alter DNA. Sickle cell disease: single missense mutation (Glu → Val) in β-globin. One base change, lifetime of consequences.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What is the start codon, and which amino acid does it code for?
    2. DOK 4. A frameshift mutation in the middle of a gene is usually more severe than a missense mutation. Why?
    Show answers
    1. AUG, methionine (Met).
    2. A frameshift (insertion or deletion of a non-multiple-of-3 number of bases) shifts the reading frame for every codon downstream. All amino acids after the mutation are wrong, and the new sequence often hits a premature stop codon → truncated nonfunctional protein. A missense changes one AA but preserves the reading frame; the rest of the protein is correctly sequenced.

    Chemistry & Biochemistry · Concept 7 of 7

    ATP, cellular metabolism, and bioenergetics P

    ATP is the universal energy currency. Every cell makes it and spends it constantly. Three pathways generate ATP at different speeds and yields: substrate-level phosphorylation, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation.

    Notes

    • ATP (adenosine triphosphate):
      • Adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates.
      • Bond between 2nd and 3rd phosphates stores ~7.3 kcal/mol when broken.
      • ATP → ADP + Pi releases energy used for cellular work (active transport, mechanical work, biosynthesis).
      • ADP + Pi → ATP requires energy input from metabolism.
      • Each cell turns over its weight in ATP every day — it's not stored, it's continuously regenerated.
    • Three ATP-producing pathways:
      1. Substrate-level phosphorylation (during glycolysis + Krebs): direct transfer of phosphate from a substrate to ADP. Small contribution; doesn't need oxygen.
      2. Oxidative phosphorylation (in mitochondria): the major source. Electron transport chain + ATP synthase using O₂ as final electron acceptor. ~26-28 ATP per glucose.
      3. Photophosphorylation: plants only (photosynthesis). Not in humans.
    • Glucose breakdown — three stages:
      1. Glycolysis (cytoplasm):
        • Glucose → 2 pyruvate.
        • Net: 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
        • Anaerobic capable.
        • If no O₂: pyruvate → lactate (lactic acid fermentation in muscle, RBC).
      2. Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA (mitochondrial matrix):
        • Each pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH (per pyruvate).
        • Per glucose: 2 acetyl-CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH.
      3. Krebs (citric acid) cycle (mitochondrial matrix):
        • Each acetyl-CoA → 2 CO₂ + 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP (≈ATP).
        • Per glucose: 4 CO₂ + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 GTP.
      4. Electron transport chain (ETC) (inner mitochondrial membrane):
        • NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons → carried through complexes I-IV.
        • Energy used to pump H⁺ across membrane → proton gradient.
        • O₂ is final electron acceptor → forms H₂O.
        • H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase → drives ATP synthesis.
        • Total ATP yield from one glucose: ~30-32 ATP (aerobic). Only 2 ATP if anaerobic.
    • Other fuels:
      • Fatty acids: β-oxidation (mitochondria) → acetyl-CoA → Krebs/ETC. Much higher ATP yield per gram.
      • Amino acids: can feed into Krebs cycle at various points (after deamination → urea synthesis to dispose of nitrogen).
      • Ketone bodies: liver-made from fatty acids during fasting; brain can use them.
    • Anabolism vs catabolism:
      • Catabolism: breaks molecules → releases energy → captures it as ATP.
      • Anabolism: uses ATP to build complex molecules.
      • Hormones tilt the balance: insulin → anabolic; glucagon, cortisol, epinephrine, GH → catabolic.
    • Metabolic rate:
      • BMR (basal metabolic rate): energy used at rest. Influenced by thyroid hormone, body size, age, sex, lean mass.
      • Total energy expenditure = BMR + thermic effect of food + physical activity.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Cyanide poisoning inhibits cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV of ETC) → cells can't use oxygen → cellular asphyxiation despite normal O₂ delivery. Treat with hydroxocobalamin or nitrites + thiosulfate. Carbon monoxide also impairs cellular respiration but mainly by binding hemoglobin and reducing O₂ delivery. Mitochondrial diseases (often maternally inherited) affect high-energy tissues — muscle (weakness), brain (encephalopathy), heart (cardiomyopathy). Metformin partially inhibits mitochondrial complex I → ↓ hepatic glucose output, helpful in T2DM; rare risk of lactic acidosis.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Where in the cell does glycolysis occur?
    2. DOK 4. Cyanide blocks Complex IV of the ETC. Explain why this is so rapidly lethal.
    Show answers
    1. Cytoplasm (cytosol). Subsequent stages happen in mitochondria.
    2. Without functional Complex IV, the ETC stops. Cells cannot produce ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. ATP demand is constant and stores are minimal — within minutes the heart and brain run out of energy and fail. Anaerobic glycolysis alone (2 ATP per glucose) cannot meet metabolic demand. Death follows quickly.
