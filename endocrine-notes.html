    Endocrine · Concept 1 of 13

    Endocrine vs nervous + hormone classes P

    Both the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate the body. The nervous system is fast and targeted; the endocrine system is slower but persistent and broadcast. Hormones group into three structural classes that determine how they travel and signal.

    Notes

    • Endocrine vs nervous comparison:
      • Speed: nervous (ms) >> endocrine (sec to days).
      • Specificity: nervous targets specific cells via synapses; endocrine reaches all cells via blood, with specificity by receptor expression.
      • Duration: nervous is brief; endocrine effects can last hours to days.
      • Overlap: many tissues use both (e.g., adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion).
    • Three hormone classes:
      • Peptide / protein: insulin, GH, ADH, oxytocin, most pituitary. Water soluble → travel free in plasma. Bind membrane receptors → second messenger cascades. Stored in vesicles, released on demand.
      • Steroid: cortisol, aldosterone, sex hormones, vitamin D. Lipid soluble → travel bound to carrier proteins. Cross cell membrane → bind intracellular receptors → modulate gene transcription. Synthesized on demand, not stored.
      • Amine: derived from tyrosine.
        • Catecholamines (E, NE, DA): water-soluble, like peptides — bind membrane receptors.
        • Thyroid hormones (T3, T4): lipid-soluble — bind intracellular receptors.
    • Hormone life cycle: synthesis → storage (peptides) or on-demand (steroids) → release → transport (free or bound) → bind receptor → effect → degradation (liver, kidney).

    Summary

    ClassSolubilityReceptorStorage
    Peptide / proteinWaterMembrane (2nd messenger)Vesicles
    SteroidLipidIntracellular (gene transcription)None (made on demand)
    Amine — catecholWaterMembraneVesicles
    Amine — thyroidLipidIntracellularColloid (T3/T4 bound to thyroglobulin)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pharmacology consequence: peptides (e.g., insulin, GH, ADH analogs) are destroyed by gut digestion → must be injected. Steroids (e.g., prednisone, levothyroxine for T4) can be oral. Onset and duration follow receptor mechanism: catecholamines act in seconds, steroids and thyroid hormones take hours to days to reach full effect because they work via transcription.

    Endocrine · Concept 2 of 13

    Hormone control: humoral, neural, hormonal + receptor regulation P

    Hormone secretion is triggered by one of three signals: a blood substance (humoral), a nerve (neural), or another hormone (hormonal). Receptors are not fixed; cells up- or down-regulate them to dampen or amplify response.

    Notes

    • Three stimulus types:
      • Humoral: blood ion or nutrient level. Example: ↓ Ca²⁺ → parathyroid releases PTH.
      • Neural: direct nerve stimulation. Example: sympathetic preganglionic fiber → adrenal medulla → E + NE.
      • Hormonal (tropic): hormone from one gland controls another. Example: TSH from anterior pituitary → thyroid → T3/T4.
    • Feedback loops:
      • Negative (the workhorse): hormone effect ↓ stimulus that triggered it. Stabilizes.
      • Positive (rare): hormone effect ↑ stimulus → escalates until event completes. Examples: oxytocin in labor, LH surge in ovulation, clotting cascade.
    • Receptor regulation:
      • Down-regulation: chronic high hormone → cell internalizes receptors → response weakens. Insulin resistance in T2DM is the classic example.
      • Up-regulation: chronic low hormone → more receptors expressed → response amplifies. Denervation supersensitivity. Beta-blocker withdrawal hypersensitivity.
    • Half-life sets dosing: peptides minutes (insulin: 5-7 min); catecholamines seconds; thyroid hormones days (T4: ~7 days, why levothyroxine is dosed once daily).

    Summary

    TriggerExampleSpeed
    Humoral (blood substance)↓ Ca²⁺ → PTH; ↑ glucose → insulinDirect, fast
    NeuralSympathetic → adrenal medulla (E/NE)Seconds
    Hormonal (tropic)TSH → thyroid; ACTH → adrenal cortexMinutes to hours

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Type 2 diabetes mellitus is built on receptor down-regulation: chronic hyperinsulinemia → fewer functional insulin receptors → ever-worsening glucose handling. Abrupt β-blocker discontinuation can cause rebound HTN and tachycardia because chronically blocked β receptors up-regulate.

    Endocrine · Concept 3 of 13

    Hypothalamic-pituitary axis A&P

    The pituitary is the relay between the brain and most peripheral endocrine organs. The anterior lobe is glandular and controlled chemically via a portal blood system. The posterior lobe is neural and controlled by axons descending from the hypothalamus.

    Notes

    • Anatomic setup:
      • Pituitary sits in the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone, attached to the hypothalamus by the infundibulum.
      • Anterior lobe (adenohypophysis): derived from oral ectoderm (Rathke's pouch). Glandular epithelial.
      • Posterior lobe (neurohypophysis): derived from neuroectoderm. Axons + supporting cells (pituicytes).
    • Anterior pituitary control: the hypophyseal portal system.
      • Hypothalamic neurons release "releasing hormones" (or inhibiting hormones) into a capillary plexus in the median eminence.
      • Portal veins carry them down to the anterior pituitary.
      • Anterior pituitary cells (acidophils, basophils) release their hormones into systemic circulation.
    • Posterior pituitary control: direct neural.
      • Cell bodies of supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei in the hypothalamus synthesize ADH and oxytocin.
      • Axons descend through the infundibulum into the posterior lobe.
      • Hormones are stored at axon terminals (Herring bodies) and released directly into blood.
    • Hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones (six pairs, most are stimulating):
      • TRH → TSH (and PRL).
      • CRH → ACTH.
      • GnRH → FSH + LH.
      • GHRH → GH (somatostatin inhibits).
      • Dopamine: tonic inhibitor of prolactin.

    Diagram: hypothalamic-pituitary connections

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pituitary adenomas can be functioning (most commonly prolactinomas → galactorrhea, infertility) or non-functioning (mass effect → bitemporal hemianopia from optic chiasm compression, headache). Sheehan syndrome: post-partum hemorrhage → pituitary infarction → hypopituitarism (failure to lactate is often the first clue).

    Endocrine · Concept 4 of 13

    Anterior pituitary hormones P

    Six hormones from six cell types. Most are tropic (control other glands). GH and PRL act directly on tissues. Each pairs with a hypothalamic releaser, an effect, and a clinical disorder of excess or deficiency.

    Notes

    • GH (growth hormone) from somatotrophs (acidophils):
      • ↑ Bone & tissue growth (via IGF-1 from liver). Anabolic.
      • Hyperglycemic (insulin antagonist).
      • Excess: gigantism (before puberty), acromegaly (after). Deficiency: short stature in children.
    • PRL (prolactin) from lactotrophs (acidophils):
      • Stimulates milk production. Tonically inhibited by dopamine.
      • Excess: galactorrhea, amenorrhea, infertility (suppresses GnRH). Dopamine agonists (cabergoline, bromocriptine) treat prolactinoma.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) from thyrotrophs (basophils):
      • Stimulates thyroid → T3, T4.
      • Used to differentiate primary vs secondary thyroid disorders.
    • ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) from corticotrophs (basophils):
      • Stimulates adrenal cortex (cortisol primarily). Mild aldosterone and androgen effects.
      • Cushing disease = pituitary ACTH adenoma.
    • FSH, LH (gonadotrophs, basophils):
      • FSH: stimulates follicle development (♀) / spermatogenesis (♂).
      • LH: triggers ovulation, corpus luteum (♀) / testosterone production by Leydig cells (♂).
    • MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone): from POMC cleavage, same precursor as ACTH. Hyperpigmentation in primary adrenal failure (high ACTH/MSH precursor) — Addison's "bronze skin."

    Summary

    HormoneTargetExcessDeficiency
    GHLiver (IGF-1), tissuesGigantism, acromegalyShort stature
    PRLBreastGalactorrhea, infertilityFailure to lactate (Sheehan)
    TSHThyroidSecondary hyperthyroid (rare)Secondary hypothyroid
    ACTHAdrenal cortexCushing diseaseSecondary adrenal insufficiency
    FSH/LHGonadsPrecocious pubertyHypogonadism, infertility

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Acromegaly in adults: enlarging hands/feet/jaw, deepening voice, glucose intolerance, sleep apnea, cardiomyopathy. GH suppression test with oral glucose challenge is diagnostic. Stalk effect: any pituitary stalk damage disrupts dopamine delivery → PRL rises (lost inhibition) while other hormones fall (lost releasing hormones).

    Quick check

    2. DOK 3. A patient with a pituitary stalk lesion has ↑ prolactin and ↓ TSH. Explain.
    Show answers
    1. Dopamine.
    2. Stalk damage interrupts hypothalamic-to-pituitary signaling. Dopamine cannot reach lactotrophs → PRL rises (disinhibition). TRH and other releasing hormones also cannot reach their targets → TSH falls (loss of stimulation).

    Endocrine · Concept 5 of 13

    Posterior pituitary hormones: ADH and oxytocin P

    The posterior pituitary stores two hormones made in the hypothalamus: ADH controls water, oxytocin drives labor and milk let-down.

    Notes

    • ADH (vasopressin):
      • Made in supraoptic nucleus.
      • Stimuli: ↑ plasma osmolarity, ↓ blood volume/BP, nausea, pain, opioids.
      • V2 receptors at collecting duct → aquaporin-2 insertion → ↑ water reabsorption.
      • V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction (at high levels, in shock).
      • Disorders: SIADH (excess), central DI (deficient), nephrogenic DI (kidney resistance). Detailed in renal notes Concept 7.
    • Oxytocin:
      • Made in paraventricular nucleus.
      • Stimuli: uterine stretch, infant suckling.
      • Effects: uterine smooth muscle contraction (labor — positive feedback), milk let-down (myoepithelial cells in alveoli of breast).
      • Clinical use: synthetic oxytocin (Pitocin) for labor induction; postpartum hemorrhage prevention.
      • Also implicated in social bonding behaviors.

    Summary

    HormoneMade inStimulusEffect
    ADHSupraoptic nucleus↑ Osm, ↓ volumeWater reabsorption
    OxytocinParaventricular nucleusStretch, sucklingUterine contraction, milk let-down

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Oxytocin's role in labor is the classic example of positive feedback in human physiology: uterine stretch → oxytocin release → uterine contraction → more stretch → more oxytocin → escalation until delivery. The loop terminates when the baby is out.

    Show answers
    1. Oxytocin (Pitocin).
    2. In the hypothalamus (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei). They are stored in the posterior pituitary at axon terminals.

    Endocrine · Concept 6 of 13

    Thyroid: structure, T3/T4, calcitonin A&P

    The thyroid sets metabolic rate. T4 is the storage form; T3 is the active form. Calcitonin is a side gig — modest role in calcium handling in humans.

    Notes

    • Anatomy:
      • Butterfly-shaped, in anterior neck inferior to larynx. Two lobes connected by isthmus. Highly vascular.
      • Functional unit: thyroid follicle (sphere of follicular cells with central colloid).
      • Parafollicular (C) cells between follicles — secrete calcitonin.
    • T3 / T4 synthesis (4 steps):
      1. Iodide trapping (Na/I symporter) → follicular cell.
      2. Oxidation to I₂; iodination of tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin (in colloid) → MIT, DIT.
      3. Coupling: MIT + DIT → T3; DIT + DIT → T4.
      4. Endocytosis of thyroglobulin; lysosomal cleavage → T3, T4 released to blood.
    • T4 vs T3:
      • T4: 90% of output. Half-life ~7 days. Pro-hormone (peripheral conversion to T3 by deiodinase).
      • T3: more potent, shorter half-life (~1 day). Most binds to nuclear receptors.
      • Both lipid-soluble → travel bound to thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG).
    • Effects of thyroid hormone (broad and metabolic):
      • ↑ Basal metabolic rate, heat production.
      • ↑ Na/K ATPase activity → ↑ O₂ consumption.
      • ↑ Cardiac output, HR (β-receptor sensitivity ↑).
      • Permissive for GH and catecholamine effects.
      • Required for normal CNS development and bone maturation in children.
    • Calcitonin: from C cells. Released when Ca²⁺ ↑. Inhibits osteoclasts → modest ↓ in serum Ca²⁺. Minor role in humans (compared to PTH).

    Summary: hyper vs hypothyroidism

    Energy / weightWeight loss despite ↑ appetite, heat intoleranceWeight gain, cold intolerance, fatigue
    HeartTachycardia, AFib, palpitationsBradycardia
    GIHyperdefecationConstipation
    Skin / hairWarm, moist; hair thinDry, coarse; hair brittle
    MentalAnxious, restlessSlow thinking, depression
    Labs (primary)↑ T3/T4, ↓ TSH↓ T3/T4, ↑ TSH
    Common causeGraves disease (TSH receptor antibody)Hashimoto thyroiditis (anti-TPO Ab)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Thyroid storm (extreme hyperthyroidism): hyperthermia, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, heart failure. Triggered by infection, surgery, trauma in a hyperthyroid patient. Mortality without treatment. Manage with β-blocker, PTU/methimazole, iodine, steroids, supportive. Myxedema coma (severe hypothyroidism): hypothermia, bradycardia, altered mental status. Mortality high. IV levothyroxine, hydrocortisone (also cover adrenal insufficiency), supportive.

    Quick check

    2. DOK 4. Why is TSH the best single screening test for thyroid disease?
    Show answers
    1. Primary hypothyroidism (thyroid gland failure). TSH rises because the negative feedback is lost.
    2. Because of the loop's logarithmic sensitivity: a small change in T4 produces a large reciprocal change in TSH. TSH detects even subtle thyroid dysfunction before T4 shifts measurably.

    Endocrine · Concept 7 of 13

    Parathyroid and calcium balance P

    Four small glands behind the thyroid keep serum calcium in a tight window. PTH is the calcium-raising hormone. Calcitriol (active vitamin D) and calcitonin round out the system.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: 4 glands on posterior surface of thyroid. Chief cells secrete PTH; oxyphil cells (function unclear).
    • Trigger: ↓ ionized Ca²⁺ detected by Ca-sensing receptor on chief cells.
    • PTH actions (raise serum Ca, lower phosphate):
      • Bone: ↑ osteoclast resorption (indirect, via osteoblasts) → ↑ Ca²⁺ and phosphate released.
      • Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption at DCT; ↓ phosphate reabsorption at PCT (phosphaturia); ↑ 1α-hydroxylase → ↑ calcitriol.
      • Gut (indirect, via calcitriol): ↑ Ca²⁺ and phosphate absorption.
    • Vitamin D activation:
      • Skin (UVB) or diet → cholecalciferol (D3) → liver (25-hydroxylation) → 25-OH-D (storage form, measured clinically) → kidney (1α-hydroxylation, PTH-driven) → 1,25-(OH)₂-D = calcitriol (active).
    • Calcium distribution: 99% in bone. Plasma 8.5-10.5 mg/dL total (40% albumin-bound, 50% ionized = active, 10% complexed).

    Summary

    HormoneTriggerEffect on CaEffect on phosphate
    PTH↓ Ca²⁺↑↓ (renal phosphaturia)
    CalcitriolPTH, ↓ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate↑↑
    Calcitonin↑ Ca²⁺↓ (modest)↓ (modest)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Hypercalcemia signs: "stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones" — kidney stones, bone pain, GI/abdominal pain, confusion. ECG: shortened QT. Hypocalcemia signs: perioral tingling, paresthesias, Chvostek (facial tap → twitch), Trousseau (carpal spasm with BP cuff), prolonged QT. Primary hyperparathyroidism: adenoma → ↑ Ca, ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH. Secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD: failing kidney can't make calcitriol → ↓ Ca → PTH rises chronically → bone disease.

    Draw a three-organ diagram (bone, kidney, gut). Show PTH arrows at each organ. Add calcitriol arrows. Mark vitamin D activation steps from skin → liver → kidney.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Where in the kidney is vitamin D activated?
    2. DOK 3. Why does CKD cause hypocalcemia despite normal calcium intake?
    Show answers
    1. Proximal tubule (1α-hydroxylase).
    2. The failing kidney can't activate vitamin D (1α-hydroxylase deficient) → ↓ calcitriol → ↓ intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption → hypocalcemia. Phosphate retention worsens the picture by complexing with Ca²⁺. Triggers secondary hyperparathyroidism and renal osteodystrophy.

    Endocrine · Concept 8 of 13

    Adrenal cortex: zones and hormones A&P

    Three zones, three steroid classes. "GFR" from outside in maps to "Salt, Sugar, Sex." Aldosterone, cortisol, androgens.

    Notes

    • Cortex zones (outside in) — mnemonic "GFR makes salt, sugar, sex":
      • Zona Glomerulosa: mineralocorticoids (aldosterone). Salt.
      • Zona Fasciculata: glucocorticoids (cortisol). Sugar.
      • Zona Reticularis: androgens (DHEA, androstenedione). Sex.
    • Aldosterone (mineralocorticoid):
      • Trigger: AT-II (RAAS) and high K⁺. Not ACTH-driven.
      • Target: principal cells of cortical collecting duct.
      • Action: ↑ Na/water reabsorption, ↑ K/H secretion → ↑ volume, ↑ BP.
      • Detailed in renal notes (Concept 8).
    • Cortisol (glucocorticoid):
      • Trigger: ACTH (from pituitary, driven by CRH).
      • Diurnal: peak ~8 AM, trough late evening.
      • Effects:
        • Metabolic: ↑ gluconeogenesis (hyperglycemia), ↑ protein catabolism, ↑ lipolysis.
        • Anti-inflammatory + immunosuppressive: ↓ leukocyte function, ↓ cytokines, ↑ neutrophil count (demargination).
        • Permissive: required for catecholamine vasoconstriction.
        • Other: ↓ bone formation, ↑ gastric acid, ↑ amyloid in CNS.
    • Adrenal androgens (DHEA):
      • Weak androgens, peripheral conversion to testosterone and estradiol.
      • Main source of androgens in adult females.
      • Pathologic excess: congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) → virilization.

    Summary: cortisol excess vs deficiency

    FeatureCushing (↑ cortisol)Addison (↓ cortisol + aldo)
    BP↑ (HTN)↓ (hypotension, orthostatic)
    Glucose↑ (hyperglycemia)↓ (hypoglycemia)
    Sodium↑ or normal↓ (hyponatremia)
    Potassium↓ or normal↑ (hyperkalemia)
    HabitusCentral obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, thin skin, easy bruisingWeight loss, fatigue, hyperpigmentation (if primary)
    Bones / moodOsteoporosis, depression/psychosisAdrenal crisis if stressed

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Exogenous steroid use is the most common cause of Cushingoid features. Chronic prednisone suppresses HPA axis → sudden withdrawal can cause adrenal crisis. Always taper. Addisonian crisis: shock, hypoglycemia, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia in someone with adrenal insufficiency under stress (illness, surgery). Treat with IV hydrocortisone, fluids, glucose. Hyperpigmentation in primary adrenal failure: high ACTH precursor → MSH activity → bronze skin, especially in skin creases and oral mucosa.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient on chronic high-dose prednisone abruptly stops. What is the danger?
    2. DOK 4. Why does primary adrenal insufficiency cause hyperpigmentation but secondary does not?
    Show answers
    1. Adrenal crisis. Chronic exogenous steroid has suppressed the HPA axis; native adrenal output is shut down. Abrupt withdrawal removes both the exogenous and endogenous source → hypotension, hypoglycemia, shock.
    2. Primary: adrenal failure → ↓ cortisol → loss of negative feedback → ↑ CRH and ACTH. ACTH is cleaved from POMC, which also generates MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone) → hyperpigmentation. Secondary: pituitary failure → ↓ ACTH (and thus ↓ MSH) → no hyperpigmentation.

    Endocrine · Concept 9 of 13

    Adrenal medulla: epinephrine and norepinephrine P

    The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion. Preganglionic sympathetic fibers synapse directly on chromaffin cells, which dump catecholamines into the blood as part of the fight-or-flight response.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla. Neural crest origin (not mesoderm like the cortex).
    • Synapse: sympathetic preganglionic fiber → chromaffin cell (acts as a modified post-ganglionic neuron). Release goes directly into blood.
    • Output: ~80% epinephrine, ~20% norepinephrine.
    • Effects:
      • ↑ HR, ↑ contractility (β1).
      • Vasoconstriction in skin and GI (α1); vasodilation in skeletal muscle (β2 — epinephrine only).
      • Bronchodilation (β2).
      • ↑ Glycogenolysis, ↑ gluconeogenesis, ↑ lipolysis.
      • Pupil dilation, ↓ GI motility.
    • Pheochromocytoma: chromaffin tumor → episodic catecholamine release → headache, palpitations, sweating, severe paroxysmal HTN. Triad. Workup: plasma or urine metanephrines. Treat with α-blocker (phenoxybenzamine) first, then β-blocker, then surgery.

    Summary

    ReceptorTissueEffect
    α1Vascular smooth muscleVasoconstriction
    β1Heart↑ HR, ↑ contractility
    β2Bronchi, skeletal muscle vesselsBronchodilation, vasodilation
    α2Pre-synaptic (negative feedback)↓ NE release

    CLINICAL THREAD

    The adrenal medulla and the sympathetic nervous system together drive the "fight-or-flight" response. Clinically, IV epinephrine in anaphylaxis works on all four major receptors at once: α1 raises BP, β1 supports HR/contractility, β2 reverses bronchospasm. This is why epinephrine is first-line in anaphylaxis, not antihistamines.

    Draw the sympathetic pre-ganglionic fiber synapsing on a chromaffin cell. Show the cell releasing E and NE into a capillary. List four target effects with the receptor type next to each.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What is the embryologic origin of the adrenal medulla?
    2. DOK 4. Why is α-blockade given before β-blockade in pheochromocytoma?
    Show answers
    1. Neural crest (unlike the cortex, which is mesoderm).
    2. Catecholamine surge causes both α1 vasoconstriction and β2 vasodilation. Blocking β2 first removes the vasodilation while α1 vasoconstriction continues unopposed → hypertensive crisis. α-blockade first, then β-blockade, prevents this.

    Endocrine · Concept 10 of 13

    Pancreatic islets: insulin, glucagon, somatostatin A&P

    Glucose homeostasis lives in the pancreas. Insulin stores energy when fed; glucagon mobilizes it when fasted. Diabetes is what happens when this loop breaks.

    Notes

    • Anatomy: islets of Langerhans (~1 million per pancreas, scattered through exocrine tissue).
    • Cell types:
      • β cells (~70%): insulin. Core of islet.
      • α cells (~20%): glucagon. Periphery.
      • δ cells (~5%): somatostatin (inhibits both insulin and glucagon, also GH).
      • PP/F cells: pancreatic polypeptide.
    • Insulin (anabolic, "fed state"):
      • Trigger: ↑ blood glucose (sensed by GLUT-2 in β cell), incretin hormones (GLP-1, GIP).
      • Receptor: tyrosine kinase. Insulin-sensitive glucose uptake via GLUT-4 (muscle, adipose).
      • Actions: ↑ glucose uptake (muscle, fat), ↑ glycogenesis (liver, muscle), ↑ lipogenesis, ↑ protein synthesis, ↓ gluconeogenesis, ↓ lipolysis, ↓ ketogenesis.
      • Also drives K⁺ into cells (useful in hyperK treatment).
    • Glucagon (catabolic, "fasted state"):
      • Trigger: ↓ blood glucose, amino acids, sympathetic stimulation.
      • Actions in liver: ↑ glycogenolysis, ↑ gluconeogenesis, ↑ ketogenesis.
      • Counter-regulatory hormone like cortisol, growth hormone, epinephrine.
    • Somatostatin: "inhibitor of everything." Inhibits insulin, glucagon, GH, gastrin, secretin. Octreotide (analog) used in variceal bleeding, acromegaly.

    Diagram: glucose feedback loop

    Summary: diabetes types

    FeatureType 1 DMType 2 DM
    CauseAutoimmune β-cell destructionInsulin resistance + relative deficiency

    CLINICAL THREAD

    DKA (T1): no insulin → fat breakdown → ketones → metabolic acidosis + hyperglycemia + osmotic diuresis. pH < 7.30, AG ↑, ketones positive. Treat with fluids, insulin, electrolyte replacement (especially K). HHS (T2): some insulin present prevents ketosis; severe hyperglycemia (often > 600) and hyperosmolar dehydration. Hypoglycemia in diabetics: missed meal, insulin overdose, exercise. Sx: tremor, sweating, confusion. Treat: oral glucose if alert; D50W or glucagon if not.

    2. DOK 4. A T2DM patient with HbA1c 11% has BMI 34. Why might starting metformin be more appropriate than insulin?

    1. β cells (beta cells) of the pancreatic islets.

    Endocrine · Concept 11 of 13

    Pineal + other endocrine tissues P

    Many organs do endocrine work as a side job. The pineal sets the clock. The heart, kidney, GI tract, placenta, and skin all release hormones tuned to their main jobs.

    Notes

    • Pineal gland:
      • Roof of third ventricle (epithalamus).
      • Pinealocytes secrete melatonin in response to darkness (suprachiasmatic nucleus → sympathetic input).
      • Melatonin sets circadian rhythm and regulates reproductive timing in some species.
      • "Brain sand" (corpora arenacea) — calcifications, increase with age, useful radiographic landmark.
    • Heart:
      • ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide): from atrial myocytes when stretched. ↑ Na excretion, ↑ GFR, ↓ renin/aldosterone. Counter-balances RAAS.
      • BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide): from ventricular myocytes. Same effects. Used clinically as HF biomarker.
    • Kidney:
      • Renin: JG cells.
      • Erythropoietin (EPO): peritubular fibroblasts → bone marrow → RBC production.
      • Calcitriol: 1α-hydroxylation of 25-OH-D in PCT.
    • GI tract:
      • Gastrin (stomach G cells): ↑ HCl secretion.
      • Secretin (duodenum): ↑ bicarbonate from pancreas.
      • CCK (duodenum): ↑ bile release, ↑ pancreatic enzymes, satiety.
      • Ghrelin (stomach): "hunger hormone."
      • GLP-1, GIP (incretins): ↑ insulin release after meal. Target of GLP-1 RA drugs.
    • Placenta (pregnancy):
      • hCG: maintains corpus luteum early; pregnancy test target.
      • Human placental lactogen (hPL): insulin-like, contributes to gestational insulin resistance.
      • Estrogen and progesterone: maintain pregnancy.
    • Skin: produces 7-dehydrocholesterol → vitamin D3 with UVB exposure.
    • Adipose: leptin (satiety, long-term energy signaling), adiponectin (insulin sensitivity).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    BNP elevation (> ~100 pg/mL) supports heart failure diagnosis. Higher levels correlate with severity. GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) and SGLT2 inhibitors are now first-line add-ons for T2DM with cardiovascular or renal disease — they exploit gut and renal endocrine biology that wasn't well used a decade ago.

    Stress response and the HPA axis P

    Acute stress is the sympathetic-adrenal medullary (SAM) response. Sustained stress is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Both prepare the body to spend energy and resist injury — but chronic activation is itself pathologic.

    Notes

    • Two stress systems:
      • SAM (fast, seconds): hypothalamus → sympathetic → adrenal medulla → E + NE.
      • HPA (slow, minutes-hours): hypothalamus → CRH → pituitary → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol.
    • Acute adaptive effects:
      • ↑ Cardiac output, ↑ BP, ↑ blood glucose, ↑ alertness.
      • Shunts blood from GI/skin to muscle and brain.
      • Mobilizes fuel: glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis.
      • Suppresses non-essential functions: digestion, growth, reproduction, immune function.
    • Chronic activation (chronic stress, untreated depression, chronic inflammation):
      • HTN, insulin resistance, central obesity, osteoporosis, immune suppression, mood disturbance, hippocampal atrophy.
      • Essentially mimics endogenous Cushing.
    • Cortisol diurnal pattern: peak ~6-8 AM, trough late evening. Lost in disease (flat diurnal pattern in chronic stress, Cushing).
    • Stress-induced hyperglycemia in critical illness: high cortisol + catecholamines + inflammatory cytokines → insulin resistance. Common in ICU even in non-diabetics. Tight glucose control improves outcomes.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Chronic stress is now well-linked to cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, and metabolic syndrome. Stress reduction interventions (sleep, exercise, mindfulness, social support) are not soft additions — they shift HPA axis activity measurably. Cortisol-binding globulin changes (pregnancy, OCP) raise total cortisol without raising free cortisol — interpret total cortisol levels accordingly.

    Endocrine · Concept 13 of 13

    Common endocrine disorders CLIN

    Each endocrine disorder is either too much hormone, too little hormone, or a wrong response. Most clinical patterns predict themselves once you know what the hormone normally does — just reverse the effect for deficiency, amplify it for excess.

    Notes

    • Diabetes mellitus — see Concept 10 for types.
      • Complications: micro (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macro (CAD, stroke, PAD).
      • HbA1c target generally < 7% (individualized).
      • Goal SBP < 130 in DM (depending on guidelines).
    • Thyroid:
      • Hyperthyroidism: Graves (autoantibody to TSH receptor), toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma, thyroiditis.
      • Hypothyroidism: Hashimoto (anti-TPO Ab), post-ablation, iodine deficiency.
      • Treatment: hyper → methimazole, β-blocker for sx, radioiodine, surgery. Hypo → levothyroxine.
    • Adrenal:
      • Cushing: pituitary (Cushing disease, ACTH-secreting adenoma), adrenal (cortisol-secreting tumor), exogenous (steroids most common).
      • Addison: primary adrenal failure (autoimmune most common in developed world).
      • Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn): adrenal adenoma → HTN, hypokalemia, low renin.
      • Pheochromocytoma: chromaffin tumor.
    • Pituitary:
      • Prolactinoma (most common functioning adenoma).
      • Acromegaly / gigantism (GH excess).
      • Hypopituitarism (Sheehan, surgical, radiation, infiltrative).
      • Diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic).
      • SIADH.
    • Parathyroid:
      • Primary hyperparathyroidism (adenoma; ↑ Ca, ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH).
      • Secondary in CKD.
      • Hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy most common).
    • MEN syndromes (multiple endocrine neoplasia):
      • MEN1: parathyroid + pancreas + pituitary (3 P's).
      • MEN2A: medullary thyroid + pheochromocytoma + parathyroid.
      • MEN2B: medullary thyroid + pheochromocytoma + mucosal neuromas + marfanoid habitus.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    For nursing assessment: endocrine emergencies look like extreme physiology — DKA (acidotic, dehydrated), thyroid storm (tachy, hyperthermic, agitated), myxedema coma (bradycardic, hypothermic, obtunded), Addisonian crisis (hypotensive, hypoglycemic, hyperK). Always check glucose and electrolytes in altered mental status. Take a medication history — exogenous steroids, levothyroxine, insulin, sulfonylureas drive much endocrine pathology.
