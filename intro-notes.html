    Intro to A&P · Concept 1 of 6

    What are anatomy and physiology? A&P

    Anatomy is structure. Physiology is function. The two are inseparable: structure predicts function, and the body's function constraints what structures could survive.

    Notes

    • Anatomy (study of structures):
      • Gross (macroscopic): seen with naked eye. Subdivisions:
        • Regional: by body area.
        • Systemic: by organ system.
        • Surface: external + palpable structures.
      • Microscopic anatomy:
        • Cytology: cells.
        • Histology: tissues.
      • Developmental anatomy (embryology); pathological anatomy (diseased structures).
    • Physiology (study of function):
      • Subdivisions usually by system (cardiovascular, renal, etc.) or by level (cell, organ, organismal).
      • Often "physiology of X" — pathophysiology = physiology gone wrong.
    • Complementarity of structure and function:
      • Bones are rigid because they support weight.
      • Alveoli are thin because gas must diffuse across them.
      • The small intestine has villi because it absorbs nutrients.
      • Knowing one half predicts the other.
    • Necessary life functions: maintain boundaries, movement, responsiveness (excitability), digestion, metabolism, excretion, reproduction, growth.
    • Survival needs: nutrients, oxygen, water, normal body temperature, appropriate atmospheric pressure.
    • Disease vs disorder:
      • Disease: identifiable cause (infection, genetic, environmental) and clear pathologic process.
      • Disorder: disruption of normal function, sometimes without identifiable cause.
    • Signs vs symptoms:
      • Sign: objective, measurable (fever, rash, ↑ HR).
      • Symptom: subjective, reported by patient (pain, fatigue, nausea).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which subdivision of anatomy studies tissues?
    2. DOK 2. Is fever a sign or a symptom?
    Show answers
    1. Histology.
    2. Sign — it can be objectively measured. Fatigue or feeling unwell are symptoms (subjective).

    Anatomical position, planes, directional terms, regions A

    Standard language. Anatomical position is the reference; every directional term assumes the body is in it, no matter the actual position.

    Notes

    • Anatomical position: standing erect, facing observer, arms at sides, palms forward, feet together.
    • Directional terms:
      • Superior (cranial) / Inferior (caudal).
      • Anterior (ventral) / Posterior (dorsal).
      • Medial (midline) / Lateral (away from midline).
      • Proximal / Distal (relative to point of attachment).
      • Superficial / Deep.
      • Ipsilateral (same side) / Contralateral (opposite side).
      • Bilateral / Unilateral.
    • Body planes:
      • Sagittal: divides into left/right. Midsagittal = equal halves; parasagittal = unequal.
      • Frontal (coronal): divides into anterior/posterior.
      • Transverse (horizontal, cross-section): divides into superior/inferior.
      • Oblique: at angle other than 90°.
    • Body regions (anterior, key terms):
      • Head/neck: cephalic, frontal, orbital, otic, nasal, oral, cervical.
      • Trunk: thoracic, mammary, abdominal, umbilical, pelvic, inguinal, pubic.
      • Upper limb: acromial, brachial (arm), antebrachial (forearm), antecubital (front elbow), carpal (wrist), palmar, digital, pollex (thumb).
      • Lower limb: coxal (hip), femoral (thigh), patellar (kneecap), crural (leg/shin), tarsal (ankle), pedal (foot), hallux (big toe).
    • Posterior regions: cephalic, cervical, dorsal/vertebral, scapular, lumbar (lower back), sacral, gluteal, popliteal (back of knee), sural (calf), calcaneal (heel), plantar (sole).
    • Abdominal regions (9): right hypochondriac, epigastric, left hypochondriac; right lumbar, umbilical, left lumbar; right iliac (inguinal), hypogastric (pubic), left iliac.
    • Abdominal quadrants (4, clinical): RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ. Used to localize pain.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Pain by quadrant: RUQ → liver, gallbladder. Epigastric → stomach, pancreas, MI (referred). LUQ → spleen. RLQ → appendix. LLQ → diverticulitis. Periumbilical → small bowel or early appendix. Diffuse → peritonitis, mesenteric ischemia, bowel obstruction.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which plane divides the body into superior and inferior portions?
    2. DOK 2. Which body region refers to the front of the elbow?
    Show answers
    1. Transverse (horizontal).
    2. Antecubital.

    Body cavities and serous membranes A

    The body has two main cavities; each is subdivided to wall off organ systems. Serous membranes line the closed cavities and reduce friction as organs move.

    Notes

    • Dorsal cavity:
      • Cranial cavity: contains brain.
      • Spinal (vertebral) cavity: contains spinal cord.
    • Ventral cavity:
      • Thoracic: divided by mediastinum (central — heart, trachea, esophagus, great vessels, thymus) and two pleural cavities (lungs).
      • Abdominopelvic: separated from thoracic by diaphragm. Abdominal (most GI, liver, kidneys) + pelvic (bladder, internal reproductive organs, rectum).
    • Serous membranes: line closed cavities; each has two layers.
      • Pericardium (heart): parietal + visceral; pericardial cavity with serous fluid.
      • Pleura (lungs): parietal + visceral; pleural cavity.
      • Peritoneum (abdomen): parietal + visceral; peritoneal cavity.
      • Function: reduce friction between organ and wall; allow organ movement (heart beating, lungs expanding, bowel peristalsis).
    • Intraperitoneal organs: stomach, jejunum, ileum, transverse colon, sigmoid colon, liver, spleen.
    • Retroperitoneal organs (mnemonic SAD PUCKER): Suprarenals, Aorta + IVC, Duodenum (2nd/3rd), Pancreas (except tail), Ureters, Colon (asc + desc), Kidneys, Esophagus, Rectum.
    • Mediastinum borders:
      • Superior: thoracic inlet.
      • Inferior: diaphragm.
      • Anterior: sternum.
      • Posterior: vertebral column.
      • Lateral: lungs.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Inflammation of each serous membrane has its own name and signature finding: pericarditis (friction rub, sharp chest pain worse lying flat), pleurisy (sharp chest pain with breathing, pleural rub), peritonitis (rigid abdomen, rebound tenderness, fever — surgical emergency).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What structure separates the thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities?
    2. DOK 3. Is the pancreas intraperitoneal or retroperitoneal?
    Show answers
    1. Diaphragm.
    2. Retroperitoneal (except the tail, which is intraperitoneal).

    Intro to A&P · Concept 4 of 6

    Levels of organization and the 11 organ systems A&P

    Six levels of organization build from atoms to a whole organism. Each level has emergent properties — abilities the level below cannot perform.

    Notes

    • Six levels, smallest to largest:
      1. Chemical: atoms, molecules.
      2. Cellular: smallest unit of life.
      3. Tissue: similar cells with shared function.
      4. Organ: 2+ tissue types working together.
      5. Organ system: organs cooperating.
      6. Organism: the whole living being.
    • 11 organ systems (with primary functions):
      • Integumentary: barrier, thermoregulation, sensation, vit D.
      • Skeletal: support, protection, movement, blood cell production, mineral storage.
      • Muscular: movement, posture, heat.
      • Nervous: rapid coordination, sensing, integration.
      • Endocrine: slow, sustained chemical coordination.
      • Cardiovascular: transport blood.
      • Lymphatic + Immune: fluid return, defense.
      • Respiratory: gas exchange.
      • Digestive: ingest, digest, absorb, eliminate.
      • Urinary: filter blood, fluid + electrolyte + acid-base balance.
      • Reproductive: produce gametes, support pregnancy.
    • Cross-system collaboration: no system works alone. Cardiovascular distributes what respiratory absorbs. Nervous and endocrine coordinate everything. Immune and integumentary defend.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Place in order from smallest to largest: tissue, atom, organ, cell.
    2. DOK 2. Which organ system is primarily responsible for fluid and electrolyte balance?
    Show answers
    1. Atom (chemical) → cell → tissue → organ.
    2. Urinary.

    Intro to A&P · Concept 5 of 6

    Homeostasis and feedback loops P

    Homeostasis is dynamic stability — variables stay within tight ranges despite external change. Negative feedback corrects deviations; positive feedback amplifies until an event completes.

    Notes

    • Homeostasis: maintenance of relatively stable internal conditions despite external change. Active, not static.
    • Components of a feedback loop:
      1. Stimulus: change in a variable.
      2. Sensor (receptor): detects change.
      3. Control center: processes input vs set point.
      4. Effector: produces the response.
      5. Response: corrects or amplifies the variable.
    • Negative feedback (workhorse):
      • Response opposes the original change → restores baseline.
      • Examples: thermoregulation, BP (baroreflex), glucose (insulin/glucagon), Ca²⁺ (PTH), thyroid (TSH-T4), cortisol (CRH-ACTH).
    • Positive feedback (rare in humans):
      • Response amplifies the original change. Self-terminates when event completes.
      • Examples: oxytocin in labor, LH surge at ovulation, blood clotting cascade, action potential depolarization.
    • Set point: the target value the system tries to maintain. Can be reset (fever resets temperature set point).
    • When homeostasis fails: disease. Most chronic diseases are framed as homeostatic failures of a specific variable (glucose, BP, fluid, pH).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Every chronic disease can be framed as a homeostatic failure. Diabetes: insulin can't maintain glucose set point. Heart failure: CO can't maintain perfusion. Hypothyroidism: T4 can't maintain metabolic rate. The drug treatments restore or substitute for the broken feedback loop element.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Is blood clotting an example of positive or negative feedback?
    2. DOK 3. A fever sets the hypothalamic temperature set point higher. Why does this cause shivering?
    Show answers
    1. Positive — each step amplifies the next until the clot forms (terminates when bleeding stops).
    2. The body now perceives its current temperature as below the new (higher) set point → activates heat-generating responses (shivering, vasoconstriction). When the fever breaks, set point drops; the body now feels too warm → sweats and vasodilates.

    Disease, signs, and symptoms — clinical reasoning CLIN

    Clinical thinking starts with separating what you see (signs) from what the patient reports (symptoms), then matching them to a pattern. Anatomy gives you where; physiology tells you why.

    Notes

    • Disease: identifiable cause + measurable pathology. Examples: diabetes (insulin signaling), tuberculosis (Mycobacterium), MI (coronary occlusion).
    • Disorder: disruption of function, sometimes without a clear single cause. Examples: anxiety disorder, IBS, functional bowel disorders.
    • Sign: objective, measurable by an observer. Examples: fever, rash, tachycardia, JVD, jaundice.
    • Symptom: subjective, reported by patient. Examples: pain, fatigue, nausea, dizziness.
    • Building a clinical picture:
      • History (mostly symptoms).
      • Physical exam (mostly signs).
      • Labs and imaging (more signs — objective).
      • Differential diagnosis: list of possible explanations, ranked by likelihood.
      • Working diagnosis: most likely based on current data.
    • Inflammatory disorders (early examples to anchor the rest of the course):
      • Peritonitis: rigid abdomen, fever, rebound tenderness. Cause: infection, perforation, ruptured appendix.
      • Pleurisy: sharp chest pain on inspiration; viral or pneumonia.
      • Pericarditis: chest pain worse lying flat, friction rub; viral or autoimmune.
      • Meningitis: fever, neck stiffness, headache; bacterial or viral.
    • Cardinal signs of inflammation (Celsus, 1st century AD): redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor), loss of function (functio laesa). Caused by vasodilation, ↑ permeability, pain mediators, and tissue damage.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Is shortness of breath a sign or a symptom?
    2. DOK 3. Name the four cardinal signs of inflammation (Celsus).
    Show answers
    1. Symptom (subjective; reported by patient). Tachypnea (the measurable fast respiratory rate) is the sign.
    2. Redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor). Loss of function (functio laesa) was added later.
