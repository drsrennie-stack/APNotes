    Regional Muscles · Concept 1 of 12

    How to learn regional muscles A

    Don't memorize muscles in isolation. Anchor each to the joint it crosses and the movement it produces. If you know where it starts (origin), where it ends (insertion), and the joint it crosses, the action falls out for free.

    Notes

    • Naming patterns tell you about the muscle:
      • Location: temporalis, occipitalis, intercostals.
      • Action: extensor digitorum, flexor carpi radialis, adductor longus.
      • Shape: deltoid (triangle), trapezius, rhomboid.
      • Size: maximus, longus, brevis.
      • Origin/insertion: sternocleidomastoid (sternum + clavicle → mastoid).
      • Direction of fibers: rectus (straight), oblique, transverse.
    • Origin vs insertion:
      • Origin = less mobile attachment (usually proximal).
      • Insertion = more mobile attachment (usually distal). Pulled toward the origin when the muscle contracts.
    • Action language:
      • Flexion/extension (sagittal plane, decreasing/increasing angle).
      • Abduction/adduction (frontal plane, away from / toward midline).
      • Rotation (transverse plane); medial vs lateral.
      • Circumduction (combined flex + abd + ext + add at ball-and-socket joints).
      • Specialized: pronation/supination (forearm), inversion/eversion (foot), dorsiflexion/plantarflexion (ankle), opposition (thumb), protraction/retraction (scapula, jaw), elevation/depression.
    • Innervation matters clinically: a nerve injury denervates every muscle it supplies; predicting deficits requires knowing the nerve, not the individual muscle.
    • Compartments: in the limbs, fascia divides muscles into compartments that share a nerve and blood supply. Learning by compartment beats learning muscle-by-muscle.

    Sketch a stick figure. At each major joint (shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle), draw a curved arrow for each movement that joint allows. Label the prime mover for that movement next to the arrow. This is your spatial map for every region.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 2 of 12

    Head & Neck: facial expression, mastication, neck movement A

    Facial muscles insert on skin (so they move skin, not bone). Mastication muscles move the mandible. Neck muscles flex, extend, and rotate the head on the cervical spine.

    Joints in this region

    • Temporomandibular joint (TMJ): condylar/hinge-glide synovial. Movements: elevation, depression, protraction, retraction, side-to-side (lateral excursion).
    • Atlanto-occipital joint (C0–C1): condylar synovial. Movements: head flexion/extension ("yes" nod).
    • Atlanto-axial joint (C1–C2): pivot synovial. Movement: head rotation ("no" shake).
    • Cervical intervertebral joints (C2–C7): contribute flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and rotation of the neck.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Bell's palsy: unilateral CN VII paralysis → ipsilateral loss of facial expression on that side (drooping mouth corner, can't close eye, can't wrinkle forehead). Distinguishes from stroke (which spares the forehead because CN VII forehead motor nucleus gets bilateral cortical input). Trigeminal neuralgia: paroxysmal facial pain in CN V distribution; treat with carbamazepine. TMJ disorders: pain, clicking, restricted opening; conservative management first.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 3 of 12

    Vertebral column & back: extension and posture A

    Superficial back muscles move the upper limb (those belong with shoulder, Concept 6). The intrinsic back muscles (under the superficial layer) extend the spine, hold you upright against gravity, and rotate or laterally bend the trunk.

    Joints in this region

    • Intervertebral joints (cartilaginous, IVD between bodies): small individual movement; sum allows flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation.
    • Zygapophyseal (facet) joints (synovial, plane): guide direction of motion in each region (cervical: most rotation; thoracic: mostly rotation; lumbar: mostly flex/extend).
    • Costovertebral joints: ribs articulating with thoracic vertebrae (relevant for breathing).

    Intrinsic back muscles (innervated by dorsal rami of spinal nerves)

    Transversospinalis: semispinalis, multifidus, rotatoresDeep, oblique fibers spanning transverse-to-spinous processesExtend and rotate spine; stabilize segments; multifidus is key to lumbar stability
    Splenius capitis / cervicisSuperior posterior neckBilateral: extends head/neck. Unilateral: rotates head to same side
    Suboccipitals (rectus capitis post. major/minor, obliquus capitis sup./inf.)Deepest, around C0–C2Fine head movements; extension and rotation of head

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Low back pain often involves the lumbar paraspinals (esp. multifidus deconditioning) and QL. Lumbar disc herniation at L4/L5 or L5/S1 compresses nerve roots, causing radicular leg pain (sciatica) and weakness in specific myotomes. Test the L5 myotome with great toe extension (EHL), S1 with plantarflexion. Scoliosis: lateral curvature, may be idiopathic in adolescents.

    Show answers
    1. Transversospinalis group, especially the multifidi.
    2. L5 (great-toe extension is an L5 myotome via EHL).

    Regional Muscles · Concept 4 of 12

    Thorax & respiration: diaphragm, intercostals, accessory muscles A

    Quiet inspiration is mostly the diaphragm. Forced inspiration recruits accessory muscles to lift the ribs and sternum. Expiration is passive at rest but becomes active (abdominals, internal intercostals) with exercise or disease.

    Joints in this region

    • Costovertebral joints: ribs to vertebrae. Synovial plane joints. Pivot point for "bucket handle" and "pump handle" rib motion.
    • Sternocostal joints: costal cartilages to sternum. Allow rib elevation/depression.
    • Sternoclavicular (SC) joint: only true bony connection of the upper limb to the axial skeleton.

    Primary respiratory muscles

    MuscleOrigin / InsertionActionInnervation
    DiaphragmSternum, lower ribs, lumbar vertebrae → central tendonContracts → flattens dome → vertical thorax expansion (inhalation). Primary muscle of quiet inspiration.Phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5 "keeps the diaphragm alive")
    External intercostals (11 pairs)Inferior border of rib above → superior border of rib below; fibers angle down/forwardElevate ribs → expand thorax (inspiration)Intercostal nerves (T1–T11)
    Internal intercostalsDeep to external, fibers angle down/backwardDepress ribs (forced expiration)Intercostal nerves
    Innermost intercostals + transversus thoracisDeepest layerAssist forced expiration; neurovascular bundle runs between innermost and internal layersIntercostal nerves

    Accessory respiratory muscles

    • Forced inspiration: SCM, scalenes, pectoralis minor, serratus posterior superior. Elevate ribs and sternum to expand thorax further.
    • Forced expiration: rectus abdominis, external/internal obliques, transversus abdominis, internal intercostals. Compress abdominal contents → push diaphragm up → depress ribs.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Phrenic nerve injury → ipsilateral hemidiaphragm paralysis; chest X-ray shows elevated hemidiaphragm. Origin from C3–C5 explains the saying "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive": cervical spine injuries at or above C3 are usually fatal without ventilation. COPD patients use accessory muscles at rest ("tripoding" with hands on knees fixes the shoulder girdle to optimize pec minor as an inspiratory muscle). Intercostal nerve block for rib fracture pain enters between innermost and internal layers in the costal groove (vein-artery-nerve, "VAN," runs superior → inferior).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which nerve supplies the diaphragm? Which spinal levels?
    2. DOK 2. A COPD patient leans forward and braces hands on knees. Which accessory muscle benefits, and why?
    Show answers
    1. Phrenic nerve, C3–C5.
    2. Pectoralis minor. With the shoulder girdle fixed by bracing the arms, pec minor's pull on the scapula now pulls the ribs up (reversed origin/insertion), expanding the thorax.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 5 of 12

    Abdominal wall & pelvic floor A

    The abdominal wall has four paired muscles arranged in three layers. They flex and rotate the trunk, generate intra-abdominal pressure (cough, defecation, parturition), and support the viscera. The pelvic floor closes the bony pelvic outlet from below.

    Joints in this region

    • Lumbar intervertebral joints + facets: permit flexion (sit-up), extension (back-bend), and trunk rotation.
    • Lumbosacral joint (L5–S1): transition between mobile spine and fixed pelvis.
    • Sacroiliac (SI) joints: limited gliding; transmit weight from spine to pelvis.

    Abdominal wall muscles

    MuscleOriginInsertionAction
    Rectus abdominisPubic symphysis & pubic crestCostal cartilages 5–7, xiphoidFlexes trunk (sit-up); compresses abdomen
    External obliqueExternal surfaces of ribs 5–12Iliac crest, linea alba (via aponeurosis)Bilateral: flex trunk. Unilateral: lateral flex to same side, rotation to opposite side. ("Hands in pockets" direction.)
    Internal obliqueIliac crest, thoracolumbar fascia, inguinal lig.Linea alba, ribs 10–12Bilateral: flex trunk. Unilateral: lateral flex & rotation to same side
    Transversus abdominis (TA)Iliac crest, thoracolumbar fascia, lower ribs, inguinal lig.Linea albaCompresses abdomen; generates intra-abdominal pressure; spinal stabilizer

    All four innervated by thoracoabdominal nerves (T7–T12) and iliohypogastric/ilioinguinal (L1).

    Pelvic floor (pelvic diaphragm)

    • Levator ani (puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus) and coccygeus: form the muscular floor of the pelvis.
    • Support pelvic viscera (bladder, rectum, uterus in females), maintain continence, contribute to intra-abdominal pressure.
    • Innervation: nerve to levator ani (S3–S4) and pudendal nerve (S2–S4).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Diastasis recti: separation of rectus abdominis halves at linea alba, common postpartum. Inguinal hernia: indirect (through deep inguinal ring, lateral to inferior epigastric vessels) is most common in young males; direct (through Hesselbach's triangle, medial to inferior epigastric) is more common in older men. Pelvic floor dysfunction: contributes to stress urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse; PT can be highly effective.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient rotates trunk to the left. Which oblique muscles contracted?
    2. DOK 1. Name the three named parts of levator ani.
    Show answers
    1. Left internal oblique (same-side rotation) + right external oblique (opposite-side rotation). They work together as a couple.
    2. Puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 6 of 12

    Pectoral girdle & shoulder A

    The scapula floats on the thorax, anchored by muscles, not bone. The glenohumeral joint trades stability for mobility, relying on the rotator cuff for active stability. Move the scapula first, then the humerus.

    Joints in this region

    • Sternoclavicular (SC) joint: saddle synovial. The only bony link of the upper limb to the axial skeleton.
    • Acromioclavicular (AC) joint: plane synovial. Transmits force between clavicle and scapula.
    • Scapulothoracic "joint": not a true joint; scapula gliding on the thoracic cage. Movements: elevation/depression, protraction/retraction, upward/downward rotation.
    • Glenohumeral (shoulder) joint: ball-and-socket synovial. Movements: flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation, circumduction. Most mobile, most commonly dislocated.

Winged scapula: long thoracic nerve injury → serratus anterior paralysis → scapula wings off the thorax when patient pushes against a wall. Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder): progressive global ROM loss, often idiopathic or post-immobilization.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 7 of 12

    Arm & elbow A

    The arm has two compartments separated by intermuscular septa. The anterior compartment flexes the elbow (and is the biceps' second job at the shoulder). The posterior compartment extends. Pronation and supination happen at the radioulnar joints.

    Joints in this region

    • Humeroulnar joint: hinge synovial. Flexion / extension (the main elbow joint).
    • Humeroradial joint: condylar. Flexion / extension; allows radius to rotate.
    • Proximal radioulnar joint: pivot. Pronation / supination of forearm (radius rotates around ulna).
    • Distal radioulnar joint: pivot. Completes pronation / supination.

    Anterior compartment of arm (musculocutaneous nerve, C5–C7)

    Biceps brachii (long & short heads)Long: supraglenoid tubercle. Short: coracoid processRadial tuberosity, bicipital aponeurosisFlexes elbow; supinates forearm (strongest supinator); flexes shoulder
    BrachialisDistal half of anterior humerusCoronoid process & tuberosity of ulnaPrimary elbow flexor (works in all positions)
    CoracobrachialisCoracoid processMedial mid-humerusFlexes & adducts shoulder

    Posterior compartment of arm (radial nerve, C6–C8)

    MuscleOriginInsertionAction
    Triceps brachii (long, lateral, medial heads)Long: infraglenoid tubercle. Lateral & medial: posterior humerusOlecranon of ulnaExtends elbow (long head also extends shoulder)
    Anconeus (small)Lateral epicondyleOlecranonAssists elbow extension; abducts ulna in pronation

    Forearm muscles producing pronation/supination (covered also in Concept 8)

    • Supinator (radial n.): supinates with elbow extended.
    • Biceps brachii: strongest supinator with elbow flexed (think turning a screwdriver clockwise with your right hand).
    • Pronator teres & pronator quadratus (median n.): pronate forearm.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Biceps tendon rupture (long head): "Popeye sign"; usually a degenerative tear at the bicipital groove. Function is often preserved because brachialis remains intact. Olecranon bursitis: "student's elbow" from prolonged pressure on the elbow. Cubital tunnel syndrome: ulnar nerve entrapment at the elbow (medial epicondyle). Tinel's sign positive, paresthesias in 4th/5th digits.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Name the primary elbow flexor (the workhorse in every position).
    2. DOK 2. A patient has weak elbow extension after a humeral shaft fracture. Which nerve is injured?
    Show answers
    1. Brachialis (biceps is the supinator/elbow flexor stylist, but brachialis does the heavy lifting in every position).
    2. Radial nerve. It runs in the radial (spiral) groove of the humerus and is vulnerable to mid-shaft fractures. Wrist drop is also classic.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 8 of 12

    Forearm & hand A

    Forearm muscles split into anterior (flexors + pronators) and posterior (extensors + supinator) compartments. The anterior compartment is mostly median-nerve territory; the posterior compartment is mostly radial-nerve. Intrinsic hand muscles are mostly ulnar-nerve, with thenar exceptions on median.

    Joints in this region

    • Radiocarpal (wrist) joint: condylar synovial. Flexion / extension; abduction (radial deviation) / adduction (ulnar deviation).
    • Carpometacarpal (CMC) joints: mostly plane; 1st CMC of thumb is saddle (allows opposition).
    • Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints: condylar. Flex/extend + abduct/adduct fingers.
    • Interphalangeal (PIP, DIP) joints: hinge. Flex/extend only.

    Intrinsic hand muscles

    • Thenar group (median n. via recurrent branch): abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis (superficial head), opponens pollicis. Allow opposition of the thumb.
    • Hypothenar group (ulnar n.): abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi brevis, opponens digiti minimi.
    • Lumbricals: flex MCP, extend IP joints (write the letter Y with your fingers). Lateral 2 = median n.; medial 2 = ulnar n.
    • Interossei (ulnar n.): Dorsal AB-duct (DAB), Palmar AD-duct (PAD). Also assist lumbrical action (flex MCP, extend IP).
    • Adductor pollicis (ulnar n.): adducts thumb.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Carpal tunnel syndrome: median nerve compression at the wrist. Numbness/tingling in lateral 3.5 digits, thenar atrophy in advanced cases. Ulnar claw hand: ulnar nerve lesion at the wrist denervates medial 2 lumbricals + interossei → loss of MCP flexion + IP extension in digits 4 & 5 → hyperextended MCP, flexed IP. Wrist drop: radial nerve lesion proximal to elbow (e.g., humeral shaft fracture) → can't extend wrist or fingers. Anatomical snuffbox tenderness after fall on outstretched hand suggests scaphoid fracture — high risk of avascular necrosis.

    Quick check

    2. DOK 2. A patient cannot oppose the thumb to the pinky and has thenar wasting. Which nerve?
    3. DOK 2. Snuffbox tenderness after a fall — what bone, what worry?
    Show answers
    1. Flexor digitorum profundus (FDP).
    2. Median nerve (typically at the carpal tunnel).
    3. Scaphoid fracture. Worry: avascular necrosis of the proximal pole (the scaphoid's blood supply enters distally and flows retrograde).

    Regional Muscles · Concept 9 of 12

    Pelvic girdle & hip A

    The hip is a deep ball-and-socket built for weight-bearing stability. Anterior muscles flex, posterior/lateral muscles extend and abduct, medial muscles adduct, and a cluster of deep muscles laterally rotate.

    Joints in this region

    • Hip (acetabulofemoral) joint: ball-and-socket synovial. Movements: flexion / extension, abduction / adduction, internal / external rotation, circumduction. More stable, less mobile than shoulder.
    • Pubic symphysis: cartilaginous; widens slightly in pregnancy.
    • Sacroiliac joints: partly synovial, partly fibrous; transmit body weight to pelvis.

    • Pectineus: adduction + flexion at hip (femoral n., not obturator).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Trendelenburg sign: with gluteus medius/minimus weakness on the stance leg, the contralateral pelvis drops when the patient lifts that leg. Indicates superior gluteal nerve dysfunction or hip pathology. Piriformis syndrome: piriformis irritates the underlying sciatic nerve → gluteal & posterior thigh pain. Hip fracture (intracapsular femoral neck): risks avascular necrosis of the femoral head; the leg classically appears shortened & externally rotated.

    Regional Muscles · Concept 10 of 12

    Thigh & knee A

    Thigh compartments map to action: anterior extends the knee (quads + femoral n.), posterior flexes the knee and extends the hip (hamstrings + sciatic/tibial n.), medial adducts the hip (covered in Concept 9). The knee is a hinge that locks at full extension.

    Joints in this region

    • Tibiofemoral (knee) joint: modified hinge synovial. Flexion / extension; small "screw home" rotation at terminal extension (lateral rotation of tibia).
    • Patellofemoral joint: sellar/plane; patella glides in trochlear groove.
    • Superior tibiofibular joint: plane synovial; allows minor fibular movement.

    Anterior compartment: knee extensors & some hip flexors (femoral n., L2–L4)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    ACL injury: common in non-contact pivoting with planted foot (skiing, soccer, basketball); pop, swelling, instability. Positive anterior drawer / Lachman test. "Unhappy triad": ACL + MCL + medial meniscus injury (classically from lateral blow to a planted knee). Patellofemoral pain syndrome: pain behind the patella, worse with stairs/squats; often related to weak VMO and poor patellar tracking. Hamstring strain: high incidence in sprinters; biceps femoris long head most common.

    2. Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). Positive Lachman test and anterior drawer test (anterior translation of tibia on femur).

    Regional Muscles · Concept 11 of 12

    Leg, ankle & foot A

    The leg has three compartments with three different nerves and three different jobs: anterior dorsiflexes (deep fibular n.), lateral everts (superficial fibular n.), posterior plantarflexes (tibial n.).

    Joints in this region

    • Talocrural (ankle) joint: hinge synovial between tibia/fibula and talus. Dorsiflexion / plantarflexion.
    • Subtalar joint: talus on calcaneus. Inversion / eversion of the foot.
    • Transverse tarsal joints & intertarsal joints: gliding; allow foot to mold to terrain.
    • MTP / IP joints of toes: flex / extend.

    Anterior compartment: dorsiflexors (deep fibular [peroneal] n., L4–S1)

    MuscleAction

    Extensor digitorum longus (EDL)Extend toes 2–5; dorsiflex foot
    Fibularis (peroneus) tertiusDorsiflex + evert foot

    MuscleAction
    Fibularis (peroneus) longusEvert & plantarflex foot; supports arches
    Fibularis (peroneus) brevisEvert & plantarflex

    Posterior compartment: plantarflexors & toe flexors (tibial n., L4–S3)

    MuscleAction
    Gastrocnemius (2 heads)Plantarflex foot + flex knee (crosses both). Powerful, fast-twitch dominant.
    SoleusPlantarflex foot (only). Slow-twitch dominant, postural.
    Plantaris (variable)Vestigial; small contribution to plantarflexion. Tendon often confused with Achilles rupture.

    Gastrocnemius + soleus = triceps surae → insert via the Achilles (calcaneal) tendon onto the calcaneus.

    Intrinsic foot muscles (brief)

    • Dorsal: extensor digitorum brevis (deep fibular n.).
    • Plantar (4 layers, mostly medial & lateral plantar n. of tibial n.): support arches, fine toe movements.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Foot drop: common fibular nerve injury (wraps around fibular neck, vulnerable to compression — crossing legs, cast pressure, fibular head fracture) → loss of dorsiflexion and eversion → steppage gait. Achilles tendon rupture: sudden "kicked in the back of the leg" pain; positive Thompson test (squeezing calf doesn't plantarflex foot). Plantar fasciitis: heel pain worst with first steps in the morning; treat with calf/plantar stretching, supportive footwear. Compartment syndrome of leg: especially anterior compartment after trauma; pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, pallor, paresthesias, late: pulselessness/paralysis. Surgical emergency (fasciotomy).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which nerve injury causes foot drop?
    2. DOK 2. Three compartments of the leg, three actions, three nerves — match them.
    Show answers
    1. Common fibular (peroneal) nerve.
    2. Anterior = dorsiflexion = deep fibular n. Lateral = eversion = superficial fibular n. Posterior = plantarflexion = tibial n.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Match joint type to typical injury: hinge joints (elbow, knee, IP) most often suffer collateral ligament tears (lateral stress). Pivot joints rarely dislocate (e.g., proximal radius head subluxation = "nursemaid's elbow" in toddlers). Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) trade stability for range, and the shoulder is the most-dislocated joint in the body. Saddle joints (1st CMC, SC) are uniquely positioned for opposition and limited rotation.
