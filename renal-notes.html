Renal + Fluid, Electrolyte & Acid-Base

The kidneys do four things: filter, reabsorb, secrete, and adjust. Everything in this unit (volume, sodium, potassium, calcium, acid-base) is one of those four operations applied to a different substance.     Renal · Concept 1 of 14

    Kidney gross anatomy and position A

    The kidneys are retroperitoneal bean-shaped filters sitting against the posterior abdominal wall. Right kidney sits slightly lower than the left because of the liver above.

    Notes

    • Location: retroperitoneal, T12 to L3. R kidney lower than L by ~1-2 cm (liver displaces it). Each ~12 cm long.
    • External features: hilum (medial); concave indentation where renal artery, vein, and ureter enter/exit. Renal capsule (fibrous), perirenal fat (cushion), renal fascia (anchor).
    • Internal regions:
      • Cortex (outer): contains glomeruli, PCT, DCT.
      • Medulla (inner): contains renal pyramids (8-15 per kidney). Pyramids project apex (papilla) toward minor calyx. Long loops of Henle and collecting ducts.
      • Renal columns: extensions of cortex between pyramids.
    • Drainage path: papilla → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter → bladder → urethra.
    • Functions:
      • Filter blood & produce urine (waste removal).
      • Regulate fluid and electrolyte balance.
      • Regulate acid-base.
      • Long-term BP regulation (RAAS).
      • Erythropoietin → RBC production.
      • Activate vitamin D (1,25-(OH)₂-D).
      • Gluconeogenesis (fasting).

    Summary

    RegionWhat's thereFunction
    CortexGlomeruli, PCT, DCTFiltration, reabsorption
    Medulla (pyramids)LoH, collecting ductsConcentration of urine
    Calyces, pelvisUrine collectionDrainage to ureter

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis) typically lodge at three narrowings: ureteropelvic junction, where the ureter crosses the iliac vessels, and ureterovesical junction. Severe colicky flank-to-groin pain.

    Renal · Concept 2 of 14

    Nephron anatomy and types A

    The nephron is the functional unit. One million per kidney. Each has a filter (renal corpuscle) and a tubule that processes the filtrate into urine.

    Notes

    • Nephron parts in order of flow:
      • Renal corpuscle: glomerulus (capillary tuft) + Bowman's capsule.
      • Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT): in cortex. Workhorse of reabsorption.
      • Loop of Henle (LoH): descending limb (water permeable, salt impermeable) and ascending limb (salt permeable, water impermeable).
      • Distal convoluted tubule (DCT): in cortex. Fine-tuning by aldosterone, PTH.
      • Collecting duct (CD): several nephrons share. ADH acts here.
    • Two nephron types:
      • Cortical (~85%): short LoH staying mostly in cortex.
      • Juxtamedullary (~15%): long LoH diving deep into medulla. Create the medullary osmotic gradient.
    • Filtration barrier (3 layers):
      • Fenestrated capillary endothelium (keeps cells in).
      • Glomerular basement membrane (negative charge, keeps albumin in).
      • Podocyte foot processes with filtration slits (keeps larger proteins in).
    • Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA):
      • JG cells (modified smooth muscle of afferent arteriole): secrete renin.
      • Macula densa (specialized cells of DCT): sense NaCl concentration in tubular fluid.
      • Mesangial cells: structural + contractile.
    • Micturition (deep dive): stretch receptors in bladder → afferent to spinal cord → parasympathetic (S2-S4) → detrusor contracts, internal sphincter opens. Cortical control over external sphincter.

    Summary

    SegmentMain jobKey regulator
    GlomerulusFilterBP, arteriolar tone
    PCTBulk reabsorption (~67% Na/H₂O)Iso-osmotic, mostly unregulated
    LoHCreate medullary gradient—
    DCTFine-tune Na, CaAldosterone, PTH
    CDFinal H₂O, Na, K, H⁺ balanceADH, aldosterone

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Proteinuria implies damage to the filtration barrier (commonly podocyte injury or basement membrane). Hematuria with RBC casts implies glomerular injury (glomerulonephritis). The nephron tells you where in the kidney pathology is happening.

    Renal · Concept 3 of 14

    Renal blood supply A

    The kidney has two capillary beds in series: glomerular (high pressure for filtration) and peritubular (low pressure for reabsorption). The arteriolar geometry makes that possible.

    Notes

    • Path: renal artery → segmental → interlobar → arcuate → cortical radiate → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries (cortical nephrons) or vasa recta (juxtamedullary nephrons) → cortical radiate v → arcuate v → interlobar v → renal vein.
    • Two arterioles, two beds:
      • Afferent constricts → ↓ glomerular pressure → ↓ GFR.
      • Efferent constricts → ↑ glomerular pressure → ↑ GFR (up to a point, then ↓ renal blood flow limits filtration).
    • Renal blood flow (RBF): ~1.1 L/min (~20-25% of CO). The kidneys receive a disproportionate share of CO for their mass.
    • Filtration fraction (FF): GFR/RBF ≈ 20%. The other 80% leaves via efferent arteriole.
    • Autoregulation keeps RBF and GFR constant across MAP 80-180 mmHg via two mechanisms:
      • Myogenic: stretch of afferent arteriole → reflex constriction.
      • Tubuloglomerular feedback: ↑ NaCl at macula densa → adenosine → afferent constriction → ↓ GFR.

    Summary

    Vessel changeEffect on GFREffect on RBF
    Afferent constricts↓↓
    Afferent dilates↑↑
    Efferent constricts↑ (modest)↓
    Efferent dilates↓↑

    CLINICAL THREAD

    ACE inhibitors dilate the efferent arteriole → ↓ glomerular pressure. Protective in diabetic nephropathy (prevents hyperfiltration injury). But can cause acute kidney injury in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis, where they depend on AT-II–driven efferent constriction to maintain GFR. NSAIDs block prostaglandins → afferent constriction → ↓ RBF → AKI risk in dehydrated patients.

    2. In renal artery stenosis, perfusion pressure to the glomerulus is low. The kidney compensates by AT-II–driven efferent constriction to maintain glomerular pressure and GFR. ACE inhibitors block AT-II formation → efferent dilates → glomerular pressure falls → GFR collapses.

    Renal · Concept 4 of 14

    Glomerular filtration and GFR P

    Filtration is just hydrostatic pressure pushing water and small solutes across a membrane, opposed by oncotic pressure and capsular pressure. GFR is the rate.

    Notes

    • Starling forces at the glomerulus:
      • PGC (glomerular capillary hydrostatic, ~55 mmHg): pushes OUT.
      • πGC (glomerular oncotic, ~30 mmHg): pulls IN.
      • PBC (Bowman's capsule hydrostatic, ~15 mmHg): pushes IN (resists filtration).
      • Net filtration pressure ≈ 55 − 30 − 15 = +10 mmHg outward.
    • Normal GFR ≈ 125 mL/min ≈ 180 L/day. Almost all of it reabsorbed (we urinate ~1.5 L/day).
    • What changes GFR:
      • ↑ Afferent constriction → ↓ GFR.
      • ↑ Efferent constriction → ↑ GFR.
      • ↑ PBC (obstruction): ↓ GFR.
      • ↑ πGC (dehydration with concentrated plasma proteins): ↓ GFR.
      • Surface area loss (lost nephrons): ↓ GFR.
    • Creatinine clearance as GFR estimate:
      • CrCl = (UCr × V) / PCr.
      • Creatinine: produced by muscle at steady rate, freely filtered, slightly secreted (modest overestimate).
      • Serum creatinine ↑ as GFR ↓ but lags acute changes.
      • Estimating equations: Cockcroft-Gault, MDRD, CKD-EPI.
    • Inulin clearance is the gold standard — freely filtered, not reabsorbed, not secreted. Rarely used clinically. Iohexol clearance is the modern reference.

    Worked calculations

    1. Creatinine clearance: 24-hour urine creatinine 1,440 mg, urine volume 1,500 mL, serum creatinine 1.0 mg/dL.
      • UCr = 1,440 mg / 1,500 mL = 0.96 mg/mL = 96 mg/dL.
      • V = 1,500 mL / 1,440 min = 1.04 mL/min.
      • CrCl = (96 × 1.04) / 1.0 = ~100 mL/min.
      • Interpretation: low end of normal.
    2. Acute change: Patient with stable baseline Cr 1.0 → 2.0 over 48 h.
      • Doubling Cr ≈ halving GFR. Estimated GFR drops from ~100 to ~50.
      • Interpretation: meets KDIGO criteria for AKI stage 2.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Serum creatinine is convenient but flawed. Sarcopenia ↓ production → falsely "normal" creatinine in low-mass patients (elderly, women). Cystatin C is an alternative for those patients. CKD staging uses eGFR: stage 1 (≥90 with damage), 2 (60-89), 3a (45-59), 3b (30-44), 4 (15-29), 5 (<15, dialysis range).

    Draw the glomerulus with the three Starling forces. Label PGC, πGC, PBC. Compute net filtration pressure.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. If PGC drops to 40 (severe hypotension), what happens to NFP and GFR?

    Show answers
    1. NFP = 40 − 30 − 15 = −5 mmHg. Net filtration would stop → GFR ≈ 0 → pre-renal AKI. This is why sustained hypotension is nephrotoxic.
    2. Creatinine is generated by muscle. Sarcopenia (lower muscle mass) ↓ generation, so serum Cr looks "normal" despite reduced clearance. Use estimating equations that include age, sex, race; consider cystatin C.

    Renal · Concept 5 of 14

    Tubular reabsorption and secretion P

    The kidney filters 180 L/day and produces ~1.5 L of urine. The PCT does the bulk recovery; the LoH builds the gradient; the DCT and collecting duct fine-tune to match the body's needs.

    Notes

    • PCT (where the volume work happens):
      • Reabsorbs ~67% of filtered Na⁺ and water (iso-osmotic).
      • 100% of filtered glucose, amino acids, ~85% bicarbonate.
      • Driven by basolateral Na/K ATPase establishing the Na⁺ gradient.
      • Apical transporters: SGLT (Na-glucose), Na/H exchanger, Na-amino acid cotransporters.
      • Secretes organic acids (incl. uric acid, many drugs).
    • Loop of Henle (gradient builder):
      • Descending: water permeable, salt impermeable. Filtrate concentrates.
      • Thick ascending: Na/K/2Cl symporter pumps salt out, water stays. Filtrate dilutes.
      • Net: creates and maintains the medullary osmotic gradient (300 mOsm cortex → 1,200 mOsm deep medulla).
    • DCT:
      • Na-Cl symporter (target of thiazide diuretics).
      • Ca²⁺ reabsorption regulated by PTH.
    • Collecting duct (the fine-tuner):
      • Principal cells: reabsorb Na⁺ (ENaC), secrete K⁺, respond to aldosterone and ADH (aquaporin-2 insertion).
      • α-Intercalated cells: secrete H⁺, reabsorb HCO₃⁻ (acid disposal).
      • β-Intercalated cells: secrete HCO₃⁻ (alkali disposal).
    • Tubular secretion: moves substances from blood → tubule. Used for K⁺ (CD), H⁺ (CD), organic anions and cations (PCT).

    Summary

    SegmentWhat is reabsorbedDiuretic that acts here
    PCT~67% Na/H₂O, glucose, AA, HCO₃⁻Acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase)
    Descending LoHWater—
    Thick ascending LoHNa, K, 2Cl (symporter)Loop diuretics (furosemide)
    DCTNa, Cl, CaThiazides (HCTZ)
    CD principalNa (ENaC), water (ADH)K⁺-sparing (amiloride, spironolactone)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Diuretic ladder for fluid overload: loops are most potent (huge sodium load behind them). Thiazides are next, useful in resistant HTN combined with loops. K⁺-sparing diuretics protect against hypokalemia. Glucosuria: when blood glucose > 180 mg/dL, SGLT transport saturates → glucose spills into urine. SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) deliberately exploit this for diabetes and HF.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which segment reabsorbs ~67% of filtered sodium?

    Show answers
    1. Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT).
    2. Furosemide blocks Na/K/2Cl in thick ascending LoH → ↑ Na delivery to the CD → principal cells exchange Na (in) for K (out) under aldosterone → ↑ K⁺ excretion → hypokalemia. Treat with K⁺ supplementation or add a K⁺-sparing agent.

    Renal · Concept 6 of 14

    Countercurrent multiplier and exchanger P

    The kidney concentrates urine using a clever trick: a salt pump in the ascending limb creates a gradient that the descending limb passively follows. The vasa recta preserve the gradient by flowing in opposite directions.

    Notes

    • Countercurrent multiplier (the loop of Henle):
      • Thick ascending limb pumps Na/K/2Cl out → interstitium becomes salty.
      • Descending limb is water-permeable → water leaves filtrate into salty interstitium.
      • Filtrate at the bottom of the loop reaches ~1,200 mOsm.
      • Net effect: builds the medullary osmotic gradient (300 → 1,200 mOsm).
    • Countercurrent exchanger (the vasa recta):
      • Blood vessels run parallel to LoH, descending and ascending limbs adjacent.
      • Passively trade water and solutes with surrounding interstitium without washing out the gradient.
    • Urea recycling (deep dive):
      • Urea is reabsorbed in the deep CD (ADH-dependent), entering the medullary interstitium, contributing ~half of the 1,200 mOsm.
      • Some diffuses into the thin LoH and is recycled.
    • Why it matters: the gradient is the engine that lets the collecting duct (under ADH) extract water from the filtrate to concentrate urine. No gradient = no concentration.
    • Loss of gradient: chronic loop diuretic use, severe medullary injury → impaired concentrating ability → polyuria.

    Summary

    StructureWhat it doesResult
    Thick ascending LoHActive salt pumpMultiplies gradient
    Descending LoHPassive water lossFiltrate concentrates
    Vasa rectaPassive countercurrent flowPreserves gradient
    Collecting duct + ADHAquaporin-2 insertionWater reabsorbed → concentrated urine

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Loop diuretics destroy the gradient. Furosemide blocks Na/K/2Cl → ↓ medullary osmolarity → ↓ ability to concentrate urine even with ADH. Useful clinically: rapid diuresis. Side effect: cannot concentrate urine well during therapy. Diabetes insipidus: central DI (low ADH) and nephrogenic DI (kidney unresponsive to ADH) both cause dilute polyuria; central DI responds to desmopressin, nephrogenic does not.

    DRAW IT

    Sketch the LoH with osmolarities marked at top (300), bottom (1,200), thick ascending exit (100). Add the vasa recta running antiparallel. Show ADH-driven water reabsorption in the CD.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Maximum osmolarity of human medullary interstitium?
    2. DOK 3. Why can a patient on chronic furosemide not concentrate urine even when dehydrated?
    Show answers
    1. ~1,200 mOsm/kg.
    2. Furosemide blocks the Na/K/2Cl transporter that drives the countercurrent multiplier. Without active salt pumping, the medullary gradient collapses. ADH can still try to extract water from the collecting duct, but with no osmotic gradient to draw against, water cannot be removed efficiently.

    Renal · Concept 7 of 14

    ADH and water balance P

    ADH (vasopressin) is the master regulator of water. It inserts aquaporins in the collecting duct to recapture water when plasma is too concentrated.

    Notes

    • Made in: hypothalamus (supraoptic + paraventricular nuclei). Released from: posterior pituitary.
    • Stimuli for release:
      • ↑ Plasma osmolarity (sensed by hypothalamic osmoreceptors, threshold ~280 mOsm).
      • ↓ Blood volume / BP (sensed by atrial and arterial baroreceptors).
      • Nausea, pain, anxiety, opioids (non-osmotic).
    • Two receptors:
      • V2: principal cells of CD → aquaporin-2 insertion → water reabsorption.
      • V1: vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction (at high levels, in shock).
    • Without ADH: CD impermeable to water → dilute urine, polyuria (up to ~20 L/day).
    • With maximum ADH: CD very permeable → concentrated urine (~1,200 mOsm).
    • Disorders:
      • SIADH: too much ADH → water retention → hyponatremia, concentrated urine.
      • Central DI: too little ADH (pituitary injury) → dilute polyuria, hypernatremia. Responds to desmopressin.
      • Nephrogenic DI: kidney resistance to ADH (lithium, hypercalcemia, congenital) → dilute polyuria. Does not respond to desmopressin.

    Summary

    ConditionADHUrineSerum Na
    Dehydration↑↑Concentrated, low volumeNormal-high
    SIADH↑ inappropriatelyConcentrated↓ (hyponatremia)
    Central DI↓Dilute, polyuria↑
    Nephrogenic DINormal/highDilute, polyuria↑

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Hyponatremia from SIADH: treat by restricting free water; severe symptomatic cases may need hypertonic saline (cautiously — too-rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination). Lithium-induced nephrogenic DI is common in long-term users. "Water deprivation test" differentiates DI from primary polydipsia and central from nephrogenic by response to desmopressin.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient on lithium develops polyuria and dilute urine. Most likely diagnosis?
    2. DOK 3. Why is rapid correction of severe hyponatremia dangerous?
    Show answers
    1. Lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.
    2. Brain cells adapt to chronic hyponatremia by losing osmolytes. Rapid correction makes plasma hypertonic relative to brain cells → water shifts out → osmotic demyelination (formerly "central pontine myelinolysis"). Correct Na slowly (≤ 8-10 mEq/L per 24 h in chronic).

    Renal · Concept 8 of 14

    RAAS, sodium balance, and aldosterone P

    The kidney owns long-term BP through volume control. Sodium is the volume hormone, and aldosterone is the sodium hormone. RAAS ties them together.

    Notes

    • RAAS cascade (see cardiovascular notes for the full diagram):
      • ↓ Renal perfusion or ↓ NaCl at macula densa or ↑ sympathetic → JG cells release renin.
      • Renin cleaves angiotensinogen (liver) → AT-I.
      • ACE (lung endothelium) converts AT-I → AT-II.
      • AT-II effects: vasoconstriction, ↑ aldosterone, ↑ ADH, ↑ thirst, direct ↑ PCT Na reabsorption.
    • Aldosterone (adrenal cortex, zona glomerulosa):
      • Targets principal cells of cortical CD.
      • Inserts ENaC (apical) and Na/K ATPase (basolateral) → Na⁺ reabsorbed, K⁺ secreted.
      • Also stimulates α-intercalated cells → H⁺ secretion.
      • Net: ↑ volume, ↑ BP, ↓ K⁺, slight acid loss.
    • Counter-regulators:
      • ANP and BNP: released from atrial / ventricular myocytes when stretched → ↑ Na excretion, ↑ GFR, ↓ renin, ↓ aldosterone.
      • Pressure natriuresis: ↑ BP → mechanical ↑ Na excretion.
    • Disorders:
      • Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome): adrenal adenoma → ↑ aldosterone → HTN, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, low renin.
      • Secondary hyperaldosteronism: ↑ renin → ↑ aldosterone (renal artery stenosis, HF, cirrhosis).
      • Addison disease (primary adrenal failure): ↓ aldosterone → hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension, acidosis.

    Summary

    StateReninAldosteroneNaK
    Primary hyperaldo (Conn)↓↑↑Normal/↑↓
    Secondary hyperaldo↑↑Normal/↑↓
    Addison↑↓↓↑
    SIADH (compare)NormalNormal↓ (dilution)Normal

    CLINICAL THREAD

    ACE inhibitors / ARBs / MRAs all reduce aldosterone action, dropping Na/water retention and conserving K⁺. Spironolactone reduces mortality in HFrEF by blocking aldosterone's pro-fibrotic and remodeling effects on the heart (beyond just diuresis).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. What electrolyte pattern do you expect in Addison's disease?
    2. DOK 4. Why does spironolactone reduce mortality in HFrEF beyond its diuretic effect?
    Show answers
    1. ↓ Na, ↑ K, ± metabolic acidosis (mineralocorticoid deficiency).
    2. Aldosterone promotes cardiac fibrosis and adverse remodeling beyond its renal effects. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism (spironolactone, eplerenone) reduces fibrosis, arrhythmias, and remodeling, lowering mortality in HFrEF independent of diuresis.

    Renal · Concept 9 of 14

    Potassium balance P

    Potassium lives mostly inside cells. Small shifts in serum K⁺ have huge consequences for excitable tissues. The kidney is the long-term gatekeeper; aldosterone, pH, and insulin shift it acutely.

    Notes

    • Distribution: ~98% intracellular, ~2% extracellular. Serum K⁺ 3.5-5.0 mEq/L.
    • Acute shifts (between compartments):
      • Insulin pushes K⁺ INTO cells (used therapeutically in hyperkalemia).
      • β2 agonists push K⁺ INTO cells (albuterol used in hyperK).
      • Alkalosis pushes K⁺ INTO cells (in exchange for H⁺).
      • Acidosis pushes K⁺ OUT (causes hyperkalemia in DKA at presentation).
      • Cell lysis (rhabdo, hemolysis, TLS) releases K⁺ → hyperkalemia.
    • Renal handling:
      • PCT: ~65% reabsorbed.
      • Thick ascending LoH: ~25% reabsorbed (Na/K/2Cl).
      • DCT / CD: variable. Principal cells SECRETE K⁺ under aldosterone; α-intercalated cells can REABSORB K⁺ in deficit states.
    • Hyperkalemia (K⁺ > 5.0):
      • Causes: renal failure, K-sparing diuretics, ACE-I/ARBs, Addison, acidosis, cell lysis, hemolyzed sample.
      • ECG: peaked T waves → wide QRS → loss of P → sine wave → asystole.
      • Emergency treatment ("C BIG K"):
        • C: Calcium gluconate (stabilizes myocardium, does not lower K).
        • B: Bicarbonate if acidotic (shifts K in).
        • I: Insulin + glucose (shifts K in).
        • G: β2-Agonist (e.g., albuterol, shifts K in).
        • K: Kayexalate / patiromer / dialysis (remove K from body).
    • Hypokalemia (K⁺ < 3.5):
      • Causes: diuretics, vomiting, diarrhea, hyperaldosteronism, alkalosis, insulin therapy.
      • ECG: U waves, flattened T, prolonged QT.
      • Symptoms: weakness, cramps, arrhythmias, ileus.
      • Replace K orally if possible; IV slowly (≤ 10 mEq/hr peripherally, ≤ 20 mEq/hr central). Also replace Mg²⁺ (low Mg perpetuates low K).

    Summary

    StateK⁺ shiftECG hallmark
    HyperkalemiaCellular leak or retentionPeaked T → wide QRS → sine wave
    HypokalemiaLoss or shift inU waves, flat T, long QT

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Always check magnesium when treating hypokalemia. Low Mg²⁺ keeps ROMK channels open → ongoing K⁺ secretion. You cannot fix K⁺ without fixing Mg²⁺. Also: digoxin toxicity is potentiated by hypokalemia (both compete for Na/K ATPase).

    DRAW IT

    Draw the K⁺ shift compartments. Show ECF and ICF with K⁺ percentages. Add arrows for insulin, β2, alkalosis (into cell) and acidosis, cell lysis (out of cell).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which ECG change is the earliest in hyperkalemia?
    2. DOK 4. A DKA patient with K⁺ 5.8 on arrival drops to 3.0 after insulin. Explain.
    Show answers
    1. Peaked (tented) T waves.
    2. DKA causes acidosis, which drives K⁺ out of cells → measured serum K⁺ is high even though total body K⁺ is depleted (from osmotic losses in urine). Insulin shifts K⁺ back INTO cells, unmasking the deficit → rapid drop. Always replace K⁺ in DKA once not severely hyperkalemic. Watch K closely as you treat.

    Renal · Concept 10 of 14

    Calcium and phosphate balance P

    Calcium and phosphate move together but are regulated like rivals. PTH raises Ca²⁺ and lowers phosphate; calcitriol raises both. The kidney converts vitamin D to its active form.

    Notes

    • Calcium distribution: 99% in bone. Plasma ~10 mg/dL (total) or ~1.2 mmol/L (ionized). 40% bound to albumin, 50% free (ionized = active), 10% complexed.
    • Three hormones, two organs (gut, kidney, bone):
      • PTH (parathyroid, in response to ↓ Ca²⁺):
        • Bone: ↑ resorption → ↑ Ca²⁺ and phosphate release.
        • Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption (DCT), ↓ phosphate reabsorption (PCT) → phosphaturia.
        • Kidney: ↑ 1α-hydroxylase → ↑ active vitamin D.
      • Calcitriol (active vitamin D, 1,25-(OH)₂-D):
        • Gut: ↑ Ca²⁺ AND phosphate absorption.
        • Bone: permissive for PTH-mediated resorption.
        • Kidney: modest ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption.
      • Calcitonin (thyroid C cells): mild ↓ Ca²⁺ via inhibition of osteoclasts. Minor role in humans.
    • Vitamin D activation:
      • Skin (UVB) or diet → cholecalciferol (D3).
      • Liver: 25-hydroxylation → 25-OH-D (storage form, what we measure).
      • Kidney: 1α-hydroxylation → 1,25-(OH)₂-D (active).
    • Phosphate: PCT reabsorption regulated by PTH (inhibits) and FGF23 (inhibits in CKD).

    Summary

    HormoneCaPhosphateTrigger
    PTH↑↓ (renal)↓ Ca²⁺
    Calcitriol↑↑PTH, ↓ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate
    Calcitonin↓ (modest)↓ (modest)↑ Ca²⁺
    FGF23 (deep)—↓ (renal)↑ Phosphate

    CLINICAL THREAD

    CKD mineral-bone disease: failing kidney can't activate vitamin D → ↓ Ca²⁺ → secondary hyperparathyroidism → bone disease. Phosphate also retained → ↑ FGF23. Manage with phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, calcimimetics. Hypocalcemia signs: Chvostek (facial tap twitches), Trousseau (carpal spasm with BP cuff), perioral tingling, prolonged QT. Hypercalcemia: "stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones" — kidney stones, bone pain, GI/abdominal pain, confusion. Shortens QT.

    Renal · Concept 11 of 14

    Renal acid-base regulation P

    The lungs handle volatile acid (CO₂) by ventilation. The kidneys handle fixed acid (H⁺ from metabolism) by reabsorbing bicarbonate and secreting protons. Slower than the lungs, but the only way to actually remove H⁺ from the body.

    Notes

    • Three buffer systems:
      • Bicarbonate (extracellular, fastest, regulated by both lungs and kidneys): H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ CO₂ + H₂O.
      • Protein (intracellular, esp. Hb): histidine residues.
      • Phosphate (urinary, intracellular).
    • Renal acid handling (two main jobs):
      • Reabsorb filtered HCO₃⁻ (PCT, ~85%; thick ascending LoH, ~10%; DCT/CD, ~5%). Done via H⁺ secretion + carbonic anhydrase.
      • Generate new HCO₃⁻ by excreting H⁺ buffered by phosphate (titratable acid) and ammonia (NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺).
    • α-Intercalated cells (CD): pump H⁺ into urine via H⁺-ATPase and H⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Generate HCO₃⁻ on the basolateral side.
    • β-Intercalated cells (CD): secrete HCO₃⁻ in alkalosis. Mirror image.
    • Compensation rules:
      • Respiratory acidosis → kidney retains HCO₃⁻ (takes days; expect ~3.5 mEq HCO₃⁻ rise per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ rise in chronic).
      • Metabolic acidosis → lungs hyperventilate (within minutes). Winters formula: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 (±2).
      • Metabolic alkalosis → hypoventilation, but limited by hypoxia.

    Summary

    Type of acidHandled bySpeed
    Volatile (CO₂)LungMinutes
    Fixed (H⁺ from diet, metabolism)KidneyHours to days
    Buffer (HCO₃⁻ generation)Kidney (phosphate + ammonia)Days

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Renal tubular acidosis (RTA):

    • Type 1 (distal): cannot secrete H⁺ → urine pH high, hypokalemia, hypercalciuria, kidney stones.
    • Type 2 (proximal): cannot reabsorb HCO₃⁻ → bicarbonate wasting, hypokalemia.
    • Type 4 (hyperkalemic): low aldosterone or resistance → ↓ Na/K/H exchange, hyperkalemia, mild acidosis.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A metabolic acidosis patient has HCO₃⁻ 14. What PaCO₂ would represent appropriate respiratory compensation?
    2. DOK 4. Explain why Type 1 distal RTA produces both acidosis and kidney stones.
    Show answers
    1. Winters: PaCO₂ ≈ 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 21 + 8 = 29 (±2). Higher than that = co-existing respiratory acidosis; lower = co-existing respiratory alkalosis.
    2. Distal H⁺ secretion fails → systemic acidosis. Urine cannot be acidified, so calcium phosphate precipitates more readily. Acidosis also drives bone Ca²⁺ release → hypercalciuria → stones. Plus, citrate (a natural stone inhibitor) is reabsorbed more in acidosis → low urinary citrate worsens stone formation.

    Renal · Concept 12 of 14

    Fluid compartments, osmolarity, tonicity P

    Total body water is ~60% of body weight. It splits between intracellular and extracellular spaces. Sodium dominates ECF, potassium dominates ICF, and water moves freely between them along osmotic gradients.

    Notes

    • Total body water (TBW): ~60% of BW in adult males, ~50% in females. Decreases with age and adiposity.
    • Compartment split (60-40-20 rule for males):
      • Total body water: 60% BW (in a 70 kg male ≈ 42 L).
      • Intracellular fluid (ICF): 40% BW (~28 L). Dominant cation: K⁺.
      • Extracellular fluid (ECF): 20% BW (~14 L). Dominant cation: Na⁺.
        • Interstitial: ~15% BW.
        • Plasma: ~5% BW.
        • Transcellular (CSF, GI, joint): ~1%.
    • Osmolarity: total solute concentration in solution (mOsm/L). Normal plasma 285-295.
    • Effective osmolarity (tonicity): only solutes that don't freely cross membranes count. Na⁺, glucose, mannitol = effective. Urea = ineffective (crosses freely).
    • Tonicity of common IV fluids (vs plasma):
      • 0.9% NS: isotonic (308 mOsm).
      • Lactated Ringers: isotonic (273 mOsm), more physiologic electrolytes.
      • 5% Dextrose (D5W): isotonic in the bag, hypotonic once dextrose is metabolized — distributes as free water.
      • 0.45% NS: hypotonic.
      • 3% NaCl: hypertonic (used for severe hyponatremia, ICP).
    • Water movement:
      • Hypertonic ECF (e.g., hypernatremia) → water leaves cells → cells shrink.
      • Hypotonic ECF (e.g., hyponatremia) → water enters cells → cells swell. In brain → cerebral edema.

    Worked calculation

    1. Plasma osmolarity: Na 140, glucose 90, BUN 14.
      • Posm ≈ 2 × [Na] + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8 = 280 + 5 + 5 = 290 mOsm/L.
      • Interpretation: normal.
    2. DKA case: Na 130, glucose 720, BUN 28.
      • Posm = 2 × 130 + 720/18 + 28/2.8 = 260 + 40 + 10 = 310 mOsm/L.
      • Interpretation: hyperosmolar despite "low" Na. Glucose drags water out of cells, diluting Na (corrected Na: add 1.6 mEq for every 100 glucose above 100 → 130 + 1.6 × 6.2 ≈ 140).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Fluid choice matters. Hypovolemic shock: isotonic (NS or LR) replaces ECF volume. Hypernatremia: hypotonic (½ NS, D5W) corrects free water deficit. Hyponatremia (severe symptomatic): 3% NS to raise Na slowly. Free water deficit ≈ TBW × (Na − 140) / 140.

    Renal · Concept 13 of 14

    Acid-base disorder interpretation P

    Use a stepwise method on every ABG. Identify the primary disorder, evaluate compensation, calculate the anion gap, and check for mixed disorders. Patterns emerge quickly with practice.

    Notes

    • Six-step interpretation:
      1. pH: acidemic (<7.35), alkalemic (>7.45), normal?
      2. PaCO₂: which way moves it?
      3. HCO₃⁻: which way moves it?
      4. Identify primary disorder (whichever value moves with the pH).
      5. Check expected compensation. If outside the predicted range → mixed disorder.
      6. If metabolic acidosis → calculate anion gap (AG = Na − Cl − HCO₃⁻; normal 8-12).
    • Compensation rules:
      • Acute respiratory acidosis: HCO₃⁻ ↑ 1 per 10 PaCO₂ ↑.
      • Chronic respiratory acidosis: HCO₃⁻ ↑ 3.5 per 10 PaCO₂ ↑.
      • Metabolic acidosis: PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 (Winters).
      • Metabolic alkalosis: PaCO₂ ↑ 0.7 per 1 HCO₃⁻ ↑ (limited by hypoxic drive).
    • High AG metabolic acidosis (MUDPILES):
      • Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, Iron/INH, Lactate, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates.
    • Normal AG metabolic acidosis:
      • Diarrhea (lose HCO₃⁻), RTA, acetazolamide. Cl⁻ rises to fill the gap (hyperchloremic).
    • Mixed disorders: two primaries at once. Examples:
      • Salicylate poisoning: metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis.
      • DKA with vomiting: high AG metabolic acidosis + metabolic alkalosis.
      • COPD on diuretic: respiratory acidosis + metabolic alkalosis.

    Worked ABG interpretations

    1. ABG: pH 7.10, PaCO₂ 22, HCO₃⁻ 6, Na 138, Cl 100.
      • Acidemic. HCO₃⁻ ↓ → primary metabolic acidosis.
      • Winters: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × 6 + 8 = 17 (±2). Actual 22 = co-existing respiratory acidosis.
      • AG = 138 − 100 − 6 = 32 → high AG.
      • Mixed: high AG metabolic acidosis + respiratory acidosis. Severe DKA with respiratory failure.
    2. ABG: pH 7.55, PaCO₂ 48, HCO₃⁻ 40, Cl 90.
      • Alkalemic. HCO₃⁻ ↑ → primary metabolic alkalosis.
      • Expected PaCO₂ ↑ 0.7 per 1 HCO₃⁻ above 24 = 0.7 × 16 ≈ 11; expected ~40+11 = 51. Actual 48 ≈ expected → appropriate respiratory compensation.
      • Metabolic alkalosis with appropriate compensation. Cause: vomiting, diuretics, NGT suction.

    Renal · Concept 14 of 14

    Common renal conditions CLIN

    Renal pathology splits into acute (AKI), chronic (CKD), tubular (RTA, AIN, ATN), glomerular, and infectious / structural (UTI, stones). The first sort tells you most of what you need to know.

    Notes

    • Acute kidney injury (AKI) (KDIGO):
      • ↑ Cr ≥ 0.3 mg/dL in 48h, or ≥ 1.5× baseline in 7 days, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h × 6h.
      • Three categories:
        • Prerenal (~60%): low perfusion. Hypovolemia, HF, sepsis, NSAIDs. FENa < 1%, BUN/Cr > 20.
        • Intrinsic: ATN (ischemic or nephrotoxic), AIN (drug, autoimmune), glomerulonephritis. FENa > 2%.
        • Postrenal: obstruction (stone, BPH, malignancy). Diagnose with ultrasound.
    • Chronic kidney disease (CKD):
      • eGFR < 60 for > 3 months, or evidence of damage (proteinuria, structural).
      • Stages 1-5 by eGFR (see Concept 4).
      • Complications: HTN, anemia (↓ EPO), hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, acidosis, uremia.
      • Management: BP control (ACE-I/ARB), glycemic control, SGLT2 inhibitors (reduce CKD progression), phosphate binders, EPO, vitamin D analogs.
    • UTI:
      • Lower (cystitis): dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain.
      • Upper (pyelonephritis): fever, flank pain, CVA tenderness, often nausea.
      • Most common pathogens: E. coli (~80%), Staph saprophyticus, Klebsiella, Enterococcus.
      • Catheter-associated UTI risk rises with each day → review catheter need daily.
    • Nephrolithiasis:
      • Calcium oxalate (~75%), uric acid, struvite (infection), cystine.
      • Severe colicky flank-to-groin pain, hematuria, nausea.
      • Stones < 5 mm often pass; > 10 mm usually need intervention.
    • Glomerular disease patterns:
      • Nephritic: hematuria, RBC casts, HTN, mild proteinuria, low GFR. Post-infectious GN, IgA, anti-GBM, ANCA vasculitis.
      • Nephrotic: proteinuria > 3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia. Minimal change, FSGS, membranous, diabetic, amyloid.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    The bedside nursing approach to AKI: volume status, medication review, urine output, foley patency. Three quick wins — stop nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast, aminoglycosides where possible), correct hypovolemia, rule out obstruction. For CKD patients, every encounter is a chance to reinforce BP and glucose targets, medication adherence, and preparation for renal replacement therapy when GFR < 20.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient with AKI has FENa 0.5%, BUN/Cr 28, oliguria, and recent diarrhea. Which AKI category?
    2. DOK 4. Why is anemia a hallmark of CKD?
    Show answers
    1. Prerenal AKI from volume depletion. The kidneys are healthy but underperfused, so they avidly reabsorb sodium (low FENa) and urea (high BUN/Cr ratio).
    2. The kidney makes erythropoietin (EPO) in peritubular fibroblasts in response to hypoxia. In CKD, EPO production declines → ↓ RBC production → normocytic normochromic anemia. Treated with ESA (erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) and iron.
