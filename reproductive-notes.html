Reproductive System + Development

    Reproductive · Concept 1 of 11

    Male reproductive anatomy A

    The male system makes sperm and delivers them. Two testes (gonads), a duct system, accessory glands that produce semen, and the penis.

    Notes

    • Testes (paired gonads):
      • In scrotum (extra-abdominal — sperm production needs temperature ~2 °C below core body).
      • Seminiferous tubules: site of spermatogenesis. Contain Sertoli cells (nurse cells; blood-testis barrier).
      • Interstitial Leydig cells (between tubules): make testosterone.
      • Cremaster muscle adjusts scrotal position to regulate temperature.
    • Duct system (sperm pathway: "SEVEN UP"):
      • Seminiferous tubules → rete testis → efferent ductules → epididymis (sperm mature, gain motility; ~20-day transit) → ductus (vas) deferens → ejaculatory duct → urethra (prostatic → membranous → spongy/penile).
    • Accessory glands (contribute to semen):
      • Seminal vesicles (~60% of semen): fructose, prostaglandins, alkaline fluid. Empty into ejaculatory duct.
      • Prostate (~30%): milky alkaline fluid with citrate, PSA (prostate-specific antigen), enzymes. Surrounds urethra below bladder.
      • Bulbourethral glands (Cowper's): pre-ejaculate mucus that lubricates and neutralizes residual acidic urine in urethra.
    • Semen: ~2-5 mL per ejaculation; ~20-300 million sperm/mL is normal range. Alkaline pH ~7.4 to neutralize female vaginal acidity.
    • Penis:
      • Three erectile bodies: two corpora cavernosa (dorsal, fill with blood during erection) + one corpus spongiosum (ventral, surrounds urethra).
      • Glans penis is the expanded tip of corpus spongiosum.
      • Erection: parasympathetic (NO → cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation → blood pools).
      • Ejaculation: sympathetic (emission of semen) + somatic (rhythmic contractions).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia): common in older men. Enlarged prostate compresses urethra → hesitancy, weak stream, frequency, nocturia, urgency. Treat: α-blockers (tamsulosin), 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride), TURP if severe. Prostate cancer: screening with PSA controversial; DRE may reveal nodular firm gland. Cryptorchidism: undescended testis. Surgical correction in childhood to preserve fertility and ↓ cancer risk. Testicular torsion: surgical emergency — twisting cuts blood supply, 6-hour window. Sudden severe scrotal pain, swelling.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which cells produce testosterone?
    2. DOK 3. A young man has sudden severe testicular pain and swelling. Why is this an emergency?
    Show answers
    1. Leydig (interstitial) cells.
    2. Testicular torsion — the spermatic cord twists, cutting off arterial supply. Testicle becomes ischemic and is rarely salvageable after ~6 hours. Surgical detorsion is urgent.

    Reproductive · Concept 2 of 11

    Spermatogenesis + testosterone P

    Sperm production is continuous from puberty onward — ~100 million new sperm per day. Testosterone drives the process and produces male secondary sex characteristics.

    Notes

    • Spermatogenesis (in seminiferous tubules, ~64-day cycle):
      1. Spermatogonium (2n, diploid) divides by mitosis → primary spermatocyte (2n).
      2. Primary spermatocyte undergoes meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes (n, haploid).
      3. Meiosis II → four spermatids.
      4. Spermatids mature (spermiogenesis) into sperm with head (DNA + acrosome), midpiece (mitochondria), tail (flagellum).
    • Sertoli cells (nurse cells):
      • Support developing sperm.
      • Blood-testis barrier (immune privilege — protects haploid cells from immune attack).
      • Secrete inhibin (→ ↓ FSH).
      • Convert testosterone to estradiol (locally).
    • Testosterone:
      • Synthesized by Leydig cells from cholesterol; stimulated by LH.
      • Functions:
        • Drives spermatogenesis (local high concentration).
        • Secondary sex characteristics: facial/body hair, voice deepening, muscle mass, bone growth.
        • Libido, anabolic effects, sperm transport.
        • Converted peripherally to DHT (more potent — by 5α-reductase) and to estradiol (by aromatase).
      • Levels gradually decline with age.
    • HPG axis (covered in Concept 5):
      • GnRH (hypothalamus) → LH + FSH (anterior pituitary) → testes.
      • LH → Leydig cells → testosterone.
      • FSH → Sertoli cells → spermatogenesis support.
      • Negative feedback: testosterone inhibits hypothalamus and pituitary; inhibin selectively inhibits FSH.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Anabolic steroid use: exogenous testosterone suppresses HPG axis → Leydig cells atrophy → testicular atrophy + infertility. Finasteride (5α-reductase inhibitor): blocks T → DHT conversion; treats BPH and male pattern hair loss. Klinefelter syndrome (XXY): small firm testes, gynecomastia, tall stature, infertility, low testosterone, ↑ FSH/LH.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which hormone stimulates testosterone production by Leydig cells?
    2. DOK 3. Why does long-term anabolic steroid use cause infertility?
    Show answers
    1. LH (luteinizing hormone).
    2. Exogenous testosterone suppresses GnRH and LH/FSH → endogenous testosterone production drops → intratesticular testosterone (needed for spermatogenesis) falls dramatically → spermatogenesis arrests. May be reversible if short-course; sometimes permanent.

    Reproductive · Concept 3 of 11

    Female reproductive anatomy A

    Two ovaries, two fallopian tubes, one uterus, one vagina, plus external structures. Functions: produce eggs, support pregnancy, deliver and nurse the infant.

    Notes

    • Ovaries (paired gonads):
      • Almond-sized; in pelvis, anchored by ovarian and suspensory ligaments.
      • Cortex: follicles at various stages.
      • Medulla: vessels, nerves.
      • Functions: produce oocytes (born with ~1-2 million primary oocytes, ~400,000 at puberty, ~500 ovulated in lifetime); secrete estrogen + progesterone.
    • Fallopian (uterine) tubes:
      • Site of fertilization (usually in ampulla, the widest middle region).
      • Fimbriae catch ovulated egg.
      • Cilia + smooth muscle waft egg toward uterus over ~3-4 days.
      • Not directly continuous with ovary — small peritoneal gap allows ectopic pregnancy risk.
    • Uterus:
      • Pear-shaped, ~7.5 cm. Regions: fundus, body, isthmus, cervix.
      • Three wall layers:
        • Endometrium: inner mucosal lining — functional layer sheds with menses, basal layer regenerates.
        • Myometrium: thick smooth muscle (contracts during labor; site of fibroids).
        • Perimetrium: outer serosa.
      • Cervix: narrow inferior portion. External os opens into vagina. Cervical canal contains mucus that thickens/thins with cycle. Site of Pap smear screening for cervical cancer.
    • Vagina: muscular tube, birth canal. Acidic pH ~3.8-4.5 (Lactobacillus normal flora) — defends against pathogens.
    • External genitalia (vulva): mons pubis, labia majora/minora, clitoris (homologous to penis — corpora cavernosa, sensitive), vestibule, Bartholin glands (lubricate).
    • Breasts:
      • Modified apocrine glands.
      • Lobes → lobules → alveoli (milk-producing cells) → lactiferous ducts → nipple.
      • Suspensory (Cooper) ligaments anchor tissue.
      • Develop under estrogen + progesterone; secrete milk under prolactin + oxytocin (let-down).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Ectopic pregnancy: implantation outside uterus — usually fallopian tube. Risk factors: prior PID, tubal surgery, IUD, smoking. Sudden severe lower abdominal pain + missed period + positive β-hCG → emergency (rupture can cause hemoperitoneum and shock). Pap smear: screens cervix for HPV-related dysplasia and cervical cancer. Endometriosis: endometrial tissue outside uterus (ovary, pelvis, peritoneum). Cyclic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, infertility.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Where in the fallopian tube does fertilization usually occur?
    2. DOK 3. Why does ectopic pregnancy in the fallopian tube cause severe pain and hemodynamic collapse?
    Show answers
    1. Ampulla (middle widest portion).
    2. The tubal wall cannot stretch like the uterus. As the embryo grows, the tube ruptures → hemoperitoneum → potential shock. Surgical emergency. Manage early with methotrexate (small unruptured) or surgery (ruptured).

    Reproductive · Concept 4 of 11

    Ovarian and uterine cycles P

    Two cycles run in parallel over ~28 days: the ovary develops a follicle and ovulates (ovarian cycle), and the uterus thickens then sheds its lining (uterine/menstrual cycle). Hormones synchronize them.

    Notes

    • Ovarian cycle (3 phases):
      1. Follicular phase (days 1-13, variable): FSH → primary follicle develops to secondary then mature (Graafian) follicle. Granulosa cells make estradiol. Estradiol rises.
      2. Ovulation (day ~14): LH surge (triggered by sustained high estrogen — switches from negative to positive feedback) → follicle ruptures → oocyte released into peritoneal cavity → swept by fimbriae into tube.
      3. Luteal phase (days 15-28, more constant ~14 days): ruptured follicle becomes corpus luteum → secretes progesterone (and some estrogen) → maintains uterine lining. If no pregnancy → corpus luteum degenerates → P and E drop → menses.
    • Uterine (menstrual) cycle (3 phases, aligned with ovarian):
      1. Menses (days 1-5): functional endometrium sheds with bleeding (~35-80 mL).
      2. Proliferative phase (days 6-14): estrogen from growing follicle → endometrium rebuilds and thickens.
      3. Secretory phase (days 15-28): progesterone from corpus luteum → endometrium becomes glandular, vascular, ready for implantation. If no implantation: P drops → spiral arteries constrict → ischemia → endometrium sloughs → menses begins.
    • Cervical mucus changes:
      • Periovulatory (high estrogen): thin, watery, stretchy ("egg white") — sperm-friendly.
      • Luteal (high progesterone): thick, sticky — blocks sperm and pathogens.
    • Basal body temperature: rises ~0.3-0.5 °C after ovulation (progesterone effect). Used historically for fertility tracking.
    • Oogenesis quirks (deep dive):
      • Primary oocytes are arrested in prophase I from before birth until ovulation (decades!) → contributes to ↑ aneuploidy risk with maternal age.
      • Each ovulated oocyte arrested in metaphase II — meiosis II completes only if fertilized.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Oral contraceptives: combined estrogen + progestin → suppress GnRH/FSH/LH → no LH surge → no ovulation. Also thicken cervical mucus, alter endometrium. PCOS: insulin resistance + hyperandrogenism + irregular ovulation. Oligomenorrhea, hirsutism, acne, infertility, often obesity. Treat: lifestyle, metformin, OCPs (regulate cycles), letrozole or clomiphene (induce ovulation if pregnancy desired). Plan B (levonorgestrel): emergency contraception works mainly by delaying or preventing ovulation if taken before LH surge.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. What hormone surge triggers ovulation?
    2. DOK 3. Why does corpus luteum failure lead to menses?
    Show answers
    1. LH surge.
    2. The corpus luteum makes progesterone (and some estrogen) that maintains the secretory endometrium. When it degenerates (no pregnancy, no hCG to rescue it), progesterone falls → spiral artery constriction → endometrial ischemia → sloughing → menses.

    Reproductive · Concept 5 of 11

    HPG axis: GnRH, FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone P

    The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis controls reproductive physiology in both sexes. Pulsatile GnRH drives FSH and LH; gonadal hormones feed back to control the pulse.

    Notes

    • Hypothalamus: pulsatile GnRH release (every ~1-2 h). Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH paradoxically suppresses LH/FSH — basis for GnRH analog therapy (leuprolide).
    • Anterior pituitary: GnRH → release of FSH and LH from gonadotrophs.
    • FSH actions:
      • Female: stimulates ovarian follicle development and granulosa cell estrogen production.
      • Male: stimulates Sertoli cells; supports spermatogenesis.
    • LH actions:
      • Female: triggers ovulation (mid-cycle surge); maintains corpus luteum after ovulation.
      • Male: stimulates Leydig cells → testosterone.
    • Estrogens (estradiol most potent):
      • Sources: ovarian granulosa cells; placenta in pregnancy; small amounts from adrenal androgens converted by aromatase.
      • Effects: female secondary sex characteristics, uterine proliferative phase, bone density, lipid profile (HDL ↑), feedback on HPG axis (mostly negative; positive at midcycle peak).
    • Progesterone:
      • Source: corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy.
      • Effects: secretory phase of endometrium, maintains pregnancy, breast development, thermogenic (raises BBT), inhibits FSH/LH.
    • Feedback patterns:
      • Most of the cycle: negative feedback (E + P inhibit GnRH, FSH, LH).
      • Mid-cycle: sustained high estrogen flips to positive feedback → LH surge → ovulation.
      • Inhibin (from granulosa or Sertoli cells): selectively inhibits FSH.
    • Menopause:
      • Ovarian follicle depletion → ↓ estrogen and inhibin → loss of feedback → ↑ FSH/LH (FSH especially high — > 30 IU/L is diagnostic).
      • Symptoms: vasomotor (hot flashes), genitourinary atrophy, accelerated bone loss, mood changes, sleep disturbance.
      • HRT controversies: short-term symptom relief vs long-term risks (DVT, breast cancer, stroke). Individualized decision.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    GnRH analogs (leuprolide): paradoxically suppress LH/FSH when given continuously → "chemical castration" used in prostate cancer, endometriosis, fibroids, precocious puberty, IVF protocols. Tamoxifen: SERM (selective estrogen receptor modulator) — antagonist in breast tissue (treats ER+ breast cancer), agonist in uterus (↑ endometrial cancer risk), agonist in bone. Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole): block estrogen synthesis from androgens; used in postmenopausal breast cancer.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which hormone selectively inhibits FSH but not LH?
    2. DOK 4. Why does continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH suppress LH instead of stimulating it?
    Show answers
    1. Inhibin (from granulosa cells in females, Sertoli cells in males).
    2. Pituitary gonadotrophs require pulsatile GnRH stimulation. Continuous binding causes receptor desensitization and down-regulation → ↓ LH/FSH release. Used therapeutically (leuprolide) to suppress sex hormone production.

    Reproductive · Concept 6 of 11

    Fertilization and implantation P

    Sperm reach the oocyte in the fallopian tube. One sperm penetrates the zona pellucida and fuses; the zygote begins dividing as it travels back to the uterus. About a week after fertilization, it implants in the endometrium.

    Notes

    • Sperm transport: ejaculation → vagina → cervix → uterus → fallopian tube. ~300 million sperm released; only ~200 reach the oocyte. Travel time minutes to hours.
    • Capacitation: sperm undergo membrane changes in the female tract that prepare them for fertilization (takes hours).
    • Fertilization (in ampulla):
      1. Sperm penetrate corona radiata (the cumulus cells around the egg).
      2. Acrosome reaction: sperm enzymes digest zona pellucida.
      3. Sperm membrane fuses with oocyte plasma membrane.
      4. Cortical reaction: oocyte releases granules that harden the zona, blocking polyspermy.
      5. Oocyte completes meiosis II.
      6. Pronuclei fuse → zygote (2n, fertilized egg). Genetic sex determined here (XX or XY based on sperm).
    • Pre-implantation development:
      • Day 1: zygote.
      • Days 2-3: cleavage into 2, 4, 8 cells (still in tube).
      • Day 4: morula (16+ cells).
      • Day 5: blastocyst — outer trophoblast (becomes placenta) + inner cell mass (becomes embryo) + blastocoel (cavity). Reaches uterus.
      • Day 6-7: blastocyst hatches from zona, attaches to endometrium.
    • Implantation (~day 6-10):
      • Trophoblast invades endometrium.
      • Forms syncytiotrophoblast (outer fused multinucleated) + cytotrophoblast (inner cellular).
      • Syncytiotrophoblast secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) → maintains corpus luteum → continued progesterone → endometrium maintained → menses prevented.
      • hCG detectable in maternal blood ~day 8-9; in urine within ~2 weeks (basis of pregnancy test).
    • Placenta: develops from trophoblast + maternal endometrial decidua over weeks. Fully functional by ~weeks 10-12. Replaces corpus luteum as main hormone source (E + P) around weeks 8-12.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    IVF: ovarian stimulation with FSH analogs → retrieve oocytes → in vitro fertilization → embryo transfer. Beta-hCG kinetics: roughly doubles every 48-72 hours in early normal pregnancy. Slower rise or plateau is concerning for ectopic or non-viable pregnancy. Implantation bleeding: small amount of spotting at implantation around expected period — sometimes mistaken for a light period.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which hormone produced by the trophoblast maintains the corpus luteum?
    2. DOK 3. Why does a slowly rising β-hCG raise concern for ectopic pregnancy?
    Show answers
    1. hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin).
    2. In a healthy intrauterine pregnancy, β-hCG roughly doubles every 48-72 hours. Slower rise suggests a non-viable or ectopic implantation where trophoblastic tissue isn't proliferating normally. Combined with transvaginal ultrasound, this trend distinguishes intrauterine from extrauterine pregnancy.

    Reproductive · Concept 7 of 11

    Pregnancy and maternal adaptations P

    Every maternal system changes during pregnancy. CO rises ~50%, blood volume rises ~45%, breathing changes, GI motility slows. Most adaptations support fetal oxygen and nutrient delivery.

    Notes

    • Trimesters: 1st (weeks 1-12), 2nd (13-26), 3rd (27-40). Term = 37-42 weeks.
    • Placental hormones:
      • hCG (early): rescue corpus luteum until placenta takes over.
      • Estrogen: from placenta + fetus + mother (placenta needs fetal DHEA-S).
      • Progesterone: maintains uterine quiescence, supports endometrium.
      • hPL (human placental lactogen): induces maternal insulin resistance → preserves glucose for fetus. Linked to gestational diabetes.
      • Relaxin: relaxes pelvic ligaments, softens cervix.
    • Cardiovascular changes:
      • CO ↑ 30-50% (↑ HR, ↑ SV).
      • Blood volume ↑ ~45%, plasma rises more than RBC mass → dilutional anemia ("physiologic anemia of pregnancy").
      • SVR ↓ (placental vasodilation).
      • BP often ↓ slightly in mid-pregnancy, returns to baseline by term.
    • Respiratory changes:
      • Progesterone ↑ tidal volume and minute ventilation → mild respiratory alkalosis (PaCO₂ ~30) compensated with renal HCO₃⁻ wasting.
      • Diaphragm elevates as uterus grows → ↓ FRC.
      • Dyspnea is common, normal in 3rd trimester.
    • Renal changes:
      • GFR ↑ ~50% → ↓ serum Cr (creatinine of 1.0 may be abnormal in pregnancy).
      • Glycosuria more common (glucose load exceeds threshold).
      • Ureteral dilation, urinary stasis → ↑ UTI/pyelonephritis risk.
    • GI changes:
      • Progesterone ↓ motility → constipation, GERD.
      • Nausea/vomiting common 1st trimester (hCG-related); hyperemesis gravidarum is severe.
      • Hemorrhoids common.
    • Hematologic changes:
      • Hypercoagulable state (↑ clotting factors II, VII, VIII, X, fibrinogen; ↓ protein S). Reduces postpartum hemorrhage risk but ↑ VTE risk through pregnancy and ~6 weeks postpartum.
    • Endocrine changes:
      • Thyroid: T-binding globulin ↑ → total T4 rises; free T4 relatively stable. TSH falls in 1st trimester from hCG cross-reactivity.
      • Insulin resistance ↑ (hPL, placental hormones) → gestational diabetes screen at 24-28 weeks.
    • Nutrient demands:
      • Folate (start before conception → ↓ neural tube defects).
      • Iron (↑ RBC mass + fetal demand).
      • Calcium, iodine, vitamin D, total calories.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Preeclampsia: new HTN (≥ 140/90) + proteinuria or end-organ damage after 20 weeks. Severe features: SBP ≥ 160 or DBP ≥ 110, severe headache, vision changes, RUQ pain, low platelets, ↑ LFTs, AKI. Eclampsia adds seizures. HELLP syndrome: Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets — severe variant. Treatment: deliver baby (definitive), magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis, BP control. Gestational diabetes: insulin resistance during pregnancy. Screen with glucose challenge at 24-28 weeks. Treat with diet, then insulin if needed. Increases later T2DM risk for mother and metabolic risk for child.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Why does pregnancy cause a mild respiratory alkalosis?
    2. DOK 4. A pregnant woman at 32 weeks has BP 165/110, severe headache, RUQ pain. Concern and immediate priorities?
    Show answers
    1. Progesterone increases ventilatory drive → ↑ minute ventilation → ↓ PaCO₂ → respiratory alkalosis. Kidneys compensate by wasting bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻ drops slightly). This shift facilitates CO₂ transfer from fetus to mother across placenta.
    2. Severe preeclampsia. Priorities: lab workup (CBC for platelets, LFTs, Cr, urine protein), BP control (labetalol or hydralazine), magnesium sulfate seizure prophylaxis, fetal monitoring, plan for delivery (definitive treatment).

    Reproductive · Concept 8 of 11

    Parturition (labor and delivery) P

    Labor is a coordinated transition from quiescence to active uterine contraction. Oxytocin is the engine. Positive feedback drives delivery.

    Notes

    • Initiating signals (still incompletely understood in humans):
      • Fetal cortisol rises near term → placental shift from progesterone production to estrogen.
      • ↑ Estrogen / progesterone ratio → uterine sensitization (↑ gap junctions, ↑ oxytocin receptors, ↑ prostaglandin synthesis).
      • Cervical ripening.
    • Oxytocin positive feedback:
      • Uterine stretch (fetal head pressing on cervix) → afferent signal → posterior pituitary releases oxytocin.
      • Oxytocin → uterine contraction → more stretch → more oxytocin.
      • Loop continues until baby is delivered.
    • Three stages of labor:
      1. Stage 1 (dilation): from onset of regular contractions to full cervical dilation (10 cm). Hours; longest in first-time mothers.
        • Latent phase (0-6 cm).
        • Active phase (6-10 cm).
      2. Stage 2 (expulsion): full dilation to delivery of baby. Minutes to hours.
      3. Stage 3 (placental): delivery of placenta within ~30 min after baby.
    • Postpartum:
      • Uterine involution (returns to non-pregnant size over ~6 weeks).
      • Lochia: discharge from uterine lining (rubra → serosa → alba).
      • Postpartum hemorrhage risk peaks in first 24 hours; major causes — uterine atony, retained placenta, lacerations, coagulopathy ("4 T's": Tone, Tissue, Trauma, Thrombin).
    • Interventions:
      • Pitocin (synthetic oxytocin): augment labor or treat postpartum hemorrhage.
      • Epidural analgesia.
      • Tocolytics (β2 agonists, magnesium, indomethacin, CCBs): slow preterm labor.
      • Cesarean section: indications include fetal distress, breech, prior C-section, failure to progress, placenta previa.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Postpartum hemorrhage: blood loss > 500 mL vaginal or > 1000 mL C-section. Most common cause: uterine atony. Fundal massage, oxytocin, additional uterotonics (methylergonovine, carboprost, misoprostol), TXA, manual exploration, surgical intervention if refractory. Postpartum depression: common; screen with EPDS. Different from "baby blues" (brief, self-limited). Treat with therapy, SSRIs (sertraline preferred during breastfeeding), and address postpartum thyroiditis if present.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which posterior pituitary hormone drives uterine contractions in labor?
    2. DOK 3. Why is oxytocin a classic example of positive feedback?
    Show answers
    1. Oxytocin.
    2. Contraction → cervical stretch → ↑ oxytocin release → stronger contraction → more stretch. The loop escalates until delivery terminates the stretch stimulus. One of the few clear positive feedback loops in human physiology.

    Reproductive · Concept 9 of 11

    Lactation P

    Breasts mature during pregnancy under estrogen + progesterone. Milk production starts after delivery when those hormones drop. Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin lets it down.

    Notes

    • Prenatal preparation:
      • Estrogen → duct growth.
      • Progesterone → alveolar (milk-producing) tissue growth.
      • Prolactin rises throughout pregnancy but milk production is suppressed by high progesterone.
    • Postpartum onset:
      • Placental delivery → P + E fall sharply → prolactin's milk-producing action unmasked → milk synthesis begins (~day 2-4).
      • Colostrum (first few days): yellow, antibody-rich (IgA), low volume, high nutrition.
      • Mature milk: transitional then mature over ~2 weeks.
    • Prolactin (PRL):
      • From anterior pituitary.
      • Stimulated by suckling (afferent → hypothalamus → ↓ dopamine → ↑ PRL).
      • Drives milk synthesis in alveolar cells.
      • Also suppresses GnRH → ↓ FSH/LH → lactational amenorrhea (not reliable contraception, but contributes).
    • Oxytocin and milk ejection ("let-down") reflex:
      • Suckling → afferent to hypothalamus → posterior pituitary releases oxytocin.
      • Oxytocin → myoepithelial cells around alveoli contract → milk pushed into ducts → ejection.
      • Conditioned reflex: can be triggered by hearing baby cry, thinking about baby, etc.
    • Maintenance: regular suckling (or pumping) is required to maintain supply. Stop = supply tapers off in days to weeks.
    • Breastfeeding benefits:
      • Infant: IgA (passive immunity), optimal nutrition, microbiome seeding, ↓ infection risk, possibly ↓ allergy/asthma risk.
      • Maternal: faster uterine involution (oxytocin), ↓ ovarian + breast cancer risk, postpartum weight loss, bonding.
    • Drugs and breastfeeding: most drugs cross into milk at low concentrations. Specific concerns: amiodarone, chemotherapy, lithium, radioactive iodine, some psychoactives. LactMed database is the standard reference.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Mastitis: bacterial infection (often Staph aureus) entering through cracked nipple → painful firm tender area in breast, fever, malaise. Treat with continued nursing (helps drain), warm compresses, antibiotics (dicloxacillin or cephalexin). Abscess may need drainage. Sheehan syndrome (review from endocrine): postpartum hemorrhage → pituitary infarction → failure to lactate is often the first clue (absent prolactin response).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which hormone is responsible for milk ejection (let-down)?
    2. DOK 3. Why does milk production start only after delivery, not during pregnancy?
    Show answers
    1. Oxytocin.
    2. Throughout pregnancy, high progesterone (from placenta) blocks prolactin's milk-producing action on alveolar cells. After placental delivery, progesterone falls sharply → prolactin's effect is unmasked → milk synthesis begins. This is why retained placenta can delay milk onset.

    Reproductive · Concept 10 of 11

    Embryology, fetal circulation, neonatal transition A&P

    In nine months, a single zygote becomes a multi-system newborn. Three germ layers form first; each gives rise to specific tissues. The fetus uses a unique circulation that shunts blood around non-functioning lungs and liver, then reroutes everything within minutes of birth.

    Notes

    • Germ layers (formed during gastrulation, week 3):
      • Ectoderm: skin epidermis, nervous system, neural crest (which gives rise to many things — adrenal medulla, melanocytes, much of facial bone, peripheral neurons, etc.).
      • Mesoderm: muscle, bone, blood, heart, vessels, kidneys, gonads, dermis, most connective tissue.
      • Endoderm: GI tract lining, respiratory tract lining, liver, pancreas, thyroid, bladder.
    • Major developmental milestones (rough):
      • Week 3: gastrulation → three germ layers.
      • Week 4: heart beats, neural tube closes (folate timing critical here).
      • Weeks 4-8: organogenesis (most major organs form). Highest teratogen vulnerability.
      • Week 8: embryo → fetus.
      • Week 12: external genitalia distinguishable.
      • Week 24: viability threshold (surfactant production starts in earnest at ~24-28 weeks).
      • Week 37+: term.
    • Fetal circulation — three shunts bypass non-functioning organs:
      1. Ductus venosus: umbilical vein → bypasses liver → IVC.
      2. Foramen ovale: opening between right and left atria → blood bypasses pulmonary circulation.
      3. Ductus arteriosus: pulmonary artery → aorta → bypasses pulmonary circulation.
    • Why bypass lungs? Pulmonary vascular resistance is very high in the fetus (fluid-filled, collapsed alveoli, hypoxic vasoconstriction). Right-to-left shunting sends most blood through systemic circulation. Oxygenation happens at the placenta, not the lungs.
    • Why bypass liver? Liver is functionally immature in fetus; doesn't need full perfusion until birth.
    • Fetal-to-neonatal transition (first minutes of life):
      1. First breath → lungs expand, alveoli fill with air → ↓ pulmonary vascular resistance.
      2. ↓ Right-sided pressures + ↑ left-sided pressures → foramen ovale closes functionally (anatomically months later → becomes fossa ovalis).
      3. ↑ Oxygenation + ↓ prostaglandin (umbilical cord clamping removes placental prostaglandins) → ductus arteriosus constricts and closes (becomes ligamentum arteriosum).
      4. Umbilical vessels constrict → ductus venosus closes (becomes ligamentum venosum); umbilical arteries → medial umbilical ligaments; umbilical vein → round ligament of liver (ligamentum teres).
      5. Liver and kidneys take over their adult roles.
    • If transition fails:
      • Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA): continuous "machinery murmur," can cause left-to-right shunt and CHF. Treat with indomethacin (blocks PGE) or surgical ligation.
      • Patent foramen ovale (PFO): often asymptomatic in adults; can be a route for paradoxical embolism.
      • Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN): pulmonary resistance fails to drop → continued right-to-left shunting → cyanosis.
    • Common congenital defects:
      • Heart: ASD, VSD, tetralogy of Fallot (PROVe — pulmonary stenosis, RVH, overriding aorta, VSD), transposition of great arteries.
      • Neural tube defects: anencephaly, spina bifida (folate prevents).
      • Cleft lip/palate.
      • Down syndrome (trisomy 21): characteristic facies, intellectual disability, heart defects (AVSD), ↑ ALL/AML risk, early Alzheimer.
      • Limb anomalies, GI atresias, cryptorchidism.
    • Teratogens: alcohol (FAS — fetal alcohol syndrome), tobacco, certain medications (warfarin, ACE-I, isotretinoin, valproate, lithium), infections (TORCH — Toxoplasmosis, Other [syphilis, varicella, parvovirus], Rubella, CMV, Herpes), radiation, maternal diabetes, maternal hyperthermia. Greatest risk during organogenesis (weeks 3-8).

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Newborn assessment (APGAR at 1 and 5 min): Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, Respiration. Score 0-10. Useful but doesn't predict long-term outcome alone. Folic acid 400 mcg daily for all reproductive-age women — pre-conception folate is the single most effective prevention of neural tube defects. RhoGAM at 28 weeks and within 72 h of delivery for Rh-negative mothers carrying Rh-positive babies (prevents future hemolytic disease of the newborn — see blood/hematology Concept 7).

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Name the three fetal shunts that bypass the lungs and liver.
    2. DOK 4. Why does indomethacin promote ductus arteriosus closure in a newborn?
    Show answers
    1. Foramen ovale (RA → LA, bypasses lungs), ductus arteriosus (pulmonary artery → aorta, bypasses lungs), ductus venosus (umbilical vein → IVC, bypasses liver).
    2. Prostaglandin E (PGE) keeps the ductus arteriosus open in utero. Indomethacin inhibits COX, blocking PGE synthesis → ductus constricts and closes. Conversely, in ductus-dependent congenital heart disease (e.g., transposition), prostaglandin E1 infusion is given to keep the ductus open until surgical correction.

    Common reproductive conditions CLIN

    A short tour of the most common reproductive pathology — most have appeared elsewhere in these notes. Use this card as a consolidated review.

    Notes

    • Male:
      • BPH: aging prostate compresses urethra.
      • Prostate cancer: most common male cancer. PSA screening controversial.
      • Erectile dysfunction: vascular (most common cause in older men), neurogenic, psychogenic, hormonal. PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil) common treatment.
      • Testicular cancer: most common cancer in men 15-35. Painless testicular mass. Highly curable.
      • Hypogonadism: low testosterone. Primary (testicular failure) vs secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic).
    • Female:
      • PCOS: insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, oligo-ovulation.
      • Endometriosis: ectopic endometrial tissue → pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, infertility.
      • Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas): benign smooth muscle tumors. Heavy menses, pressure symptoms.
      • Cervical cancer: HPV-related (16, 18). Pap screening + HPV vaccination dramatically reduced incidence.
      • Ovarian cancer: silent until late. Bloating, pelvic discomfort, weight loss. CA-125, imaging.
      • Breast cancer: most common cancer in women. Screening mammography. BRCA1/2 mutations confer high lifetime risk.
      • Menopause: vasomotor symptoms, GU atrophy, bone loss, mood/sleep changes.
    • Pregnancy-related:
      • Ectopic pregnancy.
      • Spontaneous abortion (miscarriage).
      • Hyperemesis gravidarum.
      • Gestational diabetes.
      • Preeclampsia / eclampsia / HELLP.
      • Placenta previa, abruption.
      • Preterm labor.
      • Postpartum hemorrhage, postpartum depression.
    • STIs (high-yield):
      • Chlamydia (most common bacterial STI): often asymptomatic; PID risk → infertility, ectopic. Treat: doxycycline.
      • Gonorrhea: discharge, dysuria, PID. Treat: ceftriaxone (often combined with doxy for co-infection).
      • Syphilis: primary (chancre), secondary (rash including palms/soles), latent, tertiary (cardiovascular, neuro). Treat: penicillin G.
      • HSV: painful vesicles, recurrent. Treat: acyclovir, valacyclovir.
      • HPV: cervical / anal / oropharyngeal cancer risk. Vaccine prevents most.
      • HIV (covered in immune notes).
      • Trichomoniasis: itching, malodorous discharge. Treat: metronidazole.
      • PID: ascending infection of upper genital tract. Pain, fever, cervical motion tenderness. Treat: broad-spectrum antibiotics.

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Nursing assessment in reproductive complaints: last menstrual period, obstetric history (G/P), pregnancy status (always rule out in reproductive-age women), STI exposure, contraception, family history of reproductive cancers. Use trauma-informed approach for pelvic and genital exams; respect privacy and consent. Patient education is a huge component of reproductive health.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which STI most commonly causes pelvic inflammatory disease and tubal infertility?
    2. DOK 4. Why is folate supplementation recommended before conception, not just after?
    Show answers
    1. Chlamydia trachomatis (often asymptomatic, so untreated → ascending infection → tubal scarring).
    2. The neural tube closes at ~week 4 of gestation — often before a woman knows she's pregnant. Folate is needed for nucleotide synthesis in rapidly dividing cells, including the neural tube. By the time a pregnancy is confirmed, the critical window has passed. Universal supplementation in all reproductive-age women is the safest strategy.
