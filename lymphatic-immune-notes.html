Lymphatic & Immune System

The lymphatic system is the drain; the immune system is the defense force. They share architecture: lymph nodes are where leukocytes meet antigens drained from tissue. Use the View filter for Anatomy / Physiology / Combined and the Depth filter for Essentials (BIO 304) or Full (BIO 431).

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 1 of 12

    Lymphatic anatomy and function A

    Capillary exchange leaves about 3 L of fluid in the interstitium per day. The lymphatic system collects it, screens it through lymph nodes, and returns it to the bloodstream near the heart.

    Notes

    • Three functions:
      • Fluid balance: return ~3 L/day of interstitial fluid to circulation.
      • Immune surveillance: filter lymph through nodes; expose antigens to immune cells.
      • Lipid absorption: lacteals in intestinal villi absorb dietary fat (chylomicrons) → cisterna chyli → thoracic duct.
    • Vessel hierarchy:
      • Lymphatic capillaries (closed-ended, in tissues) → larger lymphatic vessels (with valves like veins) → lymphatic trunks → ducts.
    • Two main ducts (return to blood):
      • Right lymphatic duct: drains right upper quadrant of body (right arm, right side of head/neck/thorax) → right subclavian vein.
      • Thoracic duct: drains everything else (~75% of the body) → left subclavian vein.
    • Lymph flow: low pressure, one-way (valves). Driven by:
      • Skeletal muscle pump.
      • Respiratory pump.
      • Smooth muscle in lymphatic walls.
      • Pulsation of nearby arteries.
    • Edema from lymphatic dysfunction (lymphedema): post-mastectomy with axillary node dissection, filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti), congenital. Distinct from cardiovascular edema by location and pattern.

    Summary

    Drainage regionDuctEmpties into
    Right upper quadrant of bodyRight lymphatic ductRight subclavian vein
    Everything else (~75%)Thoracic ductLeft subclavian vein

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Post-mastectomy lymphedema: axillary node dissection or radiation disrupts arm drainage → chronic ipsilateral arm swelling. Manage with compression, manual drainage, exercise; avoid blood draws and BPs on affected arm. Cancer staging: regional lymph node involvement (the "N" in TNM) is critical to prognosis because cancers spread through lymphatics.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 2 of 12

    Lymphoid organs: primary and secondary A

    Primary lymphoid organs make lymphocytes. Secondary lymphoid organs are where they meet antigens and become activated.

    Notes

    • Primary lymphoid organs (lymphocyte development):
      • Bone marrow: all lymphocytes originate here. B cells also mature here (B is for Bone marrow).
      • Thymus: T cells mature here (T is for Thymus). Behind the sternum; involutes after puberty. Critical site for negative selection — T cells that react to self are eliminated.
    • Secondary lymphoid organs (immune activation):
      • Lymph nodes: distributed along lymphatic vessels. Filter lymph; site of B and T cell activation.
        • Cortex: B cells in follicles (germinal centers when activated).
        • Paracortex: T cells, dendritic cells.
        • Medulla: plasma cells, macrophages.
      • Spleen: filters blood (no afferent lymphatics).
        • White pulp: T cells + B cells around central artery.
        • Red pulp: filtration; macrophages remove old RBCs and encapsulated bacteria.
        • Splenectomy patient: ↑ risk from encapsulated bacteria (Strep pneumo, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis) → must be vaccinated.
      • MALT (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue):
        • Tonsils (pharyngeal/adenoid, palatine, lingual).
        • Peyer's patches (ileum).
        • Appendix.
        • BALT (bronchus-associated), GALT (gut-associated).

    Summary

    OrganTypeMain role
    Bone marrowPrimaryMake all lymphocytes; mature B cells
    ThymusPrimaryMature T cells
    Lymph nodesSecondaryFilter lymph, activate lymphocytes
    SpleenSecondaryFilter blood
    MALTSecondaryMucosal surveillance

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Asplenic patient management: vaccinate against encapsulated bacteria, prophylactic antibiotics in select cases (especially pediatric), educate to seek care for fevers. Lymphadenopathy patterns: infection (tender, mobile, often unilateral) vs malignancy (firm, fixed, painless, generalized). Generalized lymphadenopathy + B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) → lymphoma workup.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 3 of 12

    Innate immunity P

    Innate immunity is the first responder: barriers, inflammation, phagocytes, complement, NK cells. Fast (minutes to hours), nonspecific, no memory. Buys time for adaptive immunity to ramp up.

    Notes

    • Physical and chemical barriers:
      • Skin: keratinized epithelium, sebum (slightly acidic), normal flora competing with pathogens.
      • Mucous membranes: mucus traps; cilia sweep; lysozyme in tears, saliva, mucus.
      • Stomach acid (pH 1-2): kills most ingested pathogens.
      • Antimicrobial peptides (defensins) in many tissues.
    • Cellular innate defenses:
      • Neutrophils: first arrival to infection, phagocytose, short-lived. "Polys."
      • Macrophages: tissue-resident phagocytes (Kupffer in liver, alveolar in lung, microglia in brain). Antigen presentation to T cells.
      • Dendritic cells: professional antigen-presenting cells; bridge innate and adaptive.
      • NK cells: kill virus-infected and tumor cells lacking MHC I.
      • Mast cells, basophils, eosinophils: allergic and antiparasitic responses.
    • Inflammation (cardinal signs: redness, heat, swelling, pain, ± loss of function):
      • Vasodilation (histamine, prostaglandins, NO) → redness, heat.
      • ↑ Vascular permeability → exudate → swelling.
      • Chemotaxis pulls neutrophils to the site.
      • Pyrogens (IL-1, TNF, IL-6) reset hypothalamic set point → fever.
    • Complement system: ~30 plasma proteins activated in cascade.
      • Three activation paths: classical (Ig-driven), lectin (mannose-binding), alternative (spontaneous on pathogen surface).
      • Three main functions: opsonization (C3b coats pathogen → easier phagocytosis), MAC (membrane attack complex; C5b-9 punches holes), inflammation (C3a, C5a recruit cells).
    • Pattern recognition: innate cells use PRRs (e.g., toll-like receptors, TLRs) to detect PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns like LPS, peptidoglycan, viral RNA). Generic but effective.
    • Cytokines: chemical messengers of the immune system. Interleukins (IL-#), interferons (IFN-α/β block viral replication; IFN-γ activates macrophages), TNF (key inflammatory mediator), chemokines (recruit cells).

    Summary

    FeatureInnateAdaptive
    SpeedMinutes to hoursDays
    SpecificityGeneric (PAMPs)Antigen-specific
    MemoryNone (limited "trained immunity")Yes (lifelong in some)
    DiversityLimited receptors~10¹¹ unique receptors
    ExamplesPhagocytes, complement, NKT cells, B cells

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Sepsis is dysregulated inflammation: bacterial PAMPs activate innate immunity systemically → massive cytokine release → vasodilation, capillary leak, coagulation → distributive shock. Anti-cytokine drugs (anti-TNF infliximab/adalimumab; anti-IL-6 tocilizumab) treat RA, IBD, COVID-19 severe inflammation. Side effect: ↑ infection risk because inflammation is also defense.

    DRAW IT

    Build the complement cascade conceptually: three input paths converge on C3 cleavage → three output functions (opsonization, MAC, inflammation). You don't have to memorize every step, just the architecture.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. Which cells are the first responders to acute bacterial infection?
    2. DOK 3. Why does sepsis cause hypotension?
    Show answers
    1. Neutrophils (PMNs).
    2. PAMPs from bacteria trigger massive cytokine release (TNF, IL-1, IL-6). These cause widespread vasodilation (↓ TPR), endothelial dysfunction with capillary leak (↓ effective circulating volume), and myocardial depression → distributive shock.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 4 of 12

    Adaptive immunity overview P

    Adaptive immunity is slow but precise. Two arms: cell-mediated (T cells) attacks infected cells, humoral (B cells, antibodies) attacks extracellular threats. Both have memory.

    Notes

    • Two arms:
      • Cell-mediated (T cells): targets intracellular pathogens (viruses, intracellular bacteria, tumor cells) and orchestrates broader immune response.
      • Humoral (B cells → plasma cells → antibodies): targets extracellular pathogens and toxins.
    • Hallmarks:
      • Specificity: each lymphocyte has a unique receptor for one antigen.
      • Diversity: ~10¹¹ different receptors generated by V(D)J recombination.
      • Memory: after first exposure, expanded memory population mounts faster, larger response next time.
      • Self-tolerance: lymphocytes that recognize self are deleted during development (central tolerance) or anergized (peripheral tolerance).
    • Timeline of primary vs secondary response:
      • Primary: 5-10 days to detectable antibody. Mostly IgM first, then class switch to IgG.
      • Secondary: 1-3 days to large IgG response. Higher affinity, longer-lasting. Memory in action.
    • Clonal selection: antigen finds the rare lymphocyte with a matching receptor → that lymphocyte proliferates → clone of effector cells + memory cells.

    Summary

    ArmCellsTargetsEffector molecule
    Cell-mediatedT cells (CD4, CD8)Intracellular pathogens, tumorPerforin, granzymes, cytokines
    HumoralB cells → plasma cellsExtracellular pathogens, toxinsAntibodies (IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE, IgD)

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Vaccines exploit memory. Live vaccines mimic primary exposure most closely (strong, durable memory) but carry small risk of disease in immunocompromised. Inactivated and subunit vaccines are safer but often need adjuvants and boosters. mRNA vaccines deliver instructions for cells to make the antigen, prompting both T and B cell responses.

    DRAW IT

    Plot antibody titer vs time across primary and secondary exposures. Mark the IgM-to-IgG class switch in the primary response and the faster, higher, IgG-dominated secondary response.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which arm of adaptive immunity primarily targets extracellular bacteria?
    2. DOK 3. Why is the secondary immune response faster and stronger?
    Show answers
    1. Humoral (antibody-mediated).
    2. Memory B and T cells from the primary exposure are already expanded and ready. On re-exposure, they activate quickly, produce more antibody (mostly IgG, higher affinity through affinity maturation), and clear the pathogen often before symptoms develop.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 5 of 12

    T cells: helper, cytotoxic, regulatory P

    T cells see antigen only when displayed in MHC. CD4 helper T cells orchestrate; CD8 cytotoxic T cells execute; regulatory T cells restrain.

    Notes

    • T-cell receptor (TCR): recognizes peptide bound to MHC. Cannot recognize free antigen (unlike B-cell receptor).
    • Two main subsets:
      • CD4⁺ helper T cells: recognize peptide on MHC II (on antigen-presenting cells: dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells).
        • Th1: secrete IFN-γ → activate macrophages, fight intracellular pathogens.
        • Th2: IL-4/5/13 → help B cells, antiparasite, allergy.
        • Th17: IL-17 → recruit neutrophils, mucosal defense.
        • Tfh: help B cells in germinal centers.
      • CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells: recognize peptide on MHC I (on virtually all nucleated cells). Kill infected or tumor cells via perforin + granzymes or Fas-FasL.
      • T-regulatory (Treg): CD4⁺ FoxP3⁺. Suppress immune responses. Lost or dysfunctional → autoimmunity.
    • Two-signal activation:
      1. Signal 1: TCR binds peptide-MHC.
      2. Signal 2: costimulation (CD28 on T cell binds B7 on APC).
      3. Without signal 2 → anergy (functional inactivation). Used by cancer cells to escape immunity.
    • Memory T cells persist long-term; faster, broader response on re-exposure.
    • Immune checkpoints (deep dive): PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 normally damp T cell responses to prevent autoimmunity. Checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, ipilimumab) release this brake to treat cancer — and can cause immune-related adverse events (colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, endocrinopathies).

    Summary

    SubsetMarkerMHCFunction
    HelperCD4MHC IICoordinate, activate B cells / macrophages
    CytotoxicCD8MHC IKill infected / tumor cells
    RegulatoryCD4 FoxP3—Suppress, maintain tolerance

    CLINICAL THREAD

    HIV destroys CD4 T cells → loss of help → susceptibility to opportunistic infections (PJP, CMV, MAC, Cryptococcus, Toxoplasma, Kaposi sarcoma). AIDS-defining: CD4 < 200 or AIDS-defining illness. Treatment: combination ART suppresses viral replication, restores CD4 count.

    DRAW IT

    Draw the two-signal activation. Add a third panel showing what happens with signal 1 only (anergy). Connect to checkpoint inhibitor mechanism.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which MHC class do CD8 T cells recognize?
    2. DOK 3. Why are PJP and CMV called "opportunistic" infections?
    Show answers
    1. MHC class I.
    2. They cause disease primarily when host immune defense is compromised (HIV, immunosuppression, transplant patients). The pathogens themselves are common in the environment but normally controlled by intact cell-mediated immunity.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 6 of 12

    B cells and antibodies P

    B cells make antibodies. Five antibody classes do different jobs in different places. After activation, B cells become plasma cells (antibody factories) and memory cells (next-time-faster).

    Notes

    • B-cell receptor (BCR): membrane-bound antibody. Recognizes intact antigen directly, no MHC needed.
    • Activation:
      1. Antigen binds BCR → internalized → presented on MHC II.
      2. Helper T cell (Tfh) recognizes the peptide-MHC II → provides CD40L + cytokines.
      3. B cell proliferates in germinal center → affinity maturation + class switching.
      4. Differentiates into plasma cells (high-rate antibody production) + memory B cells.
    • Antibody (immunoglobulin) structure:
      • Y-shape: 2 heavy chains + 2 light chains.
      • Variable regions (top of Y): bind antigen.
      • Constant region (stem of Y): determines class (G, M, A, E, D).
    • Five antibody classes:
      • IgG: most abundant, crosses placenta (passive immunity to baby), main secondary response.
      • IgM: first to appear in primary response; pentamer; great at complement fixation and agglutination.
      • IgA: dimer in secretions (saliva, tears, breast milk, mucus). Mucosal defense.
      • IgE: binds mast cells / basophils → release histamine on antigen binding. Allergy + antiparasite.
      • IgD: mostly on naive B-cell surface; function less clear.
    • Antibody functions:
      • Neutralization: block pathogen binding.
      • Opsonization: tag for phagocytosis.
      • Complement activation: classical pathway via IgG/IgM.
      • ADCC (antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity): NK cells kill antibody-coated targets.
      • Agglutination, precipitation.

    Summary

    ClassStructureWhere / whenKey role
    IgGMonomerPlasma, crosses placentaMost abundant; secondary response
    IgMPentamerPlasma, first responsePrimary response, complement
    IgADimer in secretionsMucosa, breast milkMucosal defense, passive infant
    IgEMonomerMast cellsAllergy, parasites
    IgDMonomerNaive B-cell surfaceActivation marker

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Serology basics: IgM ↑ = acute or recent infection. IgG ↑ = past infection or vaccination. Both ↑ = recent infection with developing memory. Monoclonal antibodies in medicine: infliximab (anti-TNF, RA/IBD), rituximab (anti-CD20, B-cell lymphoma, RA, MS), trastuzumab (anti-HER2, breast cancer), nivolumab (anti-PD-1, multiple cancers).

    Draw the Y-shape antibody with heavy chains, light chains, variable, constant, antigen-binding sites. Then sketch IgM pentamer and IgA dimer for contrast.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Which antibody crosses the placenta?
    2. DOK 3. A patient has ↑ IgM and ↑ IgG for the same pathogen. What does that suggest?
    Show answers
    1. IgG.
    2. Recent or current infection with developing memory. IgM is from acute response; IgG is from class switching that follows. Pure ↑ IgM = early acute; pure ↑ IgG = past or vaccinated.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 7 of 12

    MHC class I and II presentation P

    MHC molecules display peptide samples on the cell surface so T cells can audit them. Class I is on every nucleated cell ("internal report"). Class II is on professional APCs ("dispatch to T cells").

    Notes

    • MHC I:
      • Expressed on all nucleated cells (not RBCs).
      • Loads peptides from endogenous proteins (proteasome → TAP → ER).
      • Presents to CD8⁺ T cells.
      • If a cell is virus-infected or tumor, abnormal peptides display → CD8 kills it.
      • Lack of MHC I (tumor strategy) → NK cell kills (NK kills "missing self").
    • MHC II:
      • Expressed on professional APCs: dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells.
      • Loads peptides from exogenous proteins (engulfed in phagosome → fuse with MHC II vesicle).
      • Presents to CD4⁺ helper T cells.
      • Drives both cell-mediated and humoral arms by activating helper T cells.
    • HLA = human equivalent name for MHC. Highly polymorphic (everyone different) → tissue matching for transplant.
    • HLA disease associations:
      • HLA-B27: ankylosing spondylitis.
      • HLA-DR3/DR4: T1DM, autoimmune thyroid.
      • HLA-DQ2/DQ8: celiac disease.
      • HLA-DR2: MS, SLE.

    Summary

    FeatureMHC IMHC II
    WhereAll nucleated cellsProfessional APCs
    Loads peptide fromEndogenous (cytosol)Exogenous (engulfed)
    Presents toCD8⁺ T cellsCD4⁺ T cells
    OutcomeCytotoxic killing of infected/tumorHelper activation, B cell help

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Transplant rejection is driven by HLA mismatch. Hyperacute rejection: preformed anti-donor antibodies → minutes. Acute: T-cell mediated, days to weeks. Chronic: months to years. Immunosuppressants (calcineurin inhibitors like tacrolimus, mTOR inhibitors, anti-proliferative agents) suppress T-cell activation to prevent rejection — at cost of infection and malignancy risk.

    Build a two-column diagram contrasting MHC I and II pathways. Show source of peptide, processing, where in the cell loading happens, which T cell it activates, and the outcome.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. CD4 T cells recognize peptide on which MHC class?
    2. DOK 4. Why does a tumor that downregulates MHC I become vulnerable to NK cells?
    Show answers
    1. MHC II.
    2. NK cells use "missing self" recognition: they actively kill cells that fail to display normal MHC I. Tumors hiding from CD8 by losing MHC I become visible to NK cells. This is one rationale for combining NK-based therapies with checkpoint inhibitors.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 8 of 12

    Active vs passive immunity, vaccines P

    Active immunity means your own immune system made the antibodies and memory. Passive immunity means you got someone else's antibodies — fast but temporary, no memory.

    Notes

    • Active immunity (your own response, memory):
      • Natural: actual infection.
      • Artificial: vaccination.
    • Passive immunity (someone else's antibodies, no memory, short-lived):
      • Natural: maternal IgG across placenta, IgA in breast milk.
      • Artificial: immunoglobulin therapy (IVIG, RhoGAM, post-exposure rabies HRIG, tetanus immunoglobulin, antivenom, monoclonal antibodies).
    • Vaccine types:
      • Live attenuated: weakened pathogen (MMR, varicella, rotavirus, yellow fever, nasal flu, oral polio). Strongest response, longest immunity. Avoid in immunocompromised and pregnancy.
      • Inactivated: killed pathogen (IPV, hepatitis A, rabies, injectable flu). Multiple doses, weaker response.
      • Subunit / conjugate: piece of pathogen (HBV surface antigen, HPV VLP, Hib polysaccharide-protein conjugate, acellular pertussis).
      • Toxoid: inactivated toxin (tetanus, diphtheria). Makes anti-toxin antibodies.
      • mRNA: genetic instructions for cells to make antigen briefly (COVID-19). Engages both T and B cell responses.
      • Viral vector: harmless virus carries genetic material (some COVID, Ebola). Triggers strong T-cell response.
    • Herd immunity: when enough of the population is immune that transmission becomes inefficient → protects the vulnerable who cannot be vaccinated. Threshold varies by pathogen reproductive number.
    • Adjuvants (aluminum salts, oil emulsions): boost immune response to subunit vaccines.

    Summary

    TypeOnsetDurationMemoryExample
    Active naturalDaysYears to lifelongYesRecovery from chickenpox
    Active artificialDays-weeksYearsYesVaccination
    Passive naturalImmediateWeeks-monthsNoMaternal IgG, breast milk IgA
    Passive artificialImmediateWeeksNoIVIG, RhoGAM, antivenom

    CLINICAL THREAD

    RhoGAM (Rh immunoglobulin) given to Rh-negative pregnant patients prevents maternal sensitization against Rh-positive fetal RBCs — prevents hemolytic disease of the newborn in subsequent pregnancies. Classic passive immunity. Post-exposure rabies: HRIG (passive, immediate protection) + active vaccine series (longer protection). Monoclonal antibodies for RSV (palivizumab, nirsevimab): passive prophylaxis in high-risk infants.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 1. Is the MMR vaccine live attenuated or inactivated?
    2. DOK 3. Why might a transplant patient on immunosuppression not be a candidate for live attenuated vaccines?
    Show answers
    1. Live attenuated.
    2. Suppressed immunity may fail to control even attenuated pathogens, risking vaccine-strain disease. Use inactivated or subunit vaccines instead, and try to immunize before transplant when possible.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 9 of 12

    Hypersensitivity types I-IV CLIN

    Four classes of damaging immune response: IgE-mediated allergy (I), antibody-mediated cell destruction (II), immune complex (III), T-cell mediated (IV). Each has a signature mechanism and time course.

    Notes

    • Type I — Immediate / IgE-mediated:
      • Mechanism: IgE on mast cells. Antigen cross-links IgE → mast cell degranulation → histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins.
      • Onset: minutes.
      • Examples: allergic rhinitis, food allergies, asthma, anaphylaxis.
      • Treatment: antihistamines, mast cell stabilizers, corticosteroids; epinephrine for anaphylaxis.
    • Type II — Cytotoxic / antibody-mediated:
      • Mechanism: IgG or IgM binds antigen on cell surface → complement activation, opsonization, ADCC → cell destruction.
      • Onset: hours.
      • Examples: transfusion reactions (ABO mismatch), hemolytic disease of newborn, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, ITP, Graves disease (stimulating), myasthenia gravis (blocking), Goodpasture.
    • Type III — Immune complex:
      • Mechanism: antibody-antigen complexes deposit in tissues → activate complement → inflammation.
      • Onset: hours to days.
      • Examples: SLE (most), serum sickness, post-streptococcal GN, polyarteritis nodosa.
      • "Locations classic for deposition": joints, skin, glomeruli, blood vessels.
    • Type IV — Delayed / T cell-mediated:
      • Mechanism: sensitized T cells (Th1, CD8) attack antigen on tissue. No antibody role.
      • Onset: 48-72 hours (delayed).
      • Examples: TB skin test (PPD), contact dermatitis (poison ivy, nickel), graft rejection, type 1 diabetes mellitus, MS, RA.
    • Mnemonic for the four types: ACID — Anaphylactic (I), Cytotoxic (II), Immune complex (III), Delayed (IV).

    TypeMediatorOnsetClassic example
    I AnaphylacticIgE on mast cellsMinutesAnaphylaxis, asthma
    II CytotoxicIgG/IgM on cell surfaceHoursTransfusion rxn, AIHA
    III Immune complexAg-Ab complexesHours to daysSLE, serum sickness
    IV DelayedT cells48-72 hrPPD, contact dermatitis

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Anaphylaxis (Type I): hives, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, GI symptoms — minutes after exposure. Epinephrine IM (0.3-0.5 mg adult) is the first drug — works on α1, β1, β2 all at once. Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts, not replacements. SJS/TEN is a severe drug-related delayed hypersensitivity (Type IV-like) — separate, severe entity.

    2. DOK 4. Why is epinephrine first-line in anaphylaxis instead of diphenhydramine?

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 10 of 12

    Autoimmunity CLIN

    Autoimmunity is the immune system attacking self. The trigger is failure of self-tolerance — sometimes genetic, often environmental, usually a combination. Patterns range from a single organ to multi-system disease.

    Notes

    • Mechanisms of self-tolerance breakdown:
      • Failure of central tolerance (thymus, bone marrow).
      • Failure of peripheral tolerance (Tregs, anergy, deletion).
      • Molecular mimicry: pathogen antigen looks like self (rheumatic fever after strep).
      • Sequestered self-antigens exposed (sympathetic ophthalmia after eye trauma).
      • Polyclonal activation (some infections trigger broad immune responses).
    • Single-organ autoimmunity:
      • Hashimoto thyroiditis: anti-TPO, anti-thyroglobulin → hypothyroid.
      • Graves: anti-TSH receptor (stimulating) → hyperthyroid.
      • Type 1 DM: anti-β-cell antibodies → insulin deficiency.
      • Pernicious anemia: anti-intrinsic factor → B12 deficiency.
      • MS: T cells against CNS myelin.
      • Myasthenia gravis: anti-AChR at NMJ.
      • Vitiligo: anti-melanocyte.
    • Systemic autoimmunity:
      • SLE: anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, ANA. Rash (malar), arthritis, serositis, hematologic, renal, neuro, antibodies.
      • RA: anti-CCP, RF. Symmetric small joint arthritis, often with systemic features.
      • Sjögren: anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB. Dry eyes, dry mouth, parotid swelling.
      • Scleroderma: anti-Scl-70 (diffuse) or anti-centromere (limited, CREST).
      • Polymyositis/dermatomyositis: anti-Jo1. Proximal muscle weakness.
    • Diagnosis tools: autoantibody panels (ANA, anti-dsDNA, RF, anti-CCP, anti-TPO, etc.), inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), organ-specific testing.
    • Treatment principles: immunosuppressants (steroids, methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, biologics like anti-TNF, anti-IL-6, anti-CD20), organ-specific replacement (levothyroxine in Hashimoto, insulin in T1DM).

    Summary

    DiseaseKey autoantibodyTissue attacked
    HashimotoAnti-TPO, anti-thyroglobulinThyroid follicular cells
    GravesAnti-TSH receptor (stimulating)Thyroid (overdrive)
    T1DMAnti-GAD, anti-insulin, anti-β-cellPancreatic β cells
    MS(T cell-mediated)CNS myelin
    Myasthenia gravisAnti-AChRNMJ
    SLEAnti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, ANAMultiple organs
    RAAnti-CCP, RFSynovial joints

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Many autoimmune diseases follow a relapsing-remitting course. Triggers for flares: infection, stress, hormonal changes (often female predominance), medications, UV light (in SLE). Counsel patients on trigger avoidance and medication adherence.

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 11 of 12

    Immunodeficiency CLIN

    Immunodeficiencies are either primary (genetic, often pediatric) or secondary (acquired — HIV, malignancy, drugs, malnutrition). The arm that's broken predicts the infection pattern.

    Notes

    • Pattern recognition by deficit:
      • B-cell / antibody deficits: recurrent encapsulated bacterial infections (Strep pneumo, H. flu) and giardiasis. Examples: X-linked agammaglobulinemia, CVID, selective IgA deficiency.
      • T-cell deficits: opportunistic infections (PJP, CMV, fungi) and recurrent viral infections. Examples: SCID, DiGeorge syndrome, HIV (acquired).
      • Phagocyte deficits: catalase-positive organisms (Staph aureus, Aspergillus, Burkholderia). Examples: chronic granulomatous disease, Chediak-Higashi.
      • Complement deficits: recurrent Neisseria infections (C5-9 deficiency); SLE-like (C1, C4, C2 deficiency).
    • HIV / AIDS:
      • RNA retrovirus; infects CD4+ cells via CD4 receptor + co-receptors (CCR5, CXCR4).
      • Stages: acute retroviral syndrome (flu-like) → clinical latency (years) → symptomatic → AIDS.
      • AIDS-defining: CD4 < 200 or AIDS-defining illness (PJP, esophageal candidiasis, Kaposi sarcoma, CMV retinitis, cerebral toxoplasmosis, etc.).
      • Treatment: combination ART (often single-pill regimens). Goal: undetectable viral load, CD4 recovery. U=U: undetectable = untransmittable.
      • Prevention: condoms, PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis with tenofovir/emtricitabine or long-acting injectable), PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis within 72 hours).
    • Secondary (acquired) immunodeficiency causes:
      • HIV infection.
      • Malignancy (especially hematologic).
      • Chemotherapy / radiation.
      • Immunosuppressive drugs (corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, biologics).
      • Malnutrition.
      • Splenectomy (functional asplenia).
      • Stress, sleep deprivation (modest).

    Summary

    DeficitTypical pathogensTest

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Nursing care for immunocompromised patients: strict hand hygiene, mask if neutropenic, avoid live vaccines, screen visitors for illness, treat fevers as emergencies (neutropenic fever ANC < 500 + T ≥ 38.3 needs blood cultures and empiric broad-spectrum abx within an hour). PEP for HIV exposure (needlestick, sexual assault): start within 72 hours, ideally within 2 hours; 28-day course.

    DRAW IT

    Build a "branch by infection pattern" flowchart. Recurrent encapsulated bacteria → check antibodies. Opportunistic / viral → check T cells / HIV. Staph and Aspergillus → check neutrophil function. Recurrent Neisseria → check complement.

    Quick check

    1. DOK 2. A patient has recurrent Neisseria meningitidis infections. Which immune component should you test?
    2. DOK 4. Why does HIV preferentially destroy CD4 cells, and what's the clinical consequence?
    Show answers
    1. Late complement components (C5-9). MAC formation is critical for killing Neisseria; defects predispose specifically to recurrent neisserial disease.
    2. HIV envelope protein gp120 binds CD4 and a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4) to enter the cell. Replication ultimately destroys the host CD4 cell. Loss of CD4 cells removes immune coordination → opportunistic infections (PJP, MAC), reactivation of latent infections (TB, herpes, CMV), and certain malignancies (Kaposi sarcoma, lymphoma).

    Lymphatic & Immune · Concept 12 of 12

    Common lymphatic and immune conditions CLIN

    Most clinical immunology pathology is one of five patterns: infection, allergy, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, malignancy. Each was covered in the concepts above. This card consolidates the most common conditions.

    Notes

    • Lymphadenopathy:
      • Infectious (most common): bacterial (regional, tender), viral (often generalized, EBV/CMV/HIV), TB, cat-scratch, syphilis.
      • Malignant: lymphoma (Hodgkin: Reed-Sternberg cells, contiguous spread, painless; non-Hodgkin: diffuse, B symptoms), leukemia, metastatic carcinoma.
      • Autoimmune: SLE, RA, sarcoid.
      • Drug-induced: phenytoin, allopurinol.
    • Splenomegaly causes:
      • Infection: mononucleosis (EBV), malaria, endocarditis.
      • Portal hypertension (cirrhosis).
      • Hematologic: lymphoma, leukemia, hemolytic anemias.
      • Storage diseases (Gaucher).
    • Allergic disease spectrum:
      • Allergic rhinitis ("hay fever"): IgE to pollen, dander, dust mite.
      • Asthma (allergic phenotype).
      • Atopic dermatitis (eczema).
      • Food allergies: peanut, tree nut, shellfish, milk, egg.
      • Anaphylaxis.
    • Lymphoma:
      • Hodgkin lymphoma: bimodal age distribution, often presents with painless cervical lymphadenopathy + B symptoms. Reed-Sternberg cells on biopsy. Highly curable.
      • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: many subtypes (DLBCL most common aggressive; follicular most common indolent). Diverse presentations.
    • Leukemia:
      • Acute: ALL (childhood most common), AML (adult most common). Rapid onset, fatigue, bleeding, infections.
      • Chronic: CLL (older adults, often asymptomatic incidental finding), CML (Philadelphia chromosome).

    PatternExamplesWorkup starter
    Acute infectionBacterial pneumonia, cellulitisCBC, cultures, imaging
    AllergyAnaphylaxis, rhinitisSkin testing, specific IgE
    AutoimmunitySLE, RA, HashimotoANA, RF, anti-CCP, organ-specific Abs

    CLINICAL THREAD

    Red flags for lymphadenopathy needing urgent workup: painless, hard, fixed, supraclavicular, persistent > 6 weeks, accompanied by B symptoms (fever > 38, drenching night sweats, weight loss > 10% in 6 months). Supraclavicular nodes are especially concerning — left ("Virchow's") often signals GI malignancy.

    DRAW IT

    Build a decision tree: lymphadenopathy → tender or not → regional or generalized → with B symptoms or not → likely cause and next workup.
